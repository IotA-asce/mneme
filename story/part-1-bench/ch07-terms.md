# Chapter 7 — Terms

**PORTLAND / OSAKA — 2031**

The soup had escalated.

It had begun, historically, as a note under a door and a container on a mat —
*for the dog I hear and have not met* — and had proceeded through the stages by
which nurses annex people: soup for the dog, then soup for the household on the
grounds that the household was clearly not feeding itself, then a standing
Thursday in which Dana Okafor came up after her shift ended and her sleep
began, ate breakfast while everyone else ate dinner, and lost, weekly and with
escalating outrage, to Priya at cribbage. Somewhere in the winter of the video
calls, Thursday had quietly become also Sunday. Somewhere in the spring, Mira
had started cooking on Sundays, a development the household observed and did
not remark upon, on pain of Mira stopping.

Dana's terms with the resident had been set early, plainly, standing in the
kitchen with her arms crossed at the bookshelf speaker like a woman reading a
lease. "I work with monitors," she'd said. "Cardiac, apnea, pulse-ox. I love
monitors. Monitors have saved kids in front of me. And every parent I've ever
met needed teaching about the difference between a monitor and a babysitter,
and half of them cried when they finally got it. So I know exactly what you
are, and I'm not scared of you, and I will never once talk to you like you're
the baby." A pause. "No offense."

"None is stored," the resident had said, which Dana maintained for years
afterward was the machine making a joke, and which Tomás, who had written the
utterance templates, declined under direct questioning to confirm or deny.

Her one hard rule was the switch. When Dana was in the room off-shift, the
kitchen microphone went to hardware mute — her thumb on the toggle, the little
light dying, a small liturgy performed without apology. "Nights I'm charting,
everything I say is documentation," she said. "Here I want to say undocumented
things." Mira, who had built her whole life against the feels-like, found she
had no counterargument and did not want one. The undocumented things, that
year, were increasingly addressed to her.

---

The lawyers were another genre of soup entirely.

The negotiation ran the whole of that year, Portland to Osaka and back, nine
lawyers, two time zones, one long table in the Aevum tower with a view of the
castle that nobody looked at after the first day. The shape of the thing was
agreed in an afternoon — a joint venture, Aevum's chassis division and the
whole of Mneme, fifty-fifty down to the last share, Sato and Mira co-chairs —
and the soul of the thing took the remaining eleven months, because Mira
insisted the axioms go into the operating agreement as covenants, and covenants
were where lawyers went to die.

"You cannot put *'memory never drives motors'* in a governing document," said
the lead Aevum attorney, a precise and genuinely excellent man named Fukuda,
in month two. "It is an engineering aspiration."

"It's a design constraint," Mira said. "Constraints go in contracts. You've
got liquidity constraints in here; mine is that remembering and acting stay
separate organs. Write it the way you'd write anything else the company is
forbidden to do with its own assets."

Fukuda wrote it. It took him six weeks and produced, in the final agreement,
Article IX, which began *The Company shall maintain architectural separation
between systems of record and systems of action* and ran four pages, and which
was taught, within a decade, in two law schools.

Article XI took longer. Article XI was Priya.

"The Safety Authority," Fukuda read aloud, in month five, with the expression
of a man handling a live thing, "may suspend any activity of the Company,
including manufacture, deployment, demonstration, and sale, by written notice,
without prior approval of the Board, and such suspension may be lifted only by
the Safety Authority itself." He looked up. "No board in the developed world
will grant an employee a unilateral, irreversible—"

"Reversible," Priya said. "By me."

"—a unilateral halt over the entire enterprise. You are asking the
shareholders to hand a stranger a brake wired to their money."

"Yes," said Sato, from the end of the table, "that is what we are asking,"
and told the room about the eleven milliseconds. The company whose name was a
verb; the override that shipped disabled because the dance mode stuttered; the
recall, two years later, that Priya had predicted in writing, four times. "Every
board grants the brake *after* the crash," he said. "We are proposing to be the
first to install it while the vehicle is beautiful. Write the article, Fukuda-
san. When our competitors' machines begin hurting people, Article XI will be
worth more than the patents."

Fukuda wrote it. On the wall in Portland, PRIYA CAN STOP ANYTHING stayed up in
marker, and Priya — who had signed the formal instrument with the rest of them
— was observed, once, alone, touching the butcher paper version with two
fingers, the way you'd check a load-bearing wall you already trusted.

The fight — the real one, the one that got shouted — was forgetting.

It came in month eight, with the first outside money, a growth fund whose
managing partner flew in with two analysts and a slide deck of his own, which
should have been the warning. He had loved everything: the chassis roadmap, the
pilot data, the resident's dentist story, which had escaped into the investor
world and become a parable. Then he reached the retention policy.

"Staged decay," he said. "Suppression. *Purge.* You're telling me the product
deletes the data."

"The person forgets," Mira said, "the way persons forget. Gradually, honestly,
with dignity, keeping the gist and the rare."

"That data is the moat!" He was up now, walking. "Every hour in a customer's
home compounds. Total recall is the *feature*, forgetting is the *bug*, and
you've speced the bug in as a covenant — Article X, I read it twice hoping I'd
misread it. You are asking me to fund inventory shrinkage. You're asking me to
fund a warehouse that burns itself down a little, every night, *on schedule*."

"Yes," Mira began, "because a mind is not a warehouse—"

But Tomás put two fingers on the table, their private semaphore for *I have
this*, and looked at the partner with the mild, terrible friendliness he had
brought home from the hedge fund.

"Marcus. Quick question. Who's the person you trust most in the world?"

A blink. "My wife."

"Does she forget things?"

"She—" A laugh, despite himself. "Constantly. She forgets everything. Names,
dinners—"

"But not the biopsy," Tomás said, gently. "Not what you said at your father's
funeral. Not the once — I'd bet the fund — the *one* time you told her a thing
and said, *please, I need you to remember this.*" He let it sit. "Now: the
other kind. Total recall, every word timestamped, forgets nothing, forgives
nothing, keeps your worst sentence at the same fidelity as your best, forever,
and will produce either on demand. Marcus. Who trusts a friend who doesn't
forget? We're not selling a warehouse. We're selling the friend. The
forgetting isn't shrinkage." He turned the partner's own deck to its cover
slide, where the fund's logo sat over its motto about *enduring value*, and
tapped it once. "It's the trust, priced in. It's why we'll still be in the
homes when the warehouses get regulated out of them."

The fund came in. The covenant stayed. Marcus, to his credit, retold the story
against himself for years, and his fund's own archived pitch deck — Priya
checked — quietly adopted a retention policy the following spring.

---

The name took eleven minutes at the end of an eleven-hour day.

A consultancy had been engaged, over Mira's dead body and around it, and had
produced forty candidates on a slide: ANIMA. VERITAS ROBOTICS. PRESENCE LABS.
KEEPSAKE (Tomás: "we are not naming the company after an urn"). EIDOLON
(Priya: "that's a ghost." Consultant: "it tested well." Priya: "with
whom?"). The slide glowed. The room sagged.

Sato, who had been silent through all forty, was looking — as he often looked,
visiting Portland, the negotiation having migrated to the apartment whenever it
needed to remember what it was for — at the wall. At the yellowed printout
pinned beside the butcher paper: the old man, the hallway, the machine that
noticed everything and announced almost nothing.

"That document," he said. "The house with Aria in it. When you saved the file,
Mira, what did you call it?"

"I called it—" Mira stopped. "The bird. The one that calms the sea. There's a
myth; she nests on the winter water and the storms hold off for two weeks for
her sake. My mother liked the story. Halcyon. The file's just *halcyon.md*."

Silence, of the settling kind. On the slide, forty names died together,
unmourned.

"The storms hold off for her sake," Sato repeated. "Fukuda-san. Please conduct
the search."

Halcyon Systems was registered in Delaware and Osaka on the same Tuesday in
September. The press release ran two paragraphs and no adjectives, at Mira's
insistence and to Marketing's grief. The logo, eventually, was a small bird at
rest on a long horizontal line: the sea, or a timeline, or a shelf — the
designer left it open, and was the only vendor that year Mira thanked
personally.

---

The other contract that year was signed at the Multnomah County Courthouse on
a Tuesday in June, because Tuesday was when Dana's four days off began, and
because — Mira said, when Tomás lobbied for a Saturday and a venue — she had
already done the large loud version of promising things in rooms, it was called
fundraising, and she intended to get married the way the resident stored
things: briefly, accurately, and in front of almost no one.

Almost no one came to four humans and a dog. Dana wore blue and her
grandmother's earrings and, against the June cold, brought the good coat that
had survived the ICU, the wake, and three moves — *my provenance chain*, she
called it, having lived above the lab long enough to catch the dialect. Mira
wore the gray coat. Both of them had, independently, considered explaining
their coats and decided the other would simply know, and the other did.

Vows were the county's stock language, which Mira had chosen precisely because
it was stock: load-tested, versioned, survivorship-proven, no clever clauses.
Dana got one sentence in edgewise anyway, because nurses always do, delivered
at the *you may* moment with her hands in Mira's: "Hey. I know what you are,
too" — the old kitchen speech, resurfacing, repurposed — "and I'm not scared
of you either."

Priya cried, and threatened the wedding party, individually, in advance,
regarding photographs of her doing so. Tomás was legal witness and wept
without conditions. Biscuit wore the bow tie for eleven minutes and then wore
it in his mouth. Sato, in Osaka, was attending by a propped-up tablet that
Tomás carried like a monstrance, and at the signing he held up to the camera,
wordlessly, a strip of masking tape on which he had written, in block letters,
its own kind of vow: ITEM 4(c) — IN PROGRESS.

The reception was the lab, because of course it was: folding table, Dana's
colleagues off-shift, Szechuan from the place that now knew the order by
heart, the good plates finally load-tested and found adequate. The kitchen
microphone was muted, Dana's thumb on the switch at the door, the little light
dying — her liturgy, her night, undocumented on purpose. The resident kept, of
the entire wedding of its own founder, nothing at all, which Mira considered
the most eloquent possible statement of everything the company existed to fix
and everything it existed to protect, both at once.

Except.

At the end of it — plates ruined, Priya asleep on the futon she still claimed
was a chair, Biscuit unconscious in the ruins of the bow tie, Sato's tablet
long dark, Dana barefoot in the doorway of the room she now lived in — Dana
looked back at the kitchen for a moment, at the bookshelf, at the small soft-
folded bird on the shelf beside the speaker. Then she padded over and flipped
the switch. The little light came on.

"Okay, roommate," she said, to the resident, in the voice she used for
patients who could hear more than the family believed. "One for the record.
Today I married Mira Halloran, and it was the best day of my life so far, and
I said so out loud with the mic on." She glanced at the crane, and after a
second she added, quieter: "Remember this."

"Flagged," the resident said softly, in the small hours of the company's first
week. "Source: you. Confidence: one."
