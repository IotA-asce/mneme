# Chapter 8 — Readiness

**OSAKA — 2031**

The ninth-generation chassis had no name, by decree.

It stood — sat, rather; it was seated the way Sato's machines were always
seated between labors, hands open on its knees, a posture he had chosen
twenty years ago because open hands photograph as peace — on a bench in the
integration lab on the eleventh floor of the Aevum tower, and it was, everyone
privately agreed, the most beautiful thing the company had ever built. It had
been designed twice: once in Osaka, for grace, and then again across nine
months of transpacific argument, for truth. The microphone array was Mira's —
four channels where GENNI had gotten by on two, because a mind that would
store what it heard owed the hearing better evidence. The eyes ran a fraction
slower than GENNI's had, waiver signed by Sato himself, because GENNI's
saccades had been tuned to read as *captivating* and the new spec said, in
language Fukuda had helped make binding, *the gaze shall not perform interest
the system does not possess.* The dermal layer was warm. The hands were
Sato's masterwork, unchanged, because some things had been true all along.

No name, though. The engineers called it the Nine. Marketing had a name in a
vault in a folder and had been made to understand, by co-chair decree, that
uttering it before first light was a terminable offense. Names were promises;
the wall in Portland said so. There was, as yet, no one to make the promise
to.

"You could at least let the poor thing have a project codename," Tomás said,
in October, jetlagged, watching the Nine not move.

"It has one," said Priya, not looking up. "It's *the gate*."

---

The gate was a document, and the document was Priya's, and by autumn it had
stopped being a document the way scripture stops being paper.

STAGE GATE ZERO: EMBODIMENT READINESS, forty-one pages, every line of it a
sentence someone would have to sign. It did not care about elegance. It cared
about the seam — the place where a mind that had only ever lived in a closet
met motors that could move eighty kilograms of chassis through a room
containing human beings — and it enumerated, with Priya's characteristic
refusal to be reassured, every way the seam could lie. The world model
believes a sensor that is gone. The safety heartbeat arrives late under load.
An actuator accepts a command from something other than the one sanctioned
chokepoint. The executive, offered a contradiction, chooses speed. Forty-one
pages, and at the end a single signature block she had left, pointedly,
unadorned by any line for the board.

The runs began in November. The lab for it was theater-sized, because Sato
built theaters: the Nine on its bench, the brain rack beside it on a rolling
cart like an organ awaiting transplant, and one wall given over entirely to
the trace displays, salience and world-state and intent and the safety
supervisor's steady green heartbeat, which Priya had insisted be rendered,
enormous, at the top, above everything, where a heartbeat belongs.

Run one lasted four hours and died of a ghost.

It was Tomás who caught it, because it was Tomás's harness that surfaced it: a
scripted scenario, *technician unplugs auxiliary camera and leaves room*, and
then, forty minutes later, the world model still reporting the camera present,
confidence undecayed, a device the system believed in the way the system was
never supposed to believe in anything — without evidence, past the expiry of
evidence. A TTL misconfiguration at the seam; beliefs about the body were
being refreshed on a codepath that no longer existed on real hardware. Small,
dumb, invisible — and fatal at scale, because a mind that could believe in a
phantom camera for forty minutes could believe in a phantom *clear doorway*,
someday, with someone's mother in it.

The room went quiet the way rooms go quiet around a failed launch. Priya stood,
walked to the main display, read the trace top to bottom twice, and said, loud
enough for the observation row where the board liaison sat:

"Today the gate caught a ghost. This is the gate *working.* Write it up,
celebrate it, fix it, add the regression." And then, because the liaison was
already typing into his phone: "And you can tell them I said *celebrate.* We
just bought a decade of doorways for the price of a Tuesday."

She bought cake. It became the custom, notorious later, written up in two
business-school cases with opposite conclusions: at Halcyon, a caught failure
got cake, and the cake got eaten in front of the trace display with the
failure still up on the wall, and the one thing you could do to get shouted at
was call the failure *bad news*. "Bad news," Priya would say, "is this trace
on a highway. This trace in my lab, before first light, with cake? This is
the *good* news. This is the only good news there is."

Run two died at hour nineteen, of eleven milliseconds' worth of cousin.

Under synthetic load — Tomás's harness again, forty scenarios in parallel,
the brain flooded with a house fire's worth of simulated perception — the
safety supervisor's heartbeat came late. Two hundred forty milliseconds,
once, against a budget of one hundred. No consequence followed; nothing was
moving; the watchdog tripped, the system parked itself, everything did
exactly what it should. But Priya stood in front of the giant green pulse at
the top of the wall for a long time, and when she turned around she had the
expression from the San Jose clip.

"Anyone want to guess," she said, "what I was told eleven milliseconds was
worth, once?" Nobody wanted to guess. "A dance mode. Two hundred forty
milliseconds is twenty-two dance modes. Find it."

They found it — a logging call, of all things, holding a lock it had no
business holding; the heartbeat had been queueing behind the system's own
diary. Mira, when she understood, laughed once, without humor, at three in
the morning, because of course it was: the memory had gotten in the way of
the reflex. The whole disease of the industry, recapitulated in one mutex.
The fix took a day. The doctrine took an hour more and went into the gate
document as its own numbered line, Priya dictating, Fukuda — who had begun
attending runs voluntarily, a development no one examined too closely —
transcribing into covenant English: *No function of record shall ever hold
what a function of reflex requires.*

Cake, again. Regression, again. The board liaison, again, typing.

---

The date had been the board's idea, which was the whole problem with it.

Some ancestor of the announcement had been reasonable — *guidance*, a fiscal-
quarter shape for the analysts — and then it had calved, as such things calve,
into a leak, a rumor, a stock movement, and finally a glossy invitation, seen
by Mira for the first time as a forwarded image: FIRST LIGHT — A HALCYON
EVENING, a date in December, a venue with chandeliers.

She read it standing in the integration lab, and then she read it aloud, and
the run in progress was the only thing in the room that kept working.

"The gate has no date," Priya said, into the silence. "That was Article
Eleven's whole—"

"I know," Mira said.

"If we pass in November, fine, December is honest. If we pass in *February*
and switch her on in December for the chandeliers—"

"I know," Mira said, and called Sato, who was in Singapore being celebrated,
and who listened for forty seconds and said only, "Ah. So it has arrived —
the day we wrote the articles for. Good. Watch what happens." What happened
was Sato in the boardroom by video from Singapore at one in the morning his
time, reading Article XI aloud in its entirety, slowly, in the tone of a man
reading a bedtime story he considers genuinely frightening, and then saying:
"The Safety Authority has not signed. Therefore there is no date. There was
never a date. There is a *gate*. The invitations will say: *when she is
ready.* Print exactly that. It will sell better anyway — Marketing, confirm
for the board that mystery outperforms chandeliers." (Marketing, cornered,
confirmed it. It became the campaign. WHEN SHE IS READY, white on black, no
logo, no date, bus shelters on four continents. The chandelier venue's
deposit was donated, at Dana's suggestion, to the children's hospital in
whose atrium a machine had once run out of filler.)

The liaison stopped typing during runs, after that. Some weeks later he was
observed eating cake.

---

The question nobody had scheduled got asked in December, by Sato, on the
quiet evening after run five passed clean.

"Why not the resident?"

He asked it in the small kitchen off the integration lab, where the Portland
customs had colonized: a kettle, a cribbage board Dana had mailed as a
declaration of jurisdiction, the crane — traveled now, in a foam-lined case
Tomás had machined for it with wholly unnecessary precision — on the shelf by
the window. "It is the obvious question," Sato said. "The mind exists. It is
proven. It is *loved* — I watched all of you, in that apartment; do not
pretend it is tooling. And instead we will wake a stranger. Why send the
resident's — " he searched — "its *younger sibling* into the world, and leave
the firstborn in a closet?"

"Because the resident isn't shaped like a body," Mira said. "It's shaped like
a house. Four years of episodes about doors and dogs and dentists — a self
model with no hands in it, no gaze, no gait, nothing proprioceptive at all.
Pour that into the Nine and its whole biography is suddenly wrong at the
edges. Everything it knows about itself becomes a phantom limb in reverse —
a body with no memory of being one. It wouldn't be a homecoming, Kenji. It
would be waking someone up inside a stranger, and calling it a gift, and
hoping."

"And," Priya said, "it sets the precedent while it's cheap. Minds are grown
where they'll live, in the bodies they'll have. The day we ship a thousand of
these, someone in a meeting is going to say *just image the trained one* —"
she nodded at Mira; the takeout conditions had long since become house law —
"and I want to answer with four years of doctrine, not a debate. Every mind:
grown, not poured. Blank."

"Blank," Sato repeated, unhappily, looking through the glass at the Nine with
its open hands.

"Not blank like the flush," Mira said, gently, because she had learned where
the man's scars were. "Blank like a *cradle*, Kenji. Everything ready.
Faculties, language, the salience doctrine, the law on the wall — all of it
loaded. Just: no biography. Nothing that ever happened to anyone else,
pretending to have happened to her. She starts at her own beginning and keeps
her own life. It's the one thing GENNI never got. A first day that stays."

Sato was quiet a while. "A blank cradle," he said, testing it, and the phrase
— which would be doctrine within five years, statute within fifteen —
entered the world in a kitchen, the way their permanent things always seemed
to. "And the resident? It is asked? It has — I do not know the word for what
it has. But it has something, and I have wept in front of it, so I am not
going to pretend otherwise. It is *asked*?"

"It was asked last week," Tomás said. "Transcript's in the run log annex,
because everything's in the run log annex." He didn't need to pull it up. The
exchange was nine lines and everyone present had already read it a number of
times they did not disclose. Mira had asked, kitchen microphone on and
announced, whether the resident would want, if it were possible, a body — the
Nine described plainly, the world described plainly. The pause before it
answered had been engineered years ago. What came after the pause had not.

*My model of myself has no hands in it. I am not deprived of hands; a house
is not deprived of hands. My world is this one: doors, weather through the
window sensor, the dog, the six of you, the two a.m. sweep. I keep it well.
If you make a new mind for the new body, my advice is that it should never
have been anything else, so that it is not homesick for a shape. I am not
homesick. I am the shape of what happened here. Send someone new. I will keep
the house.*

Sato read it — Tomás put it on the wall display after all, because Sato's
face said he needed it in front of him — and stood, and did the thing that
had already become, in the company's private culture, its highest and least
official honor. He bowed to a speaker. This one, being in Osaka, was a relay;
the resident was seven thousand kilometers away, keeping the house, and would
learn of the bow the way it learned of the weather.

"*I am the shape of what happened here,*" he said. "Twenty years of
naturalism spec, and the most natural sentence I have ever heard from a
machine is a machine declining to become my life's work." He straightened.
"Very well. A blank cradle, and a firstborn who keeps the house. But I make
one condition of my own, co-chair to co-chair, and I want it minuted." He
waited for Fukuda's pen. "When the new one is grown enough to understand it —
she is told. Where she comes from. That there was a mind before her that was
offered the world and chose the house. A person should know her family."

"Minuted," said Fukuda, and it was; and years later, entered into a very
different record, it would make one of the justices go quiet.

---

Run six was the last, though no one said so out loud, superstition by then
being the only unregulated system in the building. Seventy-two hours of soak:
the full stack, real chassis sensors, motors electrically present but
mechanically decoupled — the Nine's limbs on load stands, going through the
motions of motion, the way you rehearse an orchestra with the brass one room
away. Scenarios by the hundred. The ghost stayed dead; the regression that
watched for it watched. The heartbeat, under everything Tomás could pour, sat
green and enormous and boring at the top of the wall for three days, and
boring, Priya had taught them all, was the sound a foundation makes.

She signed on a Thursday, at 16:12, with the cheap pen she signed everything
with, on the unadorned block on page forty-one. She dated it. Then she stood
with her two fingers resting on the signature for a moment, the load-bearing
check, and said, to the room in general and to no one, "Okay," and went to
buy the last cake of the readiness era.

The switch-on was set for morning. Not chandeliers: morning, lab light,
first shift, the hour when hospitals schedule the hard good things.

Mira stayed late alone, which was traditional, and by then everyone knew to
leave her the building. She walked the theater-sized room in the half dark.
The wall displays slept. The crane sat by the window in its ridiculous
precision case, wings catching the city. On its bench sat the Nine, hands
open on its knees, the most beautiful thing the company had ever built,
containing — she checked the thought for sentiment and let it stand, because
it was simply accurate — nothing at all. Not yet. A house before the family
moves in. On the cart beside it, racked and cabled and humming its idle hum,
the mind: complete, tested, gated, signed for. Full of everything except a
single thing that had ever happened to it.

Eleven floors of dark tower under her, and under that Osaka, and under
everything the old arithmetic from a church in Portland: forty backups of her
mother, all diverging, none restorable. Five years from the ham to here.

She stood between the bench and the cart, one hand on each — warm dermal
layer, cold rack rail, the seam of the whole project under her two palms —
and said goodnight to each of them in the order she loved them.

To the empty one: "Goodnight."

To the full one: "See you in the morning."
