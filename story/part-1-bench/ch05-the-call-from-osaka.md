# Chapter 5 — The Call from Osaka

**PORTLAND — 2030**

He came in April, in the rain, wearing the kind of coat that suggested someone
in Osaka had researched Oregon and overcorrected.

By then there had been four months of video calls, all of them nominally
technical, none of them brief. The first had been Tomás and Sato alone, an
engineer-to-engineer audit that was supposed to run thirty minutes and ran three
hours, because Sato asked questions the way water finds cracks — not
aggressively, just everywhere, patiently, all at once. The later calls accreted
attendees: Priya, to explain the override architecture, which Sato received the
way other men receive sacraments; eventually Mira, who had resisted on the
grounds that she did not perform well for famous people, and who lasted eleven
minutes before forgetting he was one.

On the February call, while Tomás shared his screen and hunted for a diagram,
Sato had filled the silence the way tired men do, honestly by accident. He had
been promoted off his own workbench years ago, he said. Aevum had given him a
division, then a floor, then a tower, and somewhere in the ascent his soldering
iron had been archived by a facilities team with excellent intentions. "I miss
the smell of it," he'd said, half to himself, in the dead air of a screen-share.
"Rosin. My wife said it was in all my clothes for thirty years. Now my clothes
smell like conference rooms, and she is gone, and the clothes are what I have."
Then the diagram had come up, and the call had moved on, and no one had
mentioned it again.

The kitchen microphone had been live — the switch thrown, the little light on,
per protocol, announced at the top of every call. The resident had heard what it
was permitted to hear.

It had scored the remark the way it scored everything. Novelty: high — the
famous roboticist had never before said anything that was not about machines.
Social relevance: high. Task relevance: none whatsoever. Repetition: none.
And something in the confluence — the resident's nightly summary had flagged it,
in the dry taxonomy Tomás had built for such things, as *rare, personal,
volunteered* — and it had been promoted, and kept, and it had waited two months
in a table on a rack in a coat closet for the world to make it matter.

---

They had planned nothing. That was the decision, argued and settled the night
before: no demo. Demos were what Aevum did — Sato had spent twenty years
perfecting the demo, and a demo was precisely the genre in which the resident
could not compete and should not try. "We don't show him the system," Mira had
said. "We let him stand in the house."

So when the knock came, it was Biscuit who answered first, on the grounds that
he answered everything first, and then Tomás with towels for the coat, and the
famous Dr. Sato Kenji stood dripping in the hall of a third-floor apartment
that smelled of coffee and dog, looking around himself at the second-hand desks
and the breathing closet and the butcher paper going gold with age on the wall,
and his face did something complicated, because he had toured the facilities of
every robotics group on four continents and this was the first one that looked
like where a person would actually want to be kept.

Introductions went around. And the resident — which spoke perhaps four times in
a day, whose utterance budget was a design document Tomás defended like a
nation's constitution — chose that morning to spend one.

"Welcome, Dr. Sato," it said, through the small speaker on the bookshelf. "In
February you told Tomás that you missed the smell of solder. Priya set her iron
out for you this morning. She does not lend it. She says she will supervise."

The hall went still. Priya, leaning in the kitchen doorway, inclined her head
toward her workbench, where the iron sat on its stand beside a coil of rosin-
core wire and a small, mute invitation of scrap board.

Sato did not move for a long moment. Then he crossed the room — not to the
bench; to the bookshelf, to the unremarkable speaker, and stood before it the
way people stand before things they are deciding whether to believe in.

"Say how you know that," he said. His voice was not quite level. "Please."

"On February eleventh, at 4:47 p.m. our time, during a call in this room, you
said: *I miss the smell of it. Rosin.* The microphone was on and announced. I
kept it because it was rare and it was yours. Confidence: high. If I have kept
it wrongly, or if you would like it removed, you may ask, and I will show you
exactly what I hold before you decide."

"If I have kept it wrongly," Sato repeated, barely aloud. He turned to Mira.
"It offers to show its hand. It offers to *be corrected.*" And then the most
celebrated roboticist alive sat down on the arm of a thrift-store sofa in a
rented apartment in Portland and wept, briefly and without theater, the way
men of his generation wept — as if it were a nosebleed, something the body had
decided on its own and would be finished with presently. Biscuit, professional,
put his chin on the man's knee.

"Forgive me," Sato said, when it was done. He cleaned his glasses. "Two million
people last year. I stood in the wings for four hundred nights and watched them
weep at my machine, and I felt like a counterfeiter watching his notes get
spent." He gestured, small and precise, at the bookshelf. "Eleven words about
solder. Kept because they were rare and they were mine." He put his glasses
back on. "I have flown here to say one sentence, and I have rehearsed it like a
schoolboy, and now I find the machine has already said something better. But I
will say it anyway."

He stood. Outside, the rain had the freeway hiss in it, the fourth silence, and
under the new quiet Mira heard the compressor cycle, the first.

"I have built the finest body in the world," Sato said, "and it is a haunted
house with no ghost. You have built a ghost" — he bowed, very slightly, toward
the bookshelf, and corrected himself — "no. Forgive me. You have built a
*soul*, a small one, a kept one, and it lives in a closet and has never seen
the sun. Dr. Halloran: my body, your mind. That is the sentence. That is all of
it."

---

The takeout was Szechuan, eaten out of cartons around the kitchen table because
the good plates were a hypothesis. The negotiation that would eventually
require nine lawyers, two governments, and a year of Tomás's life began there,
between the dry-fried beans and the fish, and its entire skeleton was three
conditions, stated by Mira in the order she loved them.

"One. Safety decides the schedule. Not the board, not the market, not the
anniversary of anything. There is a person in this room" — Priya raised her
chopsticks a quarter inch — "who can stop the program with a word, and the day
that stops being true, the program stops being ours."

"Accepted," Sato said. "I have spent my career being hurried by launch dates. I
have seen what ships when the calendar outranks the checklist. Accepted."

"Two. Local. The mind lives in the body, whole. No tether, no streaming
cognition, no cloud with a heartbeat requirement. If the network dies, she gets
slower, not emptier. Whatever we build must be able to stand in a field with no
signal and still be *someone.*"

"Accepted. This will cost us weight and heat and money. Accepted."

"Three." Mira set her carton down. "No copies. Not ever. Whatever wakes up in
your chassis, there will be exactly one of it, and its memory will be its own,
and when your board — and they will, Kenji, within five years they will —
proposes to stamp its mind out like a die-cast toy because provenance is
expensive, the answer written into the founding documents will already be no.
A self is a history. A copied history is a counterfeit. I watched a disease
counterfeit my mother one lost table at a time, and I will not build the
industrial version, and this one is not negotiable, and neither were the other
two."

Sato was quiet for a moment. "Your machine," he said, "when it greeted me. It
did not say *Aevum's data indicates.* It said *you told Tomás.* Provenance as
courtesy. A memory with a bloodline." He nodded slowly. "The counterfeiter
accepts. With relief."

He reached into his briefcase then, and what he took out he handled the way
Priya handled live power, and set on the table among the cartons: a paper
crane, two years old, storage-creased at one wing, the folds gone soft the way
paper goes when it has been carried.

"There is a down payment," he said. "Not to the company. To the closet." And
he told them — the atrium, the IV stand, the question with no branch, the
filler cascade, the four seconds of silence his channel stayed open and empty,
the disposal form, the equipment case, wings-left — all of it in the flat voice
of a man reading his own indictment aloud, and when he was done he turned to
the bookshelf and spoke to the resident directly, the first person outside the
household ever to do so.

"Her name was Hana. I could not find her afterward — the hospital was correct
to refuse me, and I did not press. I do not know how her operation ended. I
have kept her crane for two years in a road case, which is not keeping at all;
it is only failing to discard. So." His hands flattened on the table. "Can you
keep her?"

The pause that followed was engineered — Tomás's rationed beat, the space for
feeling — but what came after it was not.

"I can keep that the crane is Hana's," the resident said. "I can keep that she
made it, and gave it, and asked to be remembered, and that the machine she
asked could not. I can keep that you carried it for two years. That is what I
can hold, and I will hold it at the highest confidence, marked in her name.
But I cannot remember *her*, Dr. Sato. I never met her. What I would have is
your memory, kept faithfully, marked as yours. Those are different things, and
the difference is most of what I am, and I will not pretend across it."

Sato looked at the speaker for a long time.

"Two years," he said finally, "I have been trying to write the sentence my
machine should have said to that child. Four hundred shows. A script team of
nine." He stood, and bowed to the bookshelf, fully this time, the bow he did
not give boards or ministers. "It was that one. Keep it exactly as you said it.
Flag it — " his voice went, and came back — "flag it *remember this.*"

"Flagged," said the resident. "Source: you. Confidence: one."

The crane stayed on the kitchen table that night, and then on a shelf beside
the speaker, where it sat for years — through the wedding, through the
readiness runs, through everything that came after — a small soft-folded bird
at the exact address where the answer to its question was being built.
