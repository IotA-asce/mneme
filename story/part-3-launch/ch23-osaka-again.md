# Chapter 23 — Osaka, Again

**OSAKA — 2040**

Sato dropped the same screw three times and blamed the screw.

It was an M1.4 pan head, six millimeters long, black oxide, manufactured to a tolerance he had once described in a deposition as “offensively generous.” It lay on the white bench mat between his hands while he explained to Mira that contemporary fasteners lacked discipline.

“The screw is from 2017,” she said.

“Exactly. Standards have been declining longer than I thought.”

He picked it up on the fourth attempt and set it into the left eyelid assembly of a GENNI-1 stage chassis.

The workshop had not changed enough. Aevum had built two towers and a public gallery around it, replaced the ventilation twice, and added floor markings in three colors required by an insurer in Singapore. The old benches remained. Their maple tops carried burns from soldering irons held by engineers who had retired, died, or become executives and therefore more dangerous. Drawers still had handwritten labels. The air still held rosin, metal, and the warm mineral scent of motors under test.

Sato had once told a video call he missed the smell. The resident had remembered. Ten years later, Mira stood in the source of the memory and watched him hide an illness badly.

“Your turn,” he said.

She took the driver. The v1 eyelid used a cable return no Aevum chassis had carried since 2021. Its actuator was slow, loud, and prone to a tiny stall halfway down that made the stage face look skeptical during sleep. There was no reason to rebuild it. Sato had found the assembly in an archive cabinet, placed it on the bench, and sent Mira a photograph with the message: **Osaka. Again. Bring your bad opinions.**

She had come the next week.

Neither of them mentioned the board meeting she was supposed to attend in Portland. Tomás could frighten directors without supervision. Dana had taken June to the coast. Jenny was in Kyoto mediating a fleet consent dispute and had been told only that Mira and Sato were repairing old hardware.

This last omission had the shape of a lie, though no statement in it was false.

“Cable tension?” Sato asked.

“Point eight newtons.”

“Point seven.”

“Point eight if you want both lids to close before morning.”

“Point seven if you want them to open again.”

They argued for six minutes, found the original specification — 0.75 — and each claimed the document favored them.

It was the most pleasant hour Mira had experienced in months.

---

Sato forgot the washer at 11:20.

Not where it was. What it was for.

He held the crescent shim under the task light and turned it over twice. “This sits behind—”

The sentence stopped.

Mira knew nine kinds of silence. This was not one of them. It was forgetting in public: the pause after a name had left but before anyone agreed to notice. Her mother had inhabited it for years, first in grocery aisles and then everywhere. Mira had learned to wait without rescuing, because rescue turned every missing word into a witness.

Sato looked at the assembly. The path remained visible: cable, cam, return spring, crescent shim behind the upper guide.

“Upper guide,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I was testing you.”

“You failed to ask a question.”

“Advanced test.”

He seated the shim. His hand shook once, enough to make metal tick against metal, then steadied when the tool took load. Mira watched his fingers and hated herself for turning motion into data.

In the drawer behind him lay two clinic envelopes. Their return address was visible. Neurology. Sato had put them under a packet of cable ties with the studied care of a man hiding a lighthouse behind a fern.

“How long?” Mira asked.

“The cable?”

“Don't.”

He sat back on the stool. The workshop continued around them: a mill behind glass, compressed air, a cart squeaking at one wheel. None of the younger engineers looked over. Sato had chosen the old bench in the far alcove because it could be mistaken for nostalgia.

“I am not keeping a secret,” he said.

“You have neurology letters under zip ties.”

“A secret is better organized.”

“How long?”

“Long enough to be bored by the question.”

“Kenji.”

He flinched at his first name. In the decade she had known him, she had used it fewer than twenty times, all of them load-bearing.

“Last winter,” he said. “Tests in spring. More tests after.”

“And you told?”

“My doctor. Very responsible.”

“Priya?”

“Would remove all sharp tools and replace the stairs with a readiness gate.”

“Tomás?”

“Would make a spreadsheet called Sato Transition and hide crying in column G.”

“Jenny?”

He looked down at the open eyelid.

There it was: not fear of Jenny's grief, though he feared that too. Fear of becoming a high-salience event that expanded until it obscured every room behind it. Fear that the body he had built would watch his body fail with fidelity neither of them could control.

Mira reached for the clinic envelopes. Sato put one hand over them.

“No,” he said.

“I'm not opening them.”

“You are arranging them.”

She stopped. Her fingers had squared the corners with the bench.

He smiled without triumph. “You see? We all have symptoms.”

---

They ate lunch from the vending machines because Sato insisted this preserved workshop authenticity.

Mira's noodles were hot in two places and cold in the middle. Sato bought sweet bean bread, took one bite, and set it beside the screwdriver. He had lost weight. This observation joined the others without permission: collar loose, left hand slower, three untouched cups of tea, the way he braced before standing. Her mind scored and promoted. Grief-first engineering had made her excellent at detecting disappearance and useless at preventing it.

“You have to tell them,” she said.

“I will.”

“Date.”

“This is not a gate review.”

“Everything is a gate review if you irritate Priya enough.”

“After the Kyoto case. Jenny should finish that without carrying me into the room.”

“She is capable of holding two things.”

“I know exactly what she is capable of holding.”

His voice rose on the last word, and two engineers looked over, then looked away under Sato's glare.

Mira folded the paper sleeve from her chopsticks into smaller rectangles. June had taught her this, or she had taught June; provenance in families was mostly circular.

“She gets to decide what she carries,” Mira said.

“Yes.”

“Then why are you deciding when she knows?”

“Because time is still mine.”

The sentence was quiet. It stopped her.

He did not owe disclosure merely because they loved him. Privacy did not expire at diagnosis. Mira had built this rule for strangers and found it nearly intolerable in a friend.

“After Kyoto,” she said.

“After Kyoto.”

It was not a concession from him. It was a boundary she agreed to keep.

---

The eyelid closed at 15:43.

Too fast on the first run. It struck the lower stop with a plastic click that made both of them recoil. Sato blamed Mira's cable tension. Mira blamed his prehistoric spring. They measured, adjusted, and ran it again.

This time the lid descended with the old stage grace: not a blink, which is interruption, but the slow sleep cue GENNI-1 had performed at the end of shows while an empty system waited for power-down. Halfway, the cable hesitated.

“There,” Mira said.

“Bearing.”

“Cam.”

“Cable routing.”

“Your point-seven compromise.”

They disassembled the whole thing to discover a burr on the upper guide, small enough to catch only under warm load. Sato held it to the light. His hand was steady now.

“Thirty years,” he said. “We blamed the cable.”

“Twenty-three.”

“I am rounding for grief.”

He polished the burr with a stone, four careful strokes. On the next run, the eyelid passed the old failure point and came to rest. The face on the rig appeared peacefully asleep, although there had never been anyone inside it to wake.

Sato reached under the bench and brought out a steel case Mira remembered from
first light. The hinges were new. Everything else had been handled for decades:
calipers polished pale at the grips, ceramic tweezers, two torque drivers, a
bundle of gauges in paper sleeves marked in Sato's handwriting. On top lay a
brass key with no teeth.

“The executive archive,” he said.

“That key opens nothing.”

“Correct. The archive is digital. The key is ceremony.”

“You hate ceremony longer than eleven minutes.”

“This will take two.”

The body-side stewardship file held every Aevum tolerance judgment that had
never fit a drawing: which polymer whitened before it failed, which suppliers'
bearings changed sound in coastal air, why the Nine's left wrist should be
tested warm after any fall even when telemetry remained green. Sato had built
it across thirty-eight years and three migrations. Access had always required
his signature and that of a current workshop lead. The new authorization on
the case named Mira.

“No,” she said.

“Excellent opening. Incorrect conclusion.”

“I'm not a roboticist.”

“You spent this morning insulting cable tension with professional confidence.”

“I build minds.”

“You built the separation that keeps minds from commanding wrists. Therefore
you are the only founder I trust to remember that bodies are not accessories.”

He pushed the case across the bench. Mira did not touch it.

“Kenji, appoint Ando. Appoint Watanabe. Appoint someone who knows what all
these gauges do.”

“Watanabe is workshop steward. You are covenant steward. Different job.” He
tapped the blank key. “When a board asks for thinner wrists because the market
likes them, you ask what happens in a fall. When an engineer calls a reflex
legacy, you ask what it protects before approving its deletion. You do not
need to turn the screw. You need to remember why someone must.”

“This should happen formally.”

“It is happening formally. There are tools. We are disagreeing. That is how
all our important governance happened before lawyers made transcripts.”

He removed his old brass workshop badge and set it in the case. Badge 0001.
The gesture was nearly theatrical, and for once he did not make a joke before
it could become tender.

“The body is not mine,” he said. “It never was. But I kept watch over the
people who knew it. Keep watch over the watching.”

Mira picked up the toothless key. It weighed less than the promise attached to
it, which was true of most objects used for vows.

“Two minutes,” she said.

“One forty-eight. Honest ceremony.”

Sato watched the closed lid.

“When it ends,” he said, “Jenny should let my ending fade.”

Mira set down the meter.

He continued adjusting the return spring so he would not have to look up. “Let her remember me as I was, not as I end. Promise me she'll be allowed to.”

The request was engineered correctly. He did not ask Mira to order Jenny to forget. He did not ask for purge, or suppression, or a false version in which his decline never happened. He asked for permission: no corporate retention hold, no clinical archive justified as research, no family plea that turned the worst months into a monument. Let the ending travel the same curve as everything else. Let the rare survive by its own claim.

Mira thought of Eileen's last room. For years afterward the room had arrived before the kitchens, the winter drives, the story of the kingfisher. The ending had been newest and therefore easiest to retrieve. Memory was not justice. It needed time to become proportion.

“I can't promise what she'll keep,” she said.

“I know.”

“I won't ask her to erase anything.”

“I know.”

“I can promise no one will make her keep the clinical record vivid. Not Halcyon. Not me.”

Sato waited.

“I promise she'll be allowed to let it fade.”

He bowed his head over the screwdriver. “Good.”

Neither of them said the name printed inside the clinic envelopes.

---

They put the eyelid assembly back in the archive cabinet at dusk.

Mira wanted to label the repaired guide. Sato refused. “Let the next pair of arrogant engineers discover we were here.”

“They'll replace the cable.”

“Then they deserve the extra work.”

He closed the drawer and missed the latch the first time. Mira did not move to help. On the second try it caught.

Outside, Osaka shone in wet neon. The rain had started while they worked, a soft electrical hiss on the workshop roof. Sato stood at the loading entrance with his coat over one arm and looked back at the benches.

“Tell Jenny tomorrow,” he said.

“After Kyoto.”

“Kyoto ended an hour ago. She sent me the decision. I was negotiating for one more night.”

Mira stared at him.

“Did you win?” she asked.

“No. But I enjoyed the attempt.”

He put on his coat slowly. She did not take it from him. Love, Mira was learning, was full of actions withheld at great expense.

They walked to the station under one umbrella, arguing about whether 0.75 newtons constituted a compromise or a vindication. Sato maintained his pace until the final crossing, where the signal began to flash. Mira expected him to hurry. He did not.

Cars passed in sheets of rain. Sato watched the countdown reach zero without
apology. Years earlier he would have crossed on the flashing light and made
the city accommodate his momentum. Mira understood that not hurrying could be
defeat or wisdom and that love did not grant her the instrument to distinguish
them. When green returned, he offered her the umbrella handle as if the delay
had been her convenience.

They crossed at the pace the light allowed.

Behind them, in the old workshop, a repaired eyelid rested closed over an empty chassis, the burr gone, the hesitation no longer reproducible, and no record left to prove which of them had found it.
