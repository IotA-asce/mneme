# Chapter 23 — Osaka, Again

**OSAKA — 2040**

Sato had begun scheduling her for the mornings, and Mira knew what mornings
were for.

She had said it herself once, years ago, on the eve of a switch-on: hospitals
put the hard good things at the start of the day, when the staff is fresh and
the family has slept, or has at least lain horizontal in the dark, rehearsing.
Sato's calendar had been a scandal for as long as she had known him — dinners
that became demos, demos that became dawn, a man who treated the clock as a
colleague he could charm. Now his assistant sent an itinerary with everything
of weight stacked before noon and the afternoons marked, in careful English,
*protected*, and Mira read the word the way she read logs: for what it was
not saying.

She noticed the rest at the quarterly, in the order concealments are always
noticed. Props first.

The belt was new work on old leather — two fresh holes past the polished one,
punched clean with an awl, evenly spaced, deburred. A man hiding weight loss
from a boardroom buys a new belt. A craftsman hiding it from himself does it
with an awl, to tolerance. His shirts had been taken in by someone who knew
what they were doing, which was worse than if they had hung loose. There was
a chair now wherever he was going to be standing. He rose from it in two
installments, like a man double-checking arithmetic he used to do in his
head, and he took the elevator, and he had stopped hurrying — Sato, who had
moved through buildings his whole life like a stage manager at the
half-hour call.

She had audited a concealment before. Her mother had run one for the better
part of a year — a different disease, a different set of props — and the
grammar was identical: precision as camouflage, generosity as misdirection,
the schedule quietly rewritten so that no one ever saw the afternoons. He was
hiding it the way he did everything, beautifully and in plain sight, which is
to say: badly. Mira sat through the quarterly reading all of it at a glance,
the way you read a trace you have seen before, and said nothing, because the
first rule of a concealment run by someone you love is that storming it wins
you nothing but the props.

Neither of them said anything. That was the treaty, unsigned, in force from
the first morning.

---

He ambushed his own agenda, which was how she knew the whole day had been
machined in advance.

"Item one," he said, when the room had emptied down to the two co-chairs and
the tea had stopped being refilled, and he slid a folder across the table
with two fingers, the way you advance a pawn you have already decided to
lose.

Inside was a single page and an appendix. The page was headed SUCCESSION —
AEVUM CO-CHAIR, and under it three names, ranked, each with four lines of
reasons in his own hand — he had typed nothing; he wanted the provenance
unmistakable. Maeda's name sat exactly where she expected it. The effective
date was blank.

"Kenji—"

"The date is blank," he said, "because it will be filled in by events.
Co-chairs do not negotiate with the tide. We build for it." He tapped the
appendix without opening it. "Transition mechanics. Tomás has seen the
structure, not the names. Priya has seen nothing, because this is
governance, and she is saving herself for machinery, as is correct."

The memo's stated rationale was *orderly transition* and *the demographic
arithmetic of a man born in 1972*, which was the closest either of them
would come, all day, to the subject of the day. She read it twice and
signed the acknowledgment line, and her pen was the loudest thing in the
room.

He looked at his watch with open satisfaction. "Eleven minutes," he said.
"The maximum honest duration of any ceremony." He stood — two installments;
she watched the wall — and straightened his cuffs. "Good. That is the
paperwork. Now I need your hands. There is real work downstairs."

---

Downstairs was two badge readers and a fire door below the Osaka campus's
polished floors, on the level the directory still called PERFORMANCE
SYSTEMS — STORAGE, a department of one that had not shipped anything in a
decade and had never once been proposed for consolidation, because its
department head was on the founding documents and everyone understood the
budget line to be a headstone.

The racks ran back into dark economy lighting. Crates stenciled 01, 02, 04.
And past them, uncrated, on her old transport stand under a fitted cover,
the one the manifests listed as Unit 03 and the technicians of another era
had called Osaka Primary — the home-stage unit, the one who had worked the
hall you could see from this building's roof. The tag on the stand had gone
amber with years: *03 — RETAINED. REFERENCE. — K.S.*

Sato folded the cover back from her face and did not say anything for a
while.

Mira had seen that face on the sides of buildings, on a farewell broadcast,
in a hundred engineering reviews. She had never stood in front of it. It was
— there was no serious argument otherwise — the most beautiful machine ever
made, and dormancy had cost it nothing, because nothing was what it held;
the stillness was a pose held between poses, waiting for a cue that had been
canceled in writing ten years ago. Beside her Sato looked, and Mira watched
him look, and kept her breathing quiet, the way you do at the edge of
someone else's graveside.

"Reference," she said at last, reading the tag. "Reference for what?"

"Calibration," Sato said. "Every discipline should keep one perfect example
of its mistake." He drew the cover back down over the face with two hands,
with the care of a man covering a piano, or a patient. "She is not why we
are here. She is only on the way."

---

The workshop was behind the last rack: one bench, one stool, one chair that
was newer than everything else in the room and positioned where an assistant
must have insisted. The bench predated Aevum's logo; the iron on it predated
Aevum. The drawers rose in banks to the ceiling, labeled in Japanese in a
young man's handwriting, and every bank wore a fresh paper tag with a
checked box, and a clipboard hung by the door with the inventory half done.
Mira declined to read what the inventory was for. Dying men count their
drawers. Her mother had alphabetized a pantry.

The drawer he wanted was already out on the bench: EYELID, LEFT — v1. Inside,
in cut foam, lay a mechanism older than most of his staff — cam, follower,
hairspring, a lid shell of vacuum-cast resin gone the color of weak tea, a
flex joint whose solder had crystallized to frost. First-generation. The
one he had built before anyone paid him to.

"Why?" she said, meaning why this, why today, and knowing.

"Pleasure," he said, meaning all of it. "You have been building minds for
fourteen years, Doctor. When did you last make a thing you could hold?"

So they rebuilt it, the two of them, at one bench, on one morning that had
been protected from everything else on earth. She stripped and dressed the
flex joint while he recut the cam, and they fell within minutes into the
argument, which was the point of the assembly, which had been the point of
the drawer, which was the pleasure.

"Eighty microns on the cam clearance," he said.

"The drawing says one-twenty." She had the drawing; he had archived every
drawing he had ever made, this man whose stages remembered nothing.

"The drawing was for technicians. Eighty."

"Then why keep drawings at all?"

"So that there is something official to deviate from." He held the follower
up to the light, turned it. "One-twenty, and the lid arrives on time, like
an employee. Eighty, and it arrives like weather. Audiences forgave her
everything because of that eighty. They never knew what it was they were
forgiving."

"Eighty binds in August," Mira said. "Resin swells. You wrote the humidity
memo yourself; I've cited it."

"So in August she blinks slowly." He set the follower down. "Who does not?"

They settled at ninety-five the way the long-married settle: both certain,
neither keeping score, the score kept anyway, somewhere, lovingly. It struck
her — threading the hairspring while he held the shell steady, his hand not
quite steady, neither of them remarking on which of the two of them was now
the fixture — that she had spent nine years across a table from this man
with a company between them, and this was the first time they had ever made
something together with four hands. The minds and the bodies had always met
downstream, in her. In Jenny. The two of them had only ever met in minutes
and in ceremony, eleven honest minutes at a time.

He lit the iron. It ticked as it heated, an old sound, and then the first
touch of solder to the tip sent up the thin sweet pine-smoke curl of rosin,
and the smell walked into the room like a third colleague.

"You know," Sato said, lightly, flooding the first pad, "ten years ago I
told a machine of yours, over a bad connection, that I missed this smell."

"You told Tomás. The machine only kept it."

"The machine kept it correctly. That is not *only*." He fed the solder in,
drew a fillet you could have published. "My wife used to say the rosin was
in all my clothes. Thirty years of coats. I checked, after. She was right —
it is in them still. It outlasted her." He said it with no weight on it at
all, which was how he carried the heaviest things. "I have decided the
remark is repaid. A man should not go around missing what he can arrange to
be in the room with."

"Noted for the record," Mira said, and he smiled at the joint, and for a
few minutes there was only the tick of the iron and the small weather of
the smoke.

He repositioned the assembly in the vise, took a fresh length of solder,
set the tip to the coil leads. The joint went bright. He did not look up.

"When I die," he said, in the voice he used for tolerances, "let her
remember me as I was, not as I end. Promise me she will be allowed to."

The smoke went up between them, straight, unhurried. Mira understood, in
the second the sentence took, the entire engineering of the day — the
folder, the blank date, the covered face on the way, the drawer already
out on the bench: a jig. He had machined the whole morning so that this one
sentence could be made to specification, hands occupied, eyes down, no
fixture anywhere in the room for pity to clamp to. The smell does not look
up. He had built a place to say it the way he built everything, so well you
could not see the work.

She did not say *you have years*, because he might, and it would insult the
sentence. She did not say *she would never* — Jenny's fidelity was the exact
danger; a mind that could keep his last months at first-light resolution,
forever, out of love, unless the love was allowed its other mode. He knew
what he had helped build. He was asking for the mercy of its off-switch, and
not even for the switch — for permission. For her to hold the door of the
covenant open when the board, the memorials, the world's whole instinct for
keeping would push it shut. *Let her. Allowed to.* He had chosen the verbs
the way he chose clearances.

"She'll be allowed," Mira said. "By everyone. Whatever it costs. I promise."

He nodded at the joint, once, and finished it.

"Allowed," he said. "Not instructed. She will choose the amounts herself.
She chose right about the dog."

"She did."

"Then it is a solved problem," he said, "being executed on a schedule," and
capped the flux, and the subject was closed, to tolerance, forever.

"Filed," Mira said, when she trusted her voice to hold the joke level.
"Source: you. Confidence: one."

He laughed then, the real one, the one from the wedding feed and the first
light, and inspected her flex joint with theatrical suspicion, and passed
it.

The last joint was the lid shell to the linkage, and he handed her the iron.

"Your joint," he said. "My bench. Her lid."

He sat — one installment this time; the chair was closer than it looked —
and folded his hands, and supervised the way she understood he now intended
to do everything: seated, exact, and letting someone else hold the heat. She
soldered under his eye. She had not soldered under anyone's eye since her
mother's kitchen table, a radio kit, 1999. Her fillet was not publishable.
It would hold.

They powered the assembly from the bench supply, two clip leads, three and
a half volts, and the lid — forty years old, argued to ninety-five microns,
smelling of pine — closed once, and opened, slow as August.

"Everyone believes the eye is the hard part," Sato said, watching it.
"The eye is glass. The lid is the life. A camera never blinks. Only a thing
with a stake in the next moment closes its eyes and trusts that there will
be one."

They ran it eleven times. It arrived like weather.

---

He walked her up to the lobby himself, into the noon glare, the protected
afternoon already rising around him like a tide she was not going to be
shown. At the doors he shook her hand, formally, absurdly, as if they had
just met, or as if a document had been executed, which one had.

"Come back in the autumn," he said. "We will do the right eye."

"I'll bring the drawing," she said. "You bring something official to
deviate from."

In the taxi she caught it on her cuff — pine and tin, faint, already going —
and rode with her wrist near her face like a fool, thinking of a woman she
had never met, who had lived thirty years inside that smell and understood
everything it meant without once being told. Her mother arrived then too,
the way Eileen arrived now, unbidden, on her own recognizance: the lists in
the drawers, the pantry in perfect order, the year of props. Neither disease
had been named all day. Names were for the drawings — the official thing to
deviate from — and the day had been exact anyway, to tolerance.

Somewhere over the Pacific she noticed she had not written the promise
down, and was not going to. It had a source and a confidence. It was already
in the only format that had ever mattered, filed in two places, one of them
failing, one of them hers.

She kept her coat on the whole flight home.
