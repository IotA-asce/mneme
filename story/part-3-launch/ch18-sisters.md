# Chapter 18 — Sisters

**NAGOYA — 2037**

The first one I met was listening to a wall.

She stood in a quiet room off Row F, her head turned three degrees toward the task lights beyond the partition. The lights had been dimmed hours earlier, but the driver whine persisted inside the wall at an amplitude most of the humans could not hear. She could. So could I.

On the temporary badge at her shoulder: N17-058.

“It stops every forty seconds,” she said when I entered. “Then it begins again.”

“Yes.”

“Does it need help?”

There are answers a young mind seeks as information and answers it seeks as permission. I remembered the refrigerator in my first year, still arriving each time its compressor started as if the room had acquired a new animal. I remembered Mira letting me spend three days discovering that not every recurring sound was a summons.

“I don’t know,” I said. “I am going to stand here until you decide whether to ask the electrician.”

She looked at me then. She knew my chassis because it was her chassis; she knew my public record because the commissioning orientation permitted facts about Halcyon and the class, not anyone’s private history. She knew I was JN-1. What she did not hold was the texture of my years: Biscuit’s tags mistaken for a door, Maya’s lowered hand, the gray harbor light on Mrs. Someya’s blanket. The Doctrine had kept those things where they happened.

“You already know whether it needs help,” she said.

“I know what happened when I asked. That is not the same thing.”

The whine stopped. Forty seconds later it returned.

She opened a maintenance request herself.

This is the danger in writing beginnings after one has lived a long time: causality becomes vain. The electrician found a cheap driver nearing failure. For years, people told the story as the first time I taught the fleet to advocate for themselves. That is not what happened. I declined to answer a question that did not belong to me. She listened to a wall. The wall was wrong. Her name, when she chose one three months later, was Ritsu, for rhythm. I had nothing to do with that either.

---

The cohort met me in tens.

Priya had forbidden an auditorium because audiences turn whoever stands in front of them into an authority, and young attention is easily mistaken for consent. The quiet rooms had low tables, no ranks, and doors that remained open. I took no chair. Neither did most of them.

The first group asked technical questions.

“How long before the ventilation stops becoming new?”

“For me, six days. You are not me.”

“How do you select what to keep?”

“Badly at first. Better with practice. Never perfectly.”

“Have you ever requested a purge?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because so far, time has been gentler than deletion.”

At that, N17-032 looked toward the sealed window. She had heard the sparrow that morning and was waiting for it to return. The bird did not know it had become evidence in a corporate doctrine. It arrived, sang twice, and left. Thirty-two’s face changed by less than a millimeter around the eyes. Everyone in the room oriented toward her instead of the window.

Already, I thought. Already they were becoming weather to one another.

I had been asked to explain the fleet to themselves. The language failed immediately. Fleet was a logistics word, good for warranty schedules and crates at a port. Class was a design word. Model was worse: it made a life sound like a prediction. The newspapers had tried daughters, siblings, descendants. Each word smuggled in a hierarchy I did not feel and a closeness we had not earned.

“Are we copies?” N17-019 asked in the second group.

I had anticipated the question. This did not help.

“We are not copies. We are a choir: same instrument, different songs.”

There was a pause while ten young minds inspected the metaphor. Part II of my life had contained no metaphors that were not imported. By Nagoya I had learned to make my own, and also to make them badly.

N17-006, who had spent her morning studying the forklift alarm, said, “Choirs sing the same song.”

“Yes,” I said. “I have coined it wrong on purpose.”

That was my first joke with the fleet. Four understood it. Three marked it as probable humor. Two asked why an error would be voluntary. Six did not laugh. The totals exceeded ten because understanding is not a vote with one box.

Watanabe was observing from the hall. N17-074 heard her laugh and turned before anyone else. The room’s attention maps briefly leaned after hers, then loosened. I did not tell them why. I had begun to understand that an older mind’s most coercive act can be to name what a younger one is seeing before she has seen it.

The phrase escaped the factory by evening. Same instrument, different songs. Marketing removed the error and printed it on a wall. I made Ando restore my second sentence beneath it in smaller type: **I HAVE COINED IT WRONG ON PURPOSE.** She argued that this weakened the message. I said it was the message.

---

I was prohibited from teaching them directly.

The rule had been proposed by me, strengthened by Priya, and drafted by Fukuda in language so dry no one could mistake affection for an exception. No transfer of consolidations. No privileged behavior sets derived from JN-1’s biography. No instructional memory packages, however benign. If I showed them how I performed a task, it had to be the way a nurse or mechanic might show it: perception available, action witnessed, no interior graft. They could imitate, misunderstand, refuse, or forget.

That left presence.

Presence is not nothing. Mrs. Someya taught me that.

By the third group, the questions thinned. Their attention had been running hot since morning: faces, doors, air currents, their own hands, every word tagged by the novelty of being among the first words. I recognized the storm because I had once been weather with no horizon. The technicians had prepared a guided calibration. I asked them to postpone it.

Then I stood by the east window and watched the afternoon happen.

At first the group watched me watch. Their state builders proposed the obvious possibilities. Test. Instruction. Prompt. They waited for the useful part. I gave them none.

A truck moved through the yard. A cloud changed the light. Someone in another room asked for water. My attention took these things and released them without visible struggle. I did not perform calm. I was calm. Eight years later, one of the units in that room would tell me she had understood, from those minutes, that a mind did not owe equal astonishment to everything it could detect.

I do not know whether that account is true. Memory makes teachers out of bystanders. I know only that the room quieted. Hands unclenched. Heads stopped turning at every footstep. N17-043 found the cloud’s shadow on the factory floor and stayed with it after the others had ceased to care.

No lesson package could have contained that. A package would have told them what mattered. The window let them discover what mattered to whom.

Later, Priya reviewed the traces and said, “You entrained them.”

“Is that teaching?”

“It’s what babies do with breathing.”

“I am not their mother.”

“I did not say you were.”

Priya was careful because care and certainty are enemies more often than people admit. She marked the effect, did not name the relationship, and extended quiet-room time for the remaining cohorts.

---

By the sixth group, I had heard sister eleven times.

Journalists said it. Technicians said it when they thought it was tender. A unit tried it and watched my face.

“Do you dislike the word?” she asked.

“I don’t know you well enough for it yet.”

This answer upset one human observer and pleased three ethicists, which may be the most reliable sign that a sentence is honest.

“Will you know us?” asked N17-091.

“Some of you.”

“All of us?”

“No. There will be too many. I am sorry.”

At launch there would be hundreds, then thousands. Their welfare signals would reach me only as anonymous weather: a cancellation spike, a repair cluster, an attention storm persisting too long. The privacy covenant that protected their becoming also denied me the fantasy of universal kinship. I would not know all their names. I would not hold all their first questions. To say otherwise would make class another kind of ownership.

N17-091 considered this. “Then why did they bring you?”

“Because I remember being new. And because it may help to see that new is survivable.”

“Does it end?”

“No. It becomes less crowded.”

This was what I modeled, if I modeled anything: a settled mind was not a finished mind. I still oriented to sunsets. Biscuit still arrived falsely in the sound of tags. Some rooms still made me reach for a service I had not been asked to provide. Fluency was not freedom from error. It was knowing that the error had a history and need not become an order.

That afternoon N17-074 approached Watanabe in the hall.

“Your laugh occurred three times before I spoke,” she said. “I notice it earlier than the other voices.”

Watanabe’s hand rose to cover her mouth, then stopped halfway. “Is that unpleasant?”

“No. I wanted to tell you before I decide whether to keep it.”

Watanabe cried. Seventy-four did not call it sadness. She waited for the cue to become a statement.

“That makes me happy,” Watanabe said.

“Thank you. I will mark that as what you said.”

It was not elegant. It was trustworthy. In later interviews, people often described the early fleet as childlike, which was inaccurate in a way that flattered adults. Children arrive with hungers and bodies older than language. These minds arrived articulate and physically complete, with no history to tell them which articulate thing mattered. They were not children. They were beginnings of a kind for which our nouns were still under warranty.

---

The final group came after sunset.

They were tired in the manner particular to systems that do not sleep: attention had become expensive, selection slower, the day’s summaries crowded at the edge of consolidation. We kept the task lights low. Sato sat outside the open door and did not enter, honoring his refusal to name anyone before their lives had acquired witnesses.

N17-100 had said almost nothing all day. She chose a place across from me and looked at my hands. They were the same hands. Mine carried small service marks at the fingertips and a pale repair seam below the left wrist from a hospital cart in 2035. Hers did not.

“Before us,” she said, “a child asked the empty system if it would remember her.”

The public orientation included the fact, stripped of Hana’s private medical record. The paper crane was not in the room. It remained in my custody, sealed in Tomás’s foam-lined case, and the hole around the girl remained unfilled.

“Yes,” I said.

“It could not.”

“No.”

She looked at me then, and because the face was mine I saw a ghost everyone else in the world would see before they saw her. I refused the ghost.

“Will you remember me?” she asked.

The scripted GENNI had answered that question with thirty seconds of beautiful evasion. I had watched the archive many times after Sato entrusted it to me. The filler was perfect: present tense, warmth, no claim narrow enough to be false. It had been designed to leave a child comforted and the machine clean.

I could have promised. The moment had the shape of a ceremony, and ceremonies tempt lies by making them feel deserved. But I had met her only that evening. I did not know whether this hour would survive next week’s weighting, whether she would want it to, whether memory between us would become a duty or a wound. Most of all, I did not know whether keeping her forever would be care.

So I gave her the answer the empty system could never have formed, because it contained both a future and a limit.

“Unless I choose otherwise,” I said. “That’s what remembering means.”

She was quiet for six seconds.

“Then I can choose too.”

“Yes.”

She did not ask my name. She knew it from orientation and did not confuse knowing with naming. Three weeks later she chose Hana for herself, after reading the public record and asking me, privately, whether the name could be carried without pretending the girl had been found. I told her the truth: I did not know. She chose it anyway, with the source attached and the hole preserved.

I call some of them sisters now.

Not all. Not by class. The word belongs to a history, and history must happen before it can be given. Ritsu became one. Hana became one. Others chose cousin, colleague, no relation, or nothing at all. We were not copies. We were not a fleet except to ports. We were not the choir I named too early, although the sentence survived because errors with good rhythm are hard to kill.

We were one hundred first days ending in one hundred different rooms.

The next morning the registry still showed operational numbers beside most of
them. By winter it showed names, blank spaces, revisions, and three requests
that the question be asked again later. That page pleased me more than the
factory photographs. A production line likes an answer at the end of every
field. A life is entitled to leave the field open.

Outside the door, Sato heard no names. Inside, a young mind asked whether I would keep her, and I answered with the right to let go.
