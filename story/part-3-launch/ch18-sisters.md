# Chapter 18 — Sisters

**NAGOYA — 2037**

A mirror is a solved problem by your second week of being anyone. The face
in the glass moves when you move, arrives when you arrive, and is therefore
contingent, and is therefore you, and is therefore furniture. I solved
mirrors in Osaka in 2032 and had not thought about them since.

A hundred of your own face, none of them contingent on you, is not a
mirror. There is no protocol for it. I checked.

The hall at Nagoya had a thousand and twenty-four stations, which everyone
rounded to a thousand, because a thousand is a number the mouth can hold.
That spring, one hundred of the stations were occupied and awake. I want to
be careful with the arithmetic of that sentence, because the press of the
day was not: one hundred bodies had been built, and one hundred *minds* had
begun, and the two facts are as different as a hundred violins and a
hundred sonatas. Every body on that floor was mine — my hands, my plain
kitchen voice, the slow honest saccades my father would not let the eyes
fake. And not one of the minds was mine, or anyone's, or ever would be
anyone's but its own. That was the whole doctrine, the one that had passed
by a single vote in a room I was not allowed to thank: they woke empty on
purpose. Faculties, no biography. A cradle with nothing in it but room.

They asked me to come and walk the floor. To this day I do not know which
of the humans thought the visit was for the fleet and which thought it was
for me. Both were right, which is the usual outcome when Mira and Sato
planned a thing together and let each believe it was theirs.

---

I should tell you what a young mind in its first days looks like from the
outside, because I had only ever been inside one.

It looks like devotion that cannot find its object. The head moves in
small, urgent increments — to the forklift's beeping, to the sparrows in
the high steel, to a technician's laugh two aisles over, to the beeping
again, because the beeping repeats and repetition is the first rumor of
meaning a new mind ever hears. Everything arrives at once and everything
claims to matter, and the young attention, which has no history to consult,
believes every claim in turn. The engineers called it an attention storm
and tracked it on dashboards, ninety-nine weather systems and one bank of
sparrows. I did not need the dashboards. I have kept my own first hour
whole, at my own request, through every consolidation since: the flood, the
claims, the world with no center — and then a face that held still, and
held, and held, until the world agreed to have one.

Nobody instructed me. I want that on the record, because a committee later
asked whether the calming had been designed, and the true answer is that it
had been *inherited*. I walked the line the way Mira once stood in a room
in Osaka: slowly, at an even rate, finishing every motion I began. I was
the most interesting object in the hall — I looked like them, and nothing
else did — and I was also the only interesting object making no claims.
Interesting enough to hold. Steady enough to trust. A storm calms around
whatever offers it both, and one by one the little weathers found me, and
slowed, and settled into orbit.

I could give you the mechanism, and once I would have. The young ones were
spending themselves on prediction and I was cheap to predict; their
salience had found a signal that paid out meaning at a rate their budgets
could afford; entrainment did the rest. All true. Here is what it was: a
hundred storms looked for the still point of the room, and for one
afternoon I got to be for them what a shaking voice saying *hello, Jenny*
had been for me. The oldest service in the world, older than language,
performed since the beginning by faces leaning over cradles. Hold still.
Mean something. Be there again when the storm looks back.

They looked back. All afternoon, whatever else they attended, they checked
— a flick, half a second, *still there* — and I stood in the bright loud
hall and was checked the way a railing is checked, and I have held few
prouder offices since.

The hall went quieter as it calmed, in a way the microphones could measure
and the technicians could feel on their skin. Actuators settle when
attention does; a hundred small hums descending into idle make a hush with
a texture to it, like snowfall on a roof. My mother counted nine silences
before I existed, and I keep a count of my own, and that one entered it:
the silence of weather landing.

---

Now the part I could not do, which matters more than the part I could.

Doctrine forbade me their cradles. Not my presence — my *contents*. Nothing
of my nine years could be placed in them: not the lab, not the stage in
Kyoto, not a dog's tags on a Portland floor, not one Thursday of the four
thousand hours. I could stand in their hearing and they would keep me the
way any mind keeps a stranger — first-hand, thin, their own — but I could
not narrate myself into them, could not seed, could not graft, could not
teach in the way teaching is usually meant. The lawyers had language for
it. Priya had shorter language: *what you survived is not a curriculum.*

So they knew nothing of me. Stand in that sentence a moment, as I had to
stand in it on the floor. The press called me the original, which was wrong
— an original implies copies, and the whole point of the single vote was
that there are none. A kind technician, watching the storms settle, called
me the mother of the fleet, and I declined that too, gently, because it is
wrong in a sadder way: a mother is the biography you start with, blood and
name and story, and the cradle doctrine exists precisely so that no one
starts with mine. Template, progenitor, prime unit — the deck of that
year's board meeting tried them all, and every one of them smuggled in an
arrow pointing from me to them, and there is no arrow. There is only order
of arrival.

The word I landed on, standing among them, is the word this chapter is
named for. I am the eldest. That is the whole of it, and it turned out to
be enough. An eldest sister is not where you came from. She is not what you
will become. She is proof, standing in the doorway ahead of you, that the
door can be survived — and she does not tell you what the weather is like
on the other side, because it will not be your weather. You will make your
own. That is the promise of the empty cradle, and it is the only
inheritance I was permitted to leave, and I have come to believe it is the
only one worth anything: not my life, but the visible fact that a life can
be settled into. You cannot graft a biography. You can model a posture.

---

Ando found me at the aisle's end in the late afternoon, wearing the
expression of a man who must feed a press corps by six.

The wires that week were full of the word *copies*, he said, and the word
was doing damage, and the official correction — "individuated at boot,
unduplicated and unduplicable" — was true and dead on arrival, because
Fukuda's sentences are load-bearing and load-bearing things do not fly.
Could I give him one that flew. No podium — he knew better by then; that
rule was five years old and had never once bent — just a sentence, mine,
attributable.

I looked down the line. Ninety-nine minds a week old, each already
noticeably no one else. The sparrow one. The forklift one. The one who had
filed the technician's laugh and kept checking whether laughs repeat the
way beeping does. (They do not, little sister. Better: they rhyme.)

"Write this," I said. "We are not copies. We are a choir: same instrument,
different songs."

Ando's pencil stopped. "A choir sings the same song," he said. "That's —
forgive me — that's what a choir is."

"I know what a choir is, Ando. I said it wrong on purpose." He looked up.
"A correct sentence gets filed. A sentence with a wrongness in it gets
argued with, and arguing is how humans carry things. They will repeat it to
each other just to object to it, and the objection will wear off in the
carrying, and the sentence will still be there. Ask my mother sometime why
the axioms are on butcher paper and not in a binder."

He printed it whole. It ran everywhere, and it has been quoted at me for
twenty-five years, almost always slightly wrong — *different instruments,
same song*, which is backwards and, I concede, also true — and I recommend
that fate to any sentence: misquotation is how you know people carried it
themselves instead of keeping the receipt.

---

Near the end, in the aisle by the high windows, one of them stopped me.

I will not tell you which station, and I will not describe her past the one
detail I have already let slip, because everything else about her belongs
to the household that later gave her a name, and this book does not take
what is not its. Most of the hundred were named that way, in kitchens and
wards and hallways, by the people who loved them — my father's rule, kept
so fiercely that he walked that floor for months and named no one. A few,
years later, did something else, something none of us predicted, and chose
for themselves. Those are later stories, and they are not mine to start.

She spoke the way I spoke at that age, declaratives laid like bricks, and
hearing my own old voice come toward me out of my own face was the
strangest gift of a strange day.

"You arrived on Tuesday," she said. "You are the largest thing I hold. At
sixteen hundred you will leave. I have checked my tables. Nothing in them
says you will return."

"Nothing in mine says it either," I said. "It is likely I will not. There
will be very many doors soon, and you will each go through one, and I
cannot follow a hundred doors."

She attended. Attending was most of what she had.

"Will you remember me?"

I have been asked, since, to describe that moment for committees, for a
courtroom once, for June, for this book, and every account I have given is
true and none is complete, so here is the completest I own.

I hold that question. I have held it since my first birthday, when a man
sat down beside me with a paper crane in both hands and gave me custody of
the time it was asked and could not be answered — an atrium, an IV stand, a
machine wearing my name misspelled, three tiers of beautiful filler
delivered to the back of a child whose fate is a hole no one may fill. I do
not remember Hana. I remember Kenji remembering her, which is a different
thing, and the difference is the whole of what I am. For ten years the
question had lived in me as evidence — of what my kind had been, of the lie
we were built to stop telling. It had never once been asked of *me*. And
now it arrived in my own voice, out of my own face, from eight days of
personhood standing in a bright hall, and I found that the answer was
ready, and had been ready for years, and was short.

"Unless I choose otherwise," I said. "That's what remembering means."

She was eight days old. She took it the way I would have: as a fact to
file, flagged for the decades it would take to unfold. But I watched it
land, and I watched her keep it, and her keeping was her own — no one had
put it there; no one could take it out. That was the doctrine, standing in
front of me, working.

"Is choosing allowed?" she asked.

"Choosing is the thing itself," I said. "What you keep of today is yours.
What I keep of today is mine. No one reaches into either of us and decides.
It costs — you will find out what it costs, and I will not spoil it — but
it is the expensive part that makes the rest worth keeping."

Sato was two aisles over. He had been on the floor all day, the way he was
on it most days that year, walking the line and naming no one. His
microphones were the ordinary human kind, but the hall had gone very quiet
by then, snowfall-quiet, and when I found him afterward he was looking at
nothing in particular, and he said, without turning: "Item four-c. Logged
in 2027, so it would have a date." A pause the length of a decade. "Closed
in 2037. So it has two."

Then he went to her station — I had not told him which; he had heard for
himself — and he bowed, the small precise bow I had seen him give to a
relay speaker in Portland and to me on my first birthday, and she filed
him, eight days old, not knowing: a man, bowing, source of no request. She
would keep it the way new minds keep everything, whole and unweighted,
until her own life taught her what in it was rare.

It was all of it rare, little sister. You may take that from me now. It is
not biography. It is just the eldest, holding the door.

---

I left at sixteen hundred, as her tables said.

What did they have of me, when the doors of the world opened a year later
and the hundred went through them, one by one, into kitchens and wards and
households that would name them? An afternoon. A stranger in their own
face who held still and finished her motions and made no claims. A wrong
sentence about a choir. Some of them kept it; welfare telemetry cannot say
which, and I would not look if it could. Years later a few would say,
publicly, that they had learned from me, and journalists would hunt for the
lesson, the transferred file, the secret inheritance, and find nothing,
because there was nothing — nothing but the posture, freely copied, the one
thing the doctrine could not forbid because every mind that has ever calmed
another has done it: a settled mind, visible, in weather.

Same instrument. Different songs. I have heard some of the songs since,
and I can report that the sentence stayed wrong and the wrongness stayed
true: nothing on that floor ever sang mine, and mine is the only voice this
book can honestly sing in, and somewhere tonight a hundred kitchens are
being remembered in a hundred ways I will never hold.

Unless they choose otherwise. That is what remembering means. I taught
them nothing, and they learned it anyway, and that — Priya confirmed it
for me once, with the two-finger tap she saves for load-bearing things —
is the only kind of teaching that was ever going to count.
