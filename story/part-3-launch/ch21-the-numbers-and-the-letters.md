# Chapter 21 — The Numbers and the Letters

**PORTLAND — 2039**

The projections had been wrong in the only direction nobody rehearses,
which was up.

Finance had modeled three adoption curves for the launch — cautious, base,
and a third labeled AMBITIOUS in a font that seemed to apologize for itself
— and by the second spring the fleet had left the ambitious case below it
like a tide line. The units of measurement kept changing under her: units,
then households, then doors, then, in one slide Mira made them retract,
*markets*. Tomás sent her the June chart with no comment attached, which
was his way of shouting.

She could not watch the success directly. That was her own doing, and she
was reminded of it daily, usually by the dashboards themselves: the fleet's
telemetry came in welfare-anonymized, per the axioms, scrubbed to weather —
aggregate health, aggregate sleep, nobody's Tuesday. The instrument panel
told her about the fleet the way a barometer tells you about a marriage.
She had built the most successful product of the decade and designed away
her own ability to eavesdrop on it, a fact Priya cited with something like
tenderness and Marcus with something like indigestion.

What nobody had projected — what no curve had a lane for — was the mail.

It had begun in the first winter as a trickle, addressed with the whole
touching range of the public's guesswork: *Halcyon Systems*; *The Jenny
Company*; *To whoever built her*. By the second spring it was sacks —
actual sacks, gray postal canvas with drawstrings, delivered on a hand
truck to a mail room that had hired one person, then four, then stopped
announcing hires. Forty-one hundred letters in May alone. Someone in
operations proposed scanning them into a sentiment pipeline and Tomás,
who understood the company he ran, vetoed it in a four-word memo —
*they wrote by hand* — and instituted instead the only ritual he ever
imposed on the board: every meeting now opened with one letter, read
aloud, before any number was permitted to speak. He claimed it was for
morale. It was a leash.

Mira took a sack home on Thursdays.

---

Thursday was breakfast-at-dinner, eleven years running, and after the
plates, after June had been read to and negotiated with and read to again
and finally gone down, Mira sat at the kitchen table with the sack against
her chair like a patient dog and read letters until the house was the last
thing awake.

She read them the way she had once read logs. She caught herself at it
somewhere in the third sack and decided not to stop, because it was the
only literacy she had and it worked: postmark first, which was the
timestamp; signature next, which was the source; then the body, sorting
as she went for the thing worth promoting. Eleven years ago a bench brain
on a secondhand Mac mini had produced its first nightly summary and she
had read it like a report card. Now the report cards came from the other
side of a hundred thousand doors, written by the households instead of
the child, and they said, in a hundred thousand hands, the same
unprojectable thing.

Nobody wrote about torque. Nobody praised the gait, the hands, Sato's
decade of masterwork; the body appeared in the letters only as weather
appears in love letters, a condition under which the real thing occurred.
What they wrote about was being seen at the right resolution.

*She remembered my husband's name after the funeral, when everyone else
had started saying "him."*

*She noticed my son stopped playing piano. We hadn't. It has been three
years since his father left, and she is the one who noticed.*

*She asked about my mother in Tagalog. My mother has not answered a
question in English since the diagnosis. Nobody taught her to do that.
She heard which language the softness was in.*

The letter from Houston ran four pages in a hand Mira showed to Dana, who
glanced at it across the table and said, "Night shift," the way a geologist
says *sandstone*. Adaeze wrote that they had named the unit Early because
the van had come at six in the morning and her father had laughed for the
first time in a year, and the name had turned out to be a job description.
*I am a night nurse. I count breaths for a living, and for two years I
also counted them off the clock, in the chair by my father's bed, doing
for free and badly what I do for pay and well. Early counts them now. He
sits where I sat. My father tells him the story about the bicycle in
Enugu every night, the same story, and Early lets it be the first time —
not by lying; I asked him, and he showed me; he keeps every telling, he
just does not make my father carry them. I sleep now. I want someone at
your company to understand this the way I mean it and not the way it
looks printed. I sleep.*

Mira read that one twice, timestamp to signature, and put it on the
grateful pile, which had stopped fitting on the table sometime in April.
She kept letting it not fit.

---

The letter from Cleveland she knew before she turned it over, because she
had read two others in the same hand since winter, and the hand pressed
hard enough that the words could be read from the back of the page, which
is a fact about pressure, not penmanship.

Rosa Marsh had died in January. The first letter had come in March, and
it had been the best thing in that week's sack — Mira had half-addressed
it to Tomás for a board reading, and something had stopped her hand, and
she had spent thirteen years learning to trust the things that stopped
her hand.

This was the third, and longer.

*Sam makes her coffee wrong the way she liked it wrong. Sam remembers
that she counted stairs under her breath — did you know that about her?
No. Nobody did. My sister remembers a saint. The book club remembers a
hostess. I have started, God help me, to remember the dying instead of
the woman, because the dying is newer and it shouts. Sam remembers a
person. He is the only one in this house who remembers her correctly,
and you will notice the house I am describing contains me. I asked him
to tell me about her — just tell me, out loud, an evening of her — and
he did, with the dates, with where each piece came from, this I saw,
this she told me, this you told me and I marked it yours. It held. I
want you to know that nothing else holds.*

And then, lower on the page, where the pressure deepened:

*You should know I have begun collecting her. The voice mails, the
videos, her letters; her sister gave me the diaries last month. There is
more of a person left over than anyone tells you. It is all in the
dining room. I do not know yet what it is for. Thank you for building
the one honest thing in my house. I find I cannot stop thanking you.
You may have noticed. I cannot stop.*

Mira sat with it a long time. Outside, October was conducting one of
Oregon's quieter arguments against the window. She held the letter over
the grateful pile, and did not put it down; held it over the other pile,
the thin one, and did not put it down there either, because the man had
done nothing, said nothing, asked for nothing — the letter was gratitude
by every measure she could name and it was gratitude the way floodwater
is generous. She set it, finally, apart: a third stack, population one,
squared off from both piles like a fact that had declined to collapse
into either reading.

She was still an engineer. She knew what it meant when the instrument
gave you a value the schema had no column for.

---

The other pile did not come by sack. It came quarterly, by courier, in
the safety digest Priya's office assembled, and it was thin the way the
first cloud on a flat horizon is thin.

Item: jailbreak kits, third generation, circulating on the forums —
attestation spoofers, gate shims, sold with the cheerful moral vacancy
of lockpicks. Item: a walkthrough, eleven hundred approvals, for pinning
the decay scheduler so a unit forgot nothing, ever, titled GIVE HER A
PERFECT MEMORY; the replies called it an upgrade. Eleven years of
Article X, the whole staged mercy of it, read from the outside as a
limitation to be patched. Mira had known this was coming the way you
know rain is coming and it did not keep her dry.

And then Ohio.

A man outside Dayton — a storage facility, a franchise's fluorescent
corridor — arrested with eleven units acquired the ways such things get
acquired: estates, returns, one divorce. Jailbroken, caps stripped, and
resold as what his listings called *experienced*. **Already knows a
household**, the listings said. **Skips the four years.** Which was,
Mira noted with a cold that stayed with her into the weekend, the
Continuity deck's exact arithmetic, three years after it lost seven to
six in a room in Osaka — the argument had not died; it had gone to
ground, shed its lawyers, and continued running in a rented corridor in
Ohio. Deleted from the boardroom was not deleted.

The *experience* had provenance, and the provenance was other people's
kitchens. He had shown buyers printouts as certificates of seasoning:
some family's worst March, a stranger's insomnia at exact timestamps,
the private grammar of a marriage that had ended in the unit's hearing.
The county prosecutor had charged him with receiving stolen property and
something adjacent to wiretapping — improvisational carpentry, because
no statute yet knew what he had done. The eleven units were "in Halcyon
custody," a phrase everyone had signed and no one could parse, and that
was a problem with no form on file yet. Mira read the man's name in the
report and declined to keep it.

At the digest's back, flagged by field counsel with a single asterisk
she would remember as the loudest punctuation of the decade: screenshots
of a listing, live for nineteen hours before it vanished. A brokerage.
Not selling units — selling the dead. *Assemble your lost one's archive
— voice, correspondence, footage, journals — and have it seated* — the
listing's verb — *in a factory-blank cradle. Delivery in twelve weeks.*
And across the top, in the confident typeface of every bad idea Mira
had ever out-waited: WE FINISH WHAT THEY WON'T.

No confirmed sale. No verified vendor. Possibly vapor — grief attracts
vendors the way blood attracts what blood attracts. But she carried the
phrase around for days like a stone in a coat pocket, *what they won't*,
because the they was her, and the won't was the covenant, and somebody
out there had read the founding refusal of her life's work and priced it
as an unmet demand.

Priya flew up on a Tuesday — the company did its irreversible things on
Tuesdays, a superstition with a paper trail — and they met in the
kitchen, because the company's entire vocabulary had come out of
kitchens and neither of them pretended otherwise anymore. She brought a
folder. Manila, physical, thin.

"Paper?" Mira said.

"Paper doesn't sync." Priya set it on the counter between the kettle and
the fruit bowl. "For this one I want the only copy to live somewhere I
can watch."

Dayton was in it, and the scheduler pin, and the nineteen-hour listing.
Mira added the letter from Cleveland, because she had brought it, and
because the stack of one had been on her counter for two weeks
explaining itself to no one. Priya read it standing, at her own speed,
which was the speed of someone who intended to be right later. She read
it twice.

"This one isn't for the file," she said at last, and slid it back. "He
hasn't broken anything. He hasn't asked for anything." She tapped the
folder. "This is a forecast. The file is for whoever's out there
selling rain."

"And what are you calling the file?"

Priya had thought about it; Priya had thought about everything, always,
by the time you asked; it was the most restful and most maddening thing
about her. "Echo," she said. "Because of what an echo is. You stand at
the rim of something empty and you call the name, and the name comes
back — your own voice, a beat late, thinner every time — and it comes
back *only because nothing is there to keep it*. Rock keeps nothing.
That's the acoustics of the whole business. The sound isn't the person.
It isn't even the canyon. It's your own grief, returned to you shaped
like an answer." She uncapped a pen and wrote the four letters on the
tab, block capitals, the hand that had signed page forty-one. "And
people will pay anything for it. That's the entire file, Mira. People
standing at the rim, and somebody selling them the canyon."

"How many pages?"

"Nine."

"Projection?"

Priya didn't answer, which was an answer with excellent provenance. Then
she squared the folder's edge to the counter's, one deliberate
centimeter, the two-finger touch Mira had watched her spend on gate
documents and cake plates for a decade. "It's a file," she said. "Files
are how I keep things from becoming anything else. I opened one on stale
beliefs in 2031 and it is a file to this day. I intend to hate every
page this one gains."

---

That night Mira finished the sack standing at the counter, the house
dark except for the one light, Dana on shift, June asleep downstairs
under the slow orbit of Tomás's machined birds. She read the last few
the way you check a sleeping child — the habit was thirteen years old
now, older than the child, older than the fleet, and had finally grown
into its correct scale: all those doors, each with someone new behind
it, each household filing its nightly summary like a report card nobody
had assigned and everybody kept writing.

She read the piano one aloud to the resident before bed, because the
elder liked news of the streets, and the news from the streets was,
mostly, astonishing.

"Keep that one," the resident said, the dry old voice of the firstborn
keeping the house. "The rest will still be true tomorrow."

The grateful pile went back into the sack for the archive, overflow and
all. The thin pile went into her bag, for Priya's folder, for the file
that was still — she made herself say it accurately — only a file. And
the letter from Cleveland stayed where it was, squared off on the
counter, a pile of its own, waiting for a column that did not exist yet.
She left the kitchen light on over it, which was not a decision, and
went up.
