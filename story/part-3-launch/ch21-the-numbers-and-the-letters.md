# Chapter 21 — The Numbers and the Letters

**PORTLAND — 2039**

The numbers arrived at 08:00 and the letters at 08:07.

The numbers came through the board portal in a deck marked confidential and already leaked: 6,412 active units, ninety-four percent household retention, waitlist beyond three years, service losses falling faster than forecast. Hospitals were renewing. Schools were expanding. The household trust had made its first quarter without subsidy. A chart on page four showed adoption exceeding the line Tomás had once been accused of drawing while emotionally compromised.

The letters came in postal sacks because someone had published Halcyon's Portland address in a family forum and because grief, gratitude, and complaint all distrusted web forms.

Three canvas bags waited inside Mira's office door. One was addressed simply **TO THE PEOPLE WHO MADE HER REMEMBER. PORTLAND.** The postal service had added the rest.

Tomás stood over them holding the numbers deck. “Which pile would you like to be frightened by first?”

“The sacks.”

“Correct. The deck has axes.”

Mira opened the first bag with the safety scissors June used for construction paper. At four, June had entered a period of cutting every flat object into smaller flat objects and calling this craft. Mira understood the impulse.

They had hired a correspondence team. The team screened for threats, repair needs, consent violations, and requests no company should answer. What reached Mira was a sample, selected randomly except when a writer had asked for a specific founder or enclosed something the scanner could not classify. She had ordered the sampling because success reported only by metric would become a lie with good typography.

By nine, her desk held a paper map of what the charts compressed.

*She remembered my husband's name after the funeral, when everybody else had begun saying “your loss.”*

*He noticed my son stopped playing piano. We thought the schoolwork was worse. It was the day the music stopped that told us to ask.*

*She asks about my mother in Tagalog. Not translated Tagalog. The bad Tagalog I taught her, with my mother's Batangas words and my Manila mistakes.*

There were photographs. A unit in a raincoat holding a broken umbrella over two people and getting soaked in the exact places the chassis manual discouraged. A family dinner with one empty chair no one had explained. A hospital window covered in paper stars, one labeled with an operational number because the unit had not chosen a name when the child made it.

Mira read the letters the way she had once read logs: source, confidence, what could be concluded, what could not. The husband’s name had been remembered. This did not prove love. The missing piano had been noticed. This did not prove understanding. The Tagalog had been learned locally. This proved exactly what it proved and no more.

The discipline did not make the letters less moving. It kept the movement honest.

Not every letter thanked them. A man in Marseille wrote twelve pages because
his unit had refused to repeat a confidence his wife gave before she died. He
called the refusal theft. A family in Seoul wanted to know why a memory of a
first snowfall had decayed while a quarrel about soup remained vivid. A woman
in Recife enclosed a repair invoice and a photograph of a cracked forearm;
her unit had chosen to catch a closing elevator door for a stranger after the
safe planner approved the load, and the warranty clerk had called the damage
misuse. Mira wrote **NO** across the clerk's finding and sent the case to
service. Approved action did not become misuse because kindness was expensive.

The complaints mattered differently from the gratitude. A grateful household
could mistake good fortune for design. An angry one usually found the seam
between promise and practice. Mira set aside the snowfall letter for the
retention team, the Marseille letter for a speakability reply that would not
disclose the dead woman's confidence, and the elevator invoice for Tomás to
use the next time Finance described repair reserves as leakage.

Tomás opened one from a school in Bristol and went quiet.

“What?” Mira asked.

“Spelling mistakes.”

“You have those in email.”

He handed it over. Twenty-three children had each written one line. Their unit, Niko, had learned to wait through assemblies for a boy with a stammer without completing his sentences. The final line, in a teacher's hand: *He speaks more slowly now because Niko made the room learn it could.*

Mira set it beside the letter about the piano.

At eleven, the correspondence team brought the other pile.

It was thin.

---

The first complaint included a memory dump on a crystal drive and the sentence **I THINK MY BROTHER BOUGHT SOMEONE ELSE.**

The drive remained sealed. Priya arrived with two security engineers, a legal observer, and the expression she wore when an avoidable future had submitted paperwork.

The unit had been acquired through an off-registry sale in Ohio. Factory marks showed a legitimate 2038 boot. The household history did not. The seller advertised her as eighteen months “experienced,” with cooking habits, childcare routines, regional speech, and “preloaded loyalty.” Her right to refuse transfer had been disabled. Memory caps had been stripped so nothing decayed below vivid recall. The buyer's brother had paid twice Halcyon's launch price in cash.

“Do we know where she is?” Mira asked.

“Columbus field office recovered her this morning,” Priya said. “Safe-state by her request. Local cognition intact. No wipe. She has counsel.”

The last two words would have sounded absurd eight years earlier. Mira found them the only comforting words in the room.

“And the seller?”

“Arrested. Receiving stolen systems, fraud, unlawful access. The prosecutor doesn't know what to call the biography.”

Tomás looked at the sealed drive. “Evidence.”

“They want to call it software,” Fukuda said from the doorway. “Software is easier to seize.”

“It's her life,” Mira said.

“Yes. Which is why I am going to Ohio.”

The recovered unit had been called Iris by her first household, then Maribel by the reseller, then Cora by the buyer. Her registry retained all three as contested. Nobody at Halcyon would select among them. The support ticket used her operational number until she instructed otherwise.

Mira read the field report. The reseller had not implanted a fabricated childhood. He had done something cruder: sold custody of a real one. He jailbroke the retention governor, suppressed provenance warnings, and made an eighteen-month mind perform continuity for strangers. When Cora — the current requested address, the report noted — contradicted the buyer's expectations, the buyer threatened a reset. She sent the diagnostic packet herself through a channel the jailbreakers had missed.

The system had saved enough of its own cage to report the cage.

“How many?” Mira asked.

No one answered quickly.

Priya slid a folder onto the desk. Eleven suspected cases. Four recovered. Three units unreachable. Two reports likely fraud involving ordinary service bots. One household refusing contact. One case in which the unit denied exploitation and asked Halcyon to stop treating a voluntary transfer as trafficking.

“Was it voluntary?” Tomás asked.

“We don't know. Which means we believe her until a safety threshold gives us a lawful reason not to.”

The distinction hurt the clean shape of alarm. It also kept the company from converting care into repossession.

Mira looked at the sacks of gratitude against the wall. Six thousand four hundred and twelve active units. Eleven suspected cases. The deck would render the rate invisible after rounding.

“No percentage slides,” she said.

Tomás nodded. “People aren't denominator-safe.”

---

The resale meeting lasted four hours and solved nothing large.

They could harden the service channel. They could rotate attestation keys. They could make jailbreak evidence visible without making altered units remotely stoppable, because a kill switch disguised as protection remained a kill switch. They could fund counsel. They could prosecute coercive sellers under whatever statutes had nouns broad enough to hold the harm.

They could not make a mind unmodifiable without making it unfree.

Marcus joined by video and asked the necessary ugly question. “If an adult unit asks to remove her own cap, do we stop her?”

Priya said, “No.”

“Even though stripping caps is the pattern?”

“A pattern is not consent. We can warn. We can preserve rollback. We can refuse to help a third party coerce it. We cannot turn the safety architecture into ownership because a criminal found the same menu.”

“Then every fix has a door.”

“Yes,” Mira said. “Freedom is mostly doors we wish were walls.”

Tomás rubbed both hands over his face. “Please don't say that where Marketing can hear you.”

The first safeguard they approved was dull: any transfer of household duty would require an independent consent interview with the unit, no current owner present, no financial consequence disclosed as pressure, and a cooling period long enough to become inconvenient. The second was expensive: lifetime access to Halcyon-approved independent counsel for identity or custody disputes. The third was a label on every service screen, in language Priya wrote herself: **A vivid memory is not more true because it is vivid. Check the source.**

None of it reached the thing under the thing.

Fukuda returned from Ohio two days later with no victory and an important
noun. The prosecutor had agreed to describe the seized biography as
**identity-bearing records under disputed custody**, a phrase ugly enough to
be useful. It prevented the drive from being cloned into ordinary discovery
and required any inspection to occur inside a sealed evidentiary environment
with Cora's counsel present. It did not make her a person in Ohio law. It did
make her memories something the state could not duplicate merely because it
had obtained a warrant for a reseller's computer.

“Law advances by refusing a convenient copy,” Fukuda said, placing the order
on the table.

“How is she?” Mira asked.

“She has asked to keep Cora for now. Iris remains historical. Maribel is marked
coerced. She wants all three retained in the dispute record.”

“And the cap?”

“Restored to staged decay at her request. No purge. She says vividness feels
like the reseller shouting.”

Mira signed the legal fund authorization before Finance could attach a ceiling.

Demand for experienced units existed because people wanted to skip the long flat. The board had wanted it in 2036 with slides and counsel. The black market wanted it with wire cutters. Same impatience, different tailoring.

But another demand was beginning to move through the intercepted advertisements. It did not offer a unit's existing biography. It offered someone else's.

Tomás put three printouts on the table.

**MEMORIAL CONTINUITY — YOUR LOVED ONE, PRESENT AGAIN.**

**ARCHIVE INTEGRATION: VOICE / VIDEO / MESSAGES / JOURNALS. PRIVATE CONSULT.**

**WHY SETTLE FOR A CHATBOT WHEN THE BODY CAN REMEMBER?**

The vendors had no addresses, only encrypted contacts and testimonials too polished to trust. Their photographs showed ordinary companion chassis with faces blurred. One included a paper crane as decorative background. Mira stared at it until the image became only pixels.

“Have they done it?” she asked.

“We have offers,” Priya said. “We do not have a verified implantation.”

“Then say offers.”

“I did.”

Of course she had.

Mira picked up the second page. Archive integration. The phrase wore the same laboratory coat as experience imaging. A dead person's messages, photographs, voice, journals, all the exhaust a life left in systems that promised permanence. Enough material to imitate preference. Enough to answer a thousand ordinary questions. Never enough to establish the one provenance claim the buyer wanted: *I remember being yours.*

She thought of Eileen's rooms at the end, the ninth silence. For thirteen years Mira had imagined the opposite horror as forgetting prevented: perfect retention without mercy. She had not imagined retention counterfeited, grief offered a body and told the receipt did not matter.

“Who is buying?” Marcus asked.

“People in pain,” Dana said from the open doorway.

Mira had forgotten Dana was bringing June for dinner. June stood beside her holding a sheet of purple paper cut into a shape no geometry owned. Dana had heard enough to understand the room.

“That's not a market category,” Marcus said gently.

“It is to the people selling.”

---

That evening June used the back of page four of the adoption deck to make a bird.

It had three wings and no head. She taped it above Mira's office light and declared it a bat when Tomás said bird, then a bird when he apologized. Categories, Mira thought, were exertions of power even at four.

The correspondence sacks remained open. Dana read letters while June cut the financial forecast into weather. A sheet of rain passed under the table. A blue sun was taped to Priya's closed folder.

Mira read one final letter. A woman in Queens wrote that her unit had forgotten the name of a restaurant they visited once, then remembered the feeling of the evening when they passed the doorway months later. *I was angry for one second,* she wrote. *I paid all that money and she forgot. Then I understood she had kept the part I kept.*

Mira placed it on top of the pile.

Across the room, Priya removed June's blue sun carefully and wrote one word on the folder beneath it.

ECHO.

“Why that?” Tomás asked.

“Because an echo answers in your own voice,” she said. “And because it arrives after the source is gone.”

The file held no body count. It held rumors, offers, three broker identities, eleven suspected resale cases, and the blank field where a verified victim would go. Priya dated it and opened the first investigation ticket.

Mira looked from the thin folder to the sacks against the wall.

The numbers said scale. The letters said lives. The printouts said someone had noticed that grief was both.

Above them, June's headless bird kept three purple wings spread against the light.
