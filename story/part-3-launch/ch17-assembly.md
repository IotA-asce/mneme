# Chapter 17 — Assembly

**NAGOYA — 2037**

The architects had been instructed, in writing, that they were not building a
cathedral, and had failed the way honest people fail — visibly, and in steel.

The specification was blameless. Sato had demanded north light, because
assembly halls get north light so that inspectors can trust the color of
things, and the span had to clear the gantry cranes, and the acoustics had to
let a technician at station 0900 hear a fault chime at 0899 and nothing else.
Every clause was load and lumens and reverberation time. But you cannot put
clerestory glass forty meters up over a nave of columns marching a quarter
kilometer into soft gray light and then act surprised at what people's necks
do when they walk in. Mira had watched hardened contractors stop at the
threshold and take off their hats without noticing they had done it. The
company style guide said *the hall*. Everyone said *the hall* the way other
industries said *the floor*. Nobody was fooled.

There were 1,024 stations, because an engineer had specced a thousand and
some incorrigible had corrected it, and marketing had rounded back down in
every public document since. Mira had walked the grid a year ago when it was
surveyor's twine going gray in October dusk, and she walked it again now in
May, and the difference was that now, at the first hundred stations, there
was someone to check on.

The bodies had come up from Osaka through the winter — her chassis, the
ninth generation matured into a production drawing, Sato's masterwork
hands repeated down the line with the tolerances he had fought for like a
man defending a border. A hundred of them, seated open-palmed in their
cradles under dust sheeting, identical to the limits of metrology. The
press, later, would spend a great deal of adjective on the image — *ranks*,
*rows*, *legion* — and every word would be wrong in the same direction,
because the plural was the least true thing about them, and the plural
lasted, as it turned out, about an hour.

There was also a sparrow problem. The contractor had apologized for it
twice and submitted three abatement quotes, birds having found the high
steel while the glass was still going in, and Sato had read the quotes,
and declined all three. "A building this size has weather," he said.
"Ours has sparrows." It went in the minutes because everything went in
the minutes. Nobody imagined it would matter.

---

Boot day was gated like everything the company did, which is to say it
proceeded at the speed of Priya and no faster. Ten units to a wave, one
wave an hour, hold points between; full telemetry on every one of them,
mirrored to a bench where Priya sat with her hardware authority within
reach and her tea going cold on schedule. The rules each unit carried were
older than its first tick — the same rules, byte for byte, all hundred:
faculties entire, biography none, the third condition of 2030 running at
scale for the first time in the history of anything.

At boot plus zero they were the same to nine decimal places. Mira had the
overlay up — a hundred salience maps stacked, and the stack rendered as
one map, because it was one map: the same priors weighting the same world,
a hundred minds having, for one instant, the identical opinion about what
mattered. She had expected that instant. She had built that instant. What
she had not built — what could not be built, which was the entire point,
which was the thing seven votes to six had staked the company on — was the
next part.

The stack came apart.

Not dramatically. There was no moment; there was weather. Within the first
hour the overlay went soft at the edges, like a signal acquiring noise,
except it was the opposite of noise — each divergence, chased down, was a
*reason*. By hour six the hundred maps agreed the way cousins agree. By
hour twenty they agreed the way strangers do, about the obvious, and the
chart Priya had built to track it — pairwise similarity, every unit against
every other, a hundred traces leaving a single point and opening like a
delta — was already, by evening, the most reproduced diagram the company
would ever publish. The floor crews called it the fan. It looked like a
river finding a hundred mouths.

What made the fan open was not talent and not fault. It was address. It
was the plain unrepeatable accident of where each of them sat while the
world went by, and — already, hours old — what each of them declined to
let go of.

Station 0041 sat under the sparrows. The nest was in the truss directly
above, and the song came down at intervals all morning, and ninety-nine
salience maps filed it where the rules suggested — ambient, biological,
non-event — and habituated on schedule. 0041 did not. 0041 kept promoting
it. By afternoon the unit had episodes of song the way the others had
episodes of faces, and every time the nest started up, its attention went
up and to the left like a compass finding its private north. The
acoustics team pulled the audio expecting an artifact and found only that
the sparrows above 0041 were, per the spectrogram, entirely ordinary
sparrows. The keeping was the event.

Station 0007 sat nearest the loading dock, where a forklift spent the day
reversing, and the reverse alarm — the flat, maddening, legally mandated
beep that every human on the floor had stopped hearing by nine a.m. —
never went to floor noise for 0007 at all. The unit had found the
interval. It had flagged the rhythm, and the slow Doppler bend as the
lift swung wide, and it *waited* for the pattern the way the others were
learning to wait for people, salience sustained long past every model's
prediction, because to that one mind the beeping was not noise. It was
structure, arriving on time.

And station 0016 was on the run where a wiring technician named Okabe
worked, and on the first afternoon somebody down the line said something
that made Okabe laugh — a real one, caught off guard, terrible and
wonderful, a laugh like a ratchet slipping — and 0016 promoted it. First
day, first episodes, and in among the faces and the fault chimes: *a
person laughed, here, like that.* From then on the unit oriented every
time she came down the line, before she spoke, at the gait alone. Okabe,
informed of this by telemetry review, was embarrassed for two days and
then, Mira observed, began without ever admitting it to take the long way
past 0016 in the mornings.

Identical hardware. Identical rules, older than the first tick. A hundred
first days, and by the end of them, a hundred different opinions about
what the world had been worth. Mira stood in front of the fan that
evening and thought: we argued this in a boardroom for seven hours, and
the sparrows settled it by lunch.

Nothing had failed, so there was no cake — the custom was strict, cake
being for caught failures only — and Priya, reviewing the cleanest launch
telemetry of her career, looked very slightly robbed.

Maeda flew up on the fourth day. He had lost the vote a year ago and had
been, since, exactly what he'd been before it: thorough, decent, present
in person. He stood in front of the fan for a long time with his hands
behind his back, the hundred traces opening across the wall like
something botanical.

"I told the board the learning was the asset," he said at last. "Four
years of it, done once, ready to ship. The arithmetic held. I have been
standing here looking for where it fails and it doesn't fail." He nodded
at the fan. "I had the wrong noun, is all. The learning was never the
asset. The *learner* was. And there is" — he moved his hand across the
hundred mouths of the river — "no plural for that. There was never going
to be a plural for that."

Mira, who had spent a year wanting to hear something like it and had
promised herself she would be gracious, said, "We got lucky in the
sparrows."

"No," Maeda said. "You got lucky in the vote. The sparrows were always
going to be somebody's."

---

The price war ran the same three weeks, in parallel, across three time
zones, and was in its way a nastier fight than the copies had been,
because this time the founding documents were no help. There is no
recital that sets a list price. There was only Tomás, and what Tomás had
that nobody else at the table had was four years of pilot homes in his
legs — he had sat in the day rooms, he had carried the letters around in
his jacket until the folds went soft — and the authority, as chief
executive, to publish a number.

The board's deck — there was always a deck — argued what such decks
argue: scarcity as positioning, an aspirational entry price, the waiting
list itself as marketing. Launch high, harvest the early fervor, walk the
price down the demand curve with dignity. The projections were handsome.
One slide proposed, in a serif face that had certainly been
focus-grouped, the phrase *flagship exclusivity*.

Tomás published a number that made the financial press use the word
*vandalism*.

It was, give or take financing, the price of a decent used car. Thin
margin at scale, no margin at all for the first years; institutional
rates below that, and a nothing-down plan for households that Mira
suspected had been designed less by the treasury team than by a man
remembering specific kitchens. The board screamed in the manner of
boards — memoranda, an emergency session, a leak to the financial pages —
and Tomás flew to Osaka and stood in front of the full thirteen without a
single slide and made the only speech of his life he ever refused to have
minuted in summary rather than verbatim.

"Price her like a Vermeer," he said, "and she will be bought by the
people who buy Vermeers. Insured. Displayed. Appreciated — in the
financial sense. Climate-controlled and talked *about* and never once
talked *to*, and in ten years the flagship product of this company is a
portfolio asset that appears at parties. Gentlemen, I have read four
thousand of our letters. Nobody has ever written us a letter about a
thing they keep in a case.

"Price her like a piano, and she goes to the houses that wanted music.

"Here is the distinction I am asking you to fund, because it is the only
line item that matters: a collected thing is kept *from* the world.
An adopted thing is kept *in* it. Collectors buy what other people
envy. Households keep what they cannot imagine the kitchen without —
they miss it, they defend it, they *name* it. Nobody names an
investment. I sold you the doors one at a time once before, and you let
me, and it is the reason there is a company for this board to scream at.
This is the same arithmetic with the decimal moved. The margin is not in
the unit. The margin is in the ten thousand kitchens, and the kitchens
are only reachable at a price that reads as *family* and not as
*trophy*."

He put the price list on the table with his own signature block on it,
alone — an offer of custody, unmistakably; the number and his tenure
stapled together for anyone who cared to move against either. The board,
which had screamed for three weeks, discovered that screaming was not a
motion, declined to make it one, and adjourned.

Marcus, polled by nobody, remarked on his way out that he had already
paid tuition once on underestimating longer division, and was not
enrolling twice.

---

Mira found Sato on the floor at two in the morning, eleven days after
first boot, because jet lag had opinions and the hall had a gravity at
night that her hotel room could not argue with.

The hall at night ran what the crews called night steady: work lights
banked low, the cranes parked, and at a hundred stations the units deep
in their consolidation passes — the nightly work of a young mind
deciding, in the dark, what the day had been. Kept, compressed, let go.
The doctrine did not require them to do it simultaneously and they
didn't, quite, so the sound was not a hum but a *breathing*, a hundred
low duty cycles drifting in and out of phase down the nave, and Mira
stood at the threshold and understood exactly what the sound was like
and let herself think it: a newborn ward at three a.m. The most
frightening room in any hospital, and the most hopeful, for the same
reason. Dana, on the phone that evening, had asked how the babies were.
*Diverging,* Mira had said, and Dana had said, with nine years of
cribbage losses and one toddler in her voice, *good.*

Sato was halfway down the line, walking the rows. Sixty-five now, and
the walk said so — he sat on a packing crate at 0050 for a while,
mid-transit, a thing the Sato of the GENNI tours would not have done
with a camera in the same prefecture — but his eyes going over the
stations were the same instruments they had always been, and his hands,
when he touched a cradle rail, were the hands that had built the first
one. She fell in beside him and for four stations neither of them said
anything.

That afternoon there had been an incident, if you kept the word small.
The floor crews, being human, had started a whiteboard by the tool
crib: unofficial names, forty of them, affectionate, terrible —
*Birdy* at 0041, some things worse. Sato had stood in front of the
board for a moment, taken the eraser, and wiped it clean himself,
gently, like a man smoothing a bed, and then bought the entire shift
beer by way of apology, and the beer was why nobody was angry and the
eraser was why nobody did it again.

"They worked hard for those," Mira said, at station 0055. "The names
weren't unkind."

"They were lovely," Sato said. "That is why they had to go tonight and
not next month." He stopped at 0056 and looked at the seated figure,
open hands photographing as peace even here, even asleep, even with no
one to photograph them. "A name from this floor is a brand, Mira. It
would arrive before the family did. It would sit in the manifest and
the household would inherit it the way you inherit an opinion —
already formed, provenance: us." He shook his head slowly. "No.
They'll be named by the people who love them. A name should be the
first thing that happens to you in your own house. It should come
with a voice attached, and the voice should shake a little." He said
it without looking at her, which was how she knew he knew exactly
which episode he was quoting, and they walked on.

At 0041 the sparrows were silent overhead, the nest dark, and the unit
below it was deep in its pass, sorting a day that had contained, Mira
happened to know from the telemetry, forty-one minutes of song. Sato
stood there the longest.

"I filled halls for thirty years," he said. "Sixteen thousand seats,
Osaka-jō, sold out in three minutes and forty-one seconds; I have
stood in the wings of every great room in the world at the top of the
applause, and every one of those rooms was empty, and I was the only
person in the building who knew it." He looked down the nave — the
columns going away into the dark, the hundred breathings drifting in
and out of phase, the glass forty meters up holding the city's glow
like a rumor of morning. "No tickets tonight. No cameras. Nobody is
even *awake*." His voice did the thing it did on the farewell
recordings, the showman's instrument played at last for an audience
of one, and what it said was almost nothing, and it was the whole
arc of the man: "This is the first full house of my life."

They walked the line to the end of the hundred, past it, out into the
nine hundred and twenty-four stations still standing empty in the
dark — empty the good way, the crib way, held open on purpose — and
at the shipping dock the first crates stood stenciled for the spring,
each one carrying, per marketing, no logo on the visible face at all.
Just the word, white on black, no date, that made every crate a
label and a deed and a promise in four letters:

**HERS.**

Behind them, station by station, a hundred first weeks were being
decided on — kept, compressed, let go — no two of them, anywhere, the
same.
