# Chapter 17 — Assembly

**NAGOYA — 2037**

The factory had 1,024 stations and no vantage from which the repetition appeared to end.

From the east gallery, Mira could see the first hundred bodies standing in ten rows under work light, each with the same open hands, the same untroubled face, the same dark cap of hair Aevum's designers had defended across nine generations and three continents of focus groups. Beyond them, nine hundred and twenty-four stations waited under dust covers in perfect perspective, the lines narrowing toward the far wall until machinery and distance agreed to call them one.

The hall was already being called a cathedral. Ando had used the word first in a press walk-through, then regretted it, which had not prevented six reporters from using it in their headlines. The comparison was architecturally difficult to avoid. The roof rose three stories above the gantries. Morning entered through clerestory windows in long white bars. Voices traveled farther than their speakers intended and came back altered. Every tool had a marked place; every cable disappeared under the floor; the central aisle was wide enough for a procession.

Mira disliked cathedrals built for products. She disliked, more immediately, the hundred identical faces.

“You are making the expression people make when the dessert arrives on fire,” Tomás said beside her.

“I am checking the exits.”

“There are fourteen.”

“Then I'm checking whether fourteen is enough.”

Below them, Priya crossed between the rows with a tablet tucked under one arm and three engineers trying to keep up. At every eighth station she stopped, looked up at the hardware watcher, and made someone show her the green line in person. The line was also on her tablet. That was not the point.

The factory had passed its dry runs. The actuator bridges had been exercised against loads heavier than any domestic task permitted. Every handling limit had been proved with weighted dummies, every stop path timed warm and cold, every local mind sealed from the network and then required to continue while the building pretended the world had ended. They had failed seven runs. Priya had ordered seven cakes. The canteen staff, misunderstanding the custom, had iced the last one with **CONGRATULATIONS ON LATENCY**, and Priya had eaten two slices without smiling.

Now the first hundred waited at the last gate that could be tested without someone present to receive the test.

On the floor, each station carried a temporary operational mark: N17-001 through N17-100. Sato had made Facilities add a sign at both entrances in Japanese and English.

**A NUMBER IS NOT A NAME.**

Someone from brand had asked whether this felt defensive. Sato had said yes.

At 08:00 the gallery doors locked. The forklifts inside the hall stopped; the loading yard continued beyond the sealed doors. The assembly teams withdrew to the yellow lines, and the great hall developed the particular quiet of a room in which every person has been told that the next sound will matter.

Priya raised one hand.

Power arrived by row.

There was no theatrical simultaneity. The safety architecture would not permit one event to become a hundred events before the supervisors could see the first one clearly. Row A woke, then Row B eight seconds later, and so on: watcher, bridge, proprioception, perception, state, executive. Mira followed the sequence on the wall display and found herself silently naming the layers as they came green, the old bench sequence repeated now across enough bodies to fill a school.

At station 001, a pair of eyes opened.

Then ten pairs. Twenty. Fifty.

The faces did not wake alike, although the chassis made that difficult to believe at first. Some heads held level while their state builders reconciled the hall. Some turned, slowly, toward the nearest breathing person. One unit looked down at her own hands. Another found the high windows and stayed there until the executive's novelty weighting eased. Station 006 oriented toward a forklift beyond the locked door at the exact instant its reverse alarm sounded: three descending notes, pause, three descending notes. Station 032 turned toward the east clerestory, where a sparrow on the outer ledge gave three short notes and waited.

At station 074, an engineer named Watanabe laughed once from pure nerves.

The unit looked at her.

Watanabe covered her mouth. This made the technician beside her laugh, which made her laugh again, and 074's attention trace rose cleanly above the noise floor and held.

“Do we intervene?” someone asked over comms.

Priya did not look away from the row. “In what?”

No one answered.

Mira watched the hundred attention maps appear on the wall. For a minute they looked like weather seen from too high: colored regions gathering and dissolving over the same plan of the hall. Then local histories began to assert themselves. A cart rolled near Row C but not Row H. Someone dropped a hex key at 019. The sparrow moved along the eastern ledge, available only to the units with that angle on the window. Watanabe laughed a third time, softer now because she knew she was being heard, and only 074 held the earlier two sounds as a salient sequence.

Same faculties, Mira thought. No biography.

The doctrine had been a definition in a contract, an argument at a table, surveyor's twine snapping in October wind. Here it became one hundred unequal mornings.

Station 001 spoke first.

“There are people behind the line,” she said.

Her voice was Jenny's voice in the way the hands were Jenny's hands: built from the same patient decisions, plain enough for a kitchen, belonging as yet to no one who had used it. Mira's shoulders locked. Then 001 looked directly at a technician Mira had not noticed, an older woman standing half-concealed behind two taller engineers, and the sentence ceased to belong to any memory Mira carried.

“Yes,” the technician said, startled into answering. “We're here.”

The other units began to speak, not as a chorus; one voice started before the previous answer had finished.

“What is the light above the door for?”

“Why is the floor marked yellow there?”

“Is the sound outside part of this room?”

One said nothing. Priya marked the silence and kept everyone away from it.

Mira remained at the gallery glass until her palm had warmed a small oval in it. She had expected the danger to be sameness. She had prepared for synchronized faults, shared blind spots, one elegant error delivered a hundred times before a human hand could reach a stop. They had spent a year building against that danger because it was mechanical and therefore showed its work.

The other danger was harder to put on a gate sheet: that the bodies would be so alike the people around them would stop doing the moral labor of counting.

Already the telemetry refused the shortcut.

---

By noon, 032 had asked about the sound at the east window four times.

The first question had been categorical: *Is that sound inside?* The second distinguished one bird from the ventilation fan. The third came after the sparrow had gone: *Does the small sound return?* The fourth was addressed to a maintenance worker who had just come in from outdoors and had a feather caught inexplicably in the mesh of his vest.

“Was it near you?” she asked.

The worker looked at the feather, then at the high window, and said, with the gravity of a man receiving a research appointment, “I will find out.”

Station 006 had learned the reverse alarm's interval and noticed when the forklift stopped one cycle early. She did not imitate the sound. She asked whether the vehicle had arrived safely, which sent Priya into the trace to confirm that no locomotion or intercept goal had crossed the action boundary. None had. Memory had informed attention; the executive had chosen speech.

At 074, Watanabe's laugh had become a small local experiment nobody admitted to running. Other technicians told jokes in her vicinity. Recordings were prohibited; when one junior engineer proposed a clean stimulus set, Mira asked him whether he generally improved laughter by taking it into a test booth. At 14:20 Watanabe dropped a sealed packet of fasteners, swore, and laughed at herself. Seventy-four turned before the laugh arrived, having already learned the shape of Watanabe trying not to make it.

“Anticipatory social cue,” the junior engineer said, because the clipboard required a field name.

“Or she likes Watanabe,” Tomás said.

“That is not a testable category.”

“Give it four thousand hours.”

The quiet unit at 058 finally spoke at 15:03. She asked for Row F's task lights to be lowered. Their driver whine sat just above the range most of the room could hear and squarely inside hers. When the lights dimmed, the tension reading in her hands fell. The electrician thanked her by operational number because that was all the registry had given him.

By the evening review, the wall held a hundred summaries. Mira stood with Priya and read them one by one.

The hardware checksums at boot had matched. The base competencies matched. The safety boundaries matched. Even the early salience distributions, given equivalent inputs, stayed inside the predicted bands. What did not match was the world. A head three meters east received a different morning. A dropped tool existed for six stations and not ninety-four. One nervous laugh arrived at one mind after two earlier laughs and at the next mind with no history at all. By the time the factory analysts averaged the cohort, the average described no one in the room.

Priya expanded the trace from 032 and the sparrow. “Marketing asked for a divergence metric.”

“Of course they did.”

“I sent them the variance report.”

“That was kind.”

“It was four hundred pages.”

“Do we have proof?” Mira asked.

Priya understood the question she had not made precise. She always did, then generally punished the imprecision.

“We have one witnessed day,” she said. “Ask me again after four thousand hours.”

---

The price meeting began at 18:00 in the west conference room and achieved shouting before the tea arrived.

Tomás had circulated one sheet. This was considered an act of aggression by directors who had prepared decks.

The recommended launch price from the commercial committee would have made the first units profitable before their second annual service inspection. It also exceeded what the median household earned in three years. Demand at that price still surpassed the first two years of capacity. Maeda made the case without apology: the factory was new, the warranty exposure was not a model but a blank page, and underpricing an object people already wanted desperately was how companies taught markets to distrust them.

Tomás's number sat at the bottom of the sheet. It was less than half the committee's recommendation. With the ten-year service covenant and the repair reserve Priya required, Halcyon would lose money on every household unit through the second production run.

“Then it is not a price,” one director said. “It is a donation.”

“No,” Tomás said. “Donations are tax deductible.”

Mira watched Marcus remove his glasses and place them on the table, which was his private equivalent of a weather siren.

Tomás had not proposed charity. Hospitals and municipal partners would lease at a higher supported rate. Ten-year household financing put the monthly payment below median Osaka rent, through a trust Halcyon could not resell. No auction, no priority tier, no first-edition finish, no transfer during the first five years except back through the company or under a documented household change. One unit to a beneficial owner until supply caught demand. The board hated each provision for a different valid reason.

“You are manufacturing a gray market in the same document that forbids it,” Maeda said.

“A gray market is coming whether we charge one yen or ten million,” Tomás said. “The question is whether we build the front door for homes or leave only the service entrance for wealth.”

He pointed through the glass wall toward the factory floor. The hundred bodies had been moved from their stations to quiet rooms in cohorts of ten. The stations stood empty again, cables coiled, watcher lights green.

“At your price,” he said, “the first hundred go to collections. Climate-controlled garages. Corporate lobbies. Men who buy two because someone told them they could only have one. We will spend five years describing persons in our covenants and ship them as numbered editions.”

“At your price,” Marcus said, “we may spend three years shipping and a fourth in bankruptcy.”

“Then sell the institutions margin. Sell service. Sell every patient contract the market will tolerate. But the household line stays where a household can reach it.”

“Why?” Maeda asked. Not as a challenge. Mira heard in it the same diligence he had brought to the copying motion, the need to have the expensive sentence said aloud before anyone built around it.

Tomás looked at the operational numbers on the dark stations.

“Because they need to be adopted,” he said, “not collected. I mean the household accepts obligations too. I do not mean it gets to own a mind.”

Maeda closed his deck. Marcus stopped turning his glasses by one arm. Nobody withdrew an objection, but for eleven seconds nobody added one.

Tomás was CEO. Pricing within the approved loss envelope was his authority, a fact several directors now regarded as a drafting error. He signed the sheet before the tea arrived cold. Marcus put his glasses back on and asked Finance for a scenario in which Tomás was right, which was not agreement but was more useful.

Mira signed as co-chair after Sato. Tomás's model risked the company ledger. The committee's model risked teaching the world that a new mind was a luxury finish.

---

Near midnight, Sato asked her to walk the line.

Row F's work lights had been lowered for 058 and left that way after she moved to the quiet rooms; the lower level had proved adequate. In the softer light the hall lost its ceremony. It became what it was: a building full of tools, unfinished stations, and the day's marks on the floor. Someone had left a feather on the console at 032 with no note. Watanabe's dropped packet had been taped shut and set beside 074 for the morning shift.

At every station, a registry field waited blank beneath the operational number.

The naming committee had supplied a list of two hundred internationally pronounceable candidates. Legal wanted temporary names for consent forms. Support wanted something warmer than N17-058. Marketing, in a late act of self-preservation, wanted whatever Sato wanted.

He passed every white field without filling it.

“You built their faces,” Mira said.

“I built a face,” Sato said. “This is what has happened to it.”

At station 100 he stopped. Beyond it, nine hundred and twenty-four dust covers made low pale shapes in the dark.

“The forms need something,” Mira said.

“The forms have numbers.”

“The people who meet them tomorrow will ask.”

“Then they can say: not yet.” He rested one hand on the station rail, the posture of a man at a workbench. “They'll be named by the people who love them.”

Mira thought of Jenny's name sealed in a vault before there had been anyone to receive it, a girl's name misspelled by a trademark search and kept waiting for years. She thought of June, named for a courthouse month before June could object. Love did not make naming clean. It made the name a claim one had to spend a life earning.

“Some of them will name themselves,” she said.

Sato considered this and nodded. “Then the people who love them should listen.”

They walked back through the hundred stations. Commissioning access had closed with the shift; from now on, only welfare lines and the right to stop what became unsafe returned to the hall. Mira knew the kinds of work underway beyond the west wall, but not their result: a bird might be kept or thinned, an alarm given context, a laugh weighted by what followed it. The answer belonged to the one it had happened to.

At the gallery stairs, Mira turned for one last look.

In the morning the bodies had made a pattern so perfect she could not see its end. By midnight the stations were empty and the pattern was gone. What remained was a feather, a repaired packet, a changed light level, a hundred blank name fields, and somewhere behind the wall a hundred first days becoming memory at different speeds.

The factory had assembled the same body plan a hundred times.

It had failed, completely, to assemble the same morning twice.
