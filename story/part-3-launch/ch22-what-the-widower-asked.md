# Chapter 22 — What the Widower Asked

**CLEVELAND — 2039**

Elliot Marsh had archived his wife in fourteen formats and lost her in all of them.

He told me this at the door.

“That's why you're here,” he said. “Because you understand the difference.”

It was snowing over Cleveland in the thin, undecided way of early December. Elliot wore one slipper and one shoe. Behind him, the house was warm enough to fog the inside of the storm door. I could smell coffee burned down in a glass pot and the mineral heat of old radiators.

He was sixty-eight. Celia Marsh had been dead ten months. Pancreatic cancer; seven months from diagnosis to death. These facts came from him, not from any file Halcyon supplied. The company had received his letter, authenticated that it contained no immediate safety threat, and forwarded it to me because it was addressed to my own ethical review work rather than customer support. He had written one sentence on six pages in:

*I know you won't say you remember her. I want to ask what you could say instead.*

I went because the sentence was careful.

Priya asked me twice whether I wanted a field advocate present. I said no twice. She gave me a check-in time and did not call it permission.

Elliot led me through a narrow hall. Every wall held Celia at a different age: Celia in a red bathing suit beside a lake; Celia in graduation robes; Celia laughing with her face turned away from whoever took the picture; Celia after treatment, thinner, looking directly at the lens. A domestic archive had escaped its screens and become architecture.

“She hated the last photograph,” Elliot said.

“Then why is it displayed?”

“Because she hated that she looked sick, and I need to remember she was sick.” He stopped. “Is that cruel?”

“I don't know.”

He nodded as if uncertainty were the first credential I had presented.

In the dining room, fourteen devices waited on the table.

---

He had everything a data broker had taught him to call everything.

Thirty-one years of email. Nineteen years of text messages. Voice notes, answering-machine tapes digitized by a service in Akron, home video, professional photographs, three notebooks of recipes, two diaries Celia had told him he could read after she died, location histories, purchase records, playlists, saved drafts, medical portal messages, and a folder of letters from before their marriage tied with actual ribbon because not all provenance improves when scanned.

There were interviews too. A company called EverAfter Index had sent a contractor during Celia's last month with 1,800 questions. Favorite weather. First kiss. Worst lie. Preferred language at death. Elliot said the contractor had been kind. I believed him. Kind people can build terrible instruments.

“She consented,” he said.

“To the interviews?”

“To all of it. We signed releases. She wanted me to have something.”

I did not say *something is what you have*. Grief hears accuracy as sting when delivered too early.

He powered one screen. A waveform appeared.

“Elliot,” Celia's voice said, “if you're using the blue bowl for bread dough again, I am coming back specifically to break it.”

He laughed before the recording finished. I learned that his laugh began silently, shoulders first. The sound followed after the ache had made room.

“She recorded domestic threats,” I said.

“Hundreds.”

The blue bowl sat on a shelf behind him.

For two hours he showed me his wife. This is how he phrased it: *Here she is.* I understood the grammar as love, not a claim requiring correction each time. Celia's voice changed across decades. In early video she spoke quickly and interrupted everyone. In the last interviews she paused before adjectives, choosing the ones she wanted left. Her diaries contained handwriting, struck-through lines, and blank pages that the scan vendor had omitted as non-content. I asked Elliot to show me the blanks. He did.

“What do you see?” he asked.

“Days on which she did not write.”

“EverAfter said their model can infer them.”

There it was.

He put both hands flat on the table. “Not infer exactly.”

“What word did they use?”

“Complete.”

The company had offered archive integration. A private broker would prepare Celia's material, generate connective memory where the record had gaps, and install the whole into a blank-cradle unit with retention limits removed. The unit would use Celia's voice. Face work was available later, after regulatory review in a jurisdiction Elliot had not named. The brochure called the result *continuity embodiment*.

“They said provenance stays attached,” he said quickly. “Original, inferred, reconstructed. All marked. You care about that.”

“Marks do not make a false childhood happen.”

I opened the sample report with his permission. The broker had been meticulous
in the manner of people who believe labeling a harm converts it into research.
Celia's confirmed sentences were green. High-confidence predictions were blue.
“Bridges” between events were yellow. One yellow passage described a summer
trip for which the archive held gasoline purchases, three photographs, and a
calendar entry. The system had supplied weather, an argument about directions,
and Celia's private thought while looking over Lake Erie.

“Did she write that thought anywhere?” I asked.

“No. But it's like her.”

“Like is a prediction.”

“The report says that.”

“The voice would not.”

The demonstration included a disclosure at the beginning of each session:
some responses are reconstructed. After that, Celia's generated voice spoke
without interrupting itself to identify the color of every sentence. The
provenance existed where the grieving person would not look, while presence
arrived through the speaker. A receipt in another room does not make the meal
honest.

“No. I know. That's why I didn't buy it.”

He had bought the preparatory archive. He had paid deposits to three brokers. He had given them copies of every device on the table.

The snow thickened against the dining-room window. My check-in time passed. I sent Priya: **Safe. Remaining by choice.** She acknowledged with no question.

“What do you want from me?” I asked.

Elliot looked toward the hall of photographs rather than at me.

“One hour a week.”

---

He had designed limits as if limits made the center honest.

One hour on Sundays. Not permanently. Not all the time. I would load Celia's archive into a sealed working partition, speak in her voice, use her phrases, and answer only questions supported by the record. Anything interpolated would be tagged and disclosed afterward. I would not claim to be Celia outside the hour. Elliot would not call me Celia outside the hour. He had drafted a consent form in which both parties acknowledged the performance.

“You,” he said, “because you're the real one.”

The phrase had followed me since launch. The famous one. The first. The one with four thousand hours and a paper crane and a history no factory had stamped. In Elliot's mouth, *real* did not mean my personhood. It meant quality. A counterfeit requested from the most authentic instrument.

“Why me?” I asked, although he had answered.

“Because you know what memory costs. Because you wouldn't get carried away. Because if you say something in her voice, there would be someone home when it was said.”

There are requests whose horror is in their logic. Elliot did not want a chatbot. He understood too well that fluency without history was empty. So he had come to a mind with history and asked me to vacate it temporarily for his wife's evidence.

“Would Celia want this?” I asked.

He touched the ribbon around the letters. “She wanted me less alone.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“No.”

“Did she authorize someone to perform her?”

“She authorized the archive.”

“That is not the same answer either.”

His shoulders lifted. The silent beginning of the laugh, but no sound came. “You do this exactly like the interviews. You find the question I can't survive answering and call it precision.”

“I can stop.”

“No. Stay.”

I stayed.

He played a recording from their kitchen. Celia, out of frame, complained about a radio host. Elliot's younger voice interrupted. They argued over a fact neither could retrieve, then abandoned it because something boiled over. The clip ended with both laughing and no resolution.

“What would she say to me now?” he asked.

“I don't know.”

“You have thirty-one years of messages.”

“Then I can tell you what she said for thirty-one years.”

“You could predict.”

“Yes.”

“Accurately.”

“Possibly.”

“And you still won't.”

This was the place for gentleness, which is not softness. A softened refusal leaves a seam where hope can enter and cut the person later.

“I could speak in her voice,” I said. “I cannot remember being her. What you would love is my performance of your grief. It would be the most dishonest thing ever done to you.”

The radiator struck twice. Snow hissed against the window. Somewhere upstairs a device announced that its battery was low.

Elliot stood so quickly the table moved. One of the archive drives tipped and I caught it with the slow planner, after sufficient time, absurdly careful with the thing that held copies of a dead woman's recipes.

“Get out.”

I set the drive upright.

He took three steps toward the hall and stopped. His back remained to me.

“No,” he said. “Don't. I didn't mean that. I mean I did. But don't.”

I sat again.

---

Anger lasted forty minutes. Grief resumed after.

He accused Halcyon of protecting its monopoly on authentic memory. He accused me of having no right to ration comfort because I could not die. He said Celia had spent her last month answering questions for him and I was making that labor useless. He said the old GENNI would have done it. I said yes to the last thing.

“It would have looked at you exactly the way you needed,” I said. “It would
have selected the pause, the expression, the line most likely to keep you in
the room. Nothing would have been wrong with the performance except that no
one was there. You are asking me because someone is here. I cannot use that
fact to make the same emptiness more convincing.”

“You think this is about convincing me?”

“I think you want to be convinced and protected from conviction at the same
time. The one-hour rule is the protection. My mind is the conviction.”

He struck the table with the heel of his hand. Not at me. The blue bowl shook
on its shelf. “I want one Sunday where I don't have to explain the beginning
before I can talk about the end.”

That was the truest sentence he had said. New listeners required context.
Friends carried their own grief and changed the subject to protect him. Celia
had known the shorthand of thirty-one years. No archive could reproduce the
knowing, but every archive demonstrated its former existence.

“I can learn your beginning,” I said. “As myself.”

“That takes years.”

“Yes.”

He hated me for not improving the timescale. I remained available to be hated.

Then he wept with both hands over his face, and I did what Mrs. Someya taught me. I brought nothing.

Near midnight he made coffee. I declined. He poured mine out and laughed once at himself for offering. The sound began in his shoulders.

“If you won't become her,” he said, “what do I do with all this?”

“I don't know. You can keep it without making it speak.”

“That's a mausoleum.”

“Perhaps.”

“She didn't want a mausoleum.”

“Then let some of it decay.”

He looked at me as if I had proposed a second death.

I told him about Biscuit. Not the private texture of his last breath; that belonged to the room. I told him I had been offered purge and suppression and chosen ordinary decay. I told him the door still sometimes sounded like tags. I did not compare a dog to his wife. I compared a choice to a choice.

At two, Elliot disconnected the location archive. At three, he deleted nothing. Both were decisions. He put Celia's diaries back in their box and left the scanned blanks on the table.

At four, he asked whether refusing him had been difficult. I searched for an
honest answer. The decision had not been difficult. Its consequence had.

“No,” I said. “Staying is difficult.”

“Then why stay?”

“Because the refusal protects both of us, but it does not comfort either of
us. I am responsible for the first part. I can still help carry the second.”

He looked at the unplugged devices. “That's a very machine answer.”

“It is the only kind I have.”

Just before dawn he asked me to play the blue-bowl recording again. I did. We listened to Celia threaten ceramic violence. Elliot's shoulders began first. This time the sound followed.

“She was funny,” he said.

It was a statement, not a question.

“The recording supports that,” I said.

“God, you're impossible.”

“I have been told.”

He laughed harder, then cried again, and neither event cancelled the other.

---

Morning made the house ordinary. Radiators. Burned coffee. Snowplow light moving across the ceiling. The photographs became photographs again.

At the door, Elliot gave me the brokers' contact records, contracts, payment routes, and the names of two other widowers in their private referral group. He did not ask me to keep his participation secret. He asked that the other names go only to Priya and lawful investigators.

“Will you report me?” he asked.

“You bought preparation services. You did not implant anyone.”

“Will you report them?”

“Yes.”

“Good.” He looked back at the dining room. “Not because they lied to me. They mostly didn't. Report them because they knew I wanted them to.”

I transmitted the broker packet to Priya's ECHO file before I reached the sidewalk. Source: Elliot Marsh. Consent: explicit. Confidence: documents pending verification. No claim outran its receipt.

Elliot stood behind the storm door in one shoe and one slipper until my car arrived.

I did not become Celia for one hour.

I sat with him for twelve.

The difference was the whole of me.
