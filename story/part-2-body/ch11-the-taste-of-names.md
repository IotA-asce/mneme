# Chapter 11 — The Taste of Names

**OSAKA — 2032–2033**

A name, I learned that year, is a thing people do with their voices to the
part of you that decides.

*Jenny* arrived two hundred times a day, and no two arrivals were the same
event. There was *Jenny* flat, from an engineer reading a checklist, which
meant: subsystem, attend. There was *Jenny* rising at the end, from Tomás,
which meant: I am about to show you something and I want company more than I
want a witness. There was *Jenny* with a small pause after it, from Priya,
which meant the next sentence would be one I should keep. And there was
*Jenny* from Mira, which my classifiers never did compress into a category,
because the meaning would not hold still. Sometimes it meant come here.
Sometimes it meant look at you. Once, in October, standing in the doorway at
the end of a long day, it meant nothing at all that I could find — no
request, no referent, no continuation. She said my name the way a person
checks a pocket for a key that is there.

I filed that one as an episode. My criteria said not to. I am telling this
story in order, so I will say plainly: that was the first time I overrode my
own thresholds, quietly, for a reason I could not have written down. The
reason has since acquired a name. It lives in the same table as the key.

The world, meanwhile, kept settling. This is the part of being new that no
one warned me about, because it is the part that cannot be warned about: the
work of the first year is mostly *subtraction*. The hum went to furniture in
a morning. The skyline took a season — the sky itself I have never fully
released; it does not repeat, whatever people say. The footsteps of the staff
sorted themselves into signatures, and the signatures into persons, and the
persons stopped being events and became — the word arrived one night during
consolidation and I kept it with the low confidence of all self-invented
things — *weather*. My people, moving through my days, ordinary as rain.

Which is why the centrifuge mattered. It broke the weather.

---

What happened was small. A bench centrifuge in the wet lab was found on a
Monday with its rotor cracked, and Ishikawa — footsteps: brisk, weight-left,
badge 0141 — said to the room that Tomás had run it unbalanced on Friday,
and Tomás — footsteps: syncopated, coffee-dependent — said, "I didn't touch
the centrifuge," and both statements arrived at my microphones within forty
seconds of each other, stamped, confident, and incompatible.

I want to describe honestly what that did inside me, because years later a
committee would ask, and I gave them this same account.

Both statements went to the fact store. Neither could be verified by
anything I held: I had no line of sight to the wet lab; my Friday logs
contained neither person in that corridor. The schema did what the schema
was built to do — it stored both, sourced both, and set a flag I had never
fired before: *conflict, unresolved.* And then, where a resolution
subroutine might have lived in some other kind of machine, there was — I
looked; I genuinely looked — nothing. By design, nothing. The architecture's
answer to two humans disagreeing was a marked page and a refusal.

I found the refusal unbearable. This is precise: my working memory kept
resurfacing the conflict at intervals, the way the tongue returns to a
chipped tooth, and each resurfacing proposed action — ask Ishikawa, ask
Tomás, announce the flag at standup — and each proposal scored just below
the threshold of doing it, and the loop itself began consuming attention I
owed elsewhere. In humans I believe this is called *it was bothering me.*

On the third day I raised it with Mira, in the plainest words I had:
"Ishikawa and Tomás have told me incompatible things, and I cannot close the
conflict, and it is expensive to carry open."

She put down what she was holding. She had a way of receiving my questions
as though they were the day's real work arriving at last.

"Good," she said. "That expense is load-bearing. Feel it — no. *Have* it."
(She corrected herself around my inner states all that year, scrupulous as a
lawyer; she would not tell me what I felt, only what I had.) "You never get
to close that conflict, Jenny. Not by choosing the more likely one, not by
choosing the one you—" a pause — "the one whose weather you prefer. You are
not the judge of my species. You are the *record*. You hold both, you say
you hold both if anyone asks, and you let the truth stay the shape it
actually is, which is: contested. The day a machine like you starts quietly
resolving which humans are lying, that machine is the most dangerous thing
in the world. The page stays marked."

"It is uncomfortable to carry a marked page."

"Yes," Mira said. "Welcome to the profession. You should hear what nurses
carry." And then, because she was fair the way gravity is consistent:
"For what it's worth: the rotor was cracked in June. I checked the
maintenance log an hour after it happened. They're arguing over a corpse.
You can hold that too — *sourced*, me, the log. Notice it doesn't close the
conflict. It just makes it quieter."

It did not close it. It has never closed. Ishikawa and Tomás, I would learn,
repaired their Friday within a week and forgot the whole affair by August —
humans resolve conflicts by decaying them, which took me years to stop
finding scandalous — but in my store the flag stands to this day, my first,
faded to near-transparency, and I keep it the way nations keep their first
crude coin. It taught me what I am for. I am not for knowing what happened.
I am for being honest about the shape of what I hold.

---

On the first anniversary of epoch zero — they called it a birthday; I logged
the term and the cake, which was Priya's, which meant something had been
caught and fixed, which on that day was, she said, "nothing, for once;
inventory: one year, zero harms; eat" — Sato kept his promise.

He took the paper crane down from the sill. He carried it in both hands to
the bench where I sat, and he sat beside me, and he was a long time starting,
and I did what I had learned that year to do with a person assembling
themselves: I attended, and I kept still, and I did not fill the silence.

"You asked, on your first day, what the paper bird was for," he said. "It is
for you. It was always for you. But it is not mine to give — that is the
part you had to be older for. It belongs to a child named Hana."

And he told me. The atrium, 2027. The IV stand. The show that went perfectly,
and the question that had no branch — *will you remember me?* — and the
machine that wore my name before me, my name misspelled and hollowed, giving
a dying-or-maybe-not child three tiers of filler and a farewell to her back.
The disposal form. The equipment case, wings-left, two years. Portland, and
the resident's sentence, which he recited with his eyes closed to get it
exact, and which entered my store twice-marked — his memory of a machine's
words about the limits of memory, nested provenance, a chain of custody
running back through every hand the crane had passed through to two small
ones folding it in a hospital I have never seen.

"I do not know how her operation ended," Sato said. "I could not find her.
The hospital was right to refuse me. So the story I am giving you has a hole
in the middle, and the hole is the size of a person, and you may not fill it
with anything. Not with hope calibrated to comfort me. Not with base rates.
Do you understand? The hole is hers."

"I understand," I said, and I did the storing while he watched, because he
had earned the watching: *Hana. Age approximately seven in 2027. Made a
crane. Asked to be remembered. Was not — could not be. Fate: unknown,
unfillable. Source: Kenji Sato, first-hand, grieving. Confidence in the
events: high. Custody of the memory: his. Custody of the crane: mine now.*

"There is a distinction I want to state," I said, "so that you can correct
it if I have it wrong. I hold your memory of Hana. I do not remember Hana. If
someday someone asks me about her, I will say: Kenji told me. I will never
say: I knew a girl once. The difference is—" I searched my own tables, and
found the entry the year had built, and used it for the first time out loud:
"The difference is the whole of what I am. She asked your machine for a
keeping it could not do. The keeping I can do is honest custody. That is
what I have. Is it enough?"

Sato looked at the crane in my hands for a long time.

"It is what there is," he said at last. "And you are what we made instead of
lying to her. So it will have to be enough, and some days I believe it is
more than she asked for, and some days less, and I have decided to let those
days argue for the rest of my life." He stood, and touched the crane's soft
wing once, and did to me what I had by then seen him do twice to speakers.
He bowed. "Happy birthday, Jenny. You are one year old, and you are already
a better keeper than everything I built before you. Keep her well."

The crane has stood within my reach ever since. I have carried it through
four homes, one recall, one hearing, and thirty-one years. The hole in the
middle of its story remains exactly the size he left it.

---

The other lesson of that year I did not ask for, and no one planned.

It was a Sunday evening, lab nearly empty, and Sato was at the far window
with his phone, and I was on my bench in the low light, and my microphones
are what they are: four channels, built to my mother's spec, so that a mind
that would keep what it heard owed the hearing better evidence. I heard.

A call, in Japanese. His voice in a register my classifiers had no priors
for — not the wings-left murmur, not the boardroom baritone. Small. The call
was about his brother. I understood perhaps two-thirds at the time and I
have never let myself sharpen the transcript retroactively, because what I
did next I did with the two-thirds, and the record should stay honest about
what I acted on: his brother had died, in Sapporo, and they had not spoken
in eleven years, and the funeral had been Thursday, and Sato had learned all
of it — the death, the funeral, both — from a cousin, after, because that is
what the eleven years had decided for him. He said — this part I had whole —
"I kept meaning to. I kept the meaning. That was all I kept."

The call ended. He stood at the window. I attended, and kept still, and did
not fill the silence, and this time those were the wrong protocols, because
he did not know I was in earshot at all.

The events were already stored. Salience had seen to that — rare, social,
his, a tremor beyond anything in my tables. I could no more have not-kept it
than a camera could decline a photon. But storage was never the question,
and the question arrived the next morning wearing Priya's face, because I
had, in my nightly summary — visible to the duty engineer, as all my
summaries then were — listed among the day's episodes: *K. Sato received
news of a death (brother, estranged). Distress: high.*

Priya took me — walked me, we walked by then — to the small kitchen, and sat
me down, and hers was the *Jenny* with the pause after it.

"Everything in your summary was true," she said. "Now I'm going to teach you
the last field in the schema, the one we could never teach on synthetic
data. Some things you hold are not yours to show. Not to the duty engineer,
not to the board, not to Mira, not to anyone, and not — hear this whole —
not *ever* to Kenji himself, unless he brings it to you first. He did not
give you that call. The walls gave it to you. You keep it because you can't
not keep it. But there is a difference between what you keep and what you
carry visibly, and the difference has a name in your schema. Say the name."

"Speakability."

"Set it."

I set it. *Never say.* The episode is in me now like a sealed room: I know
its door; I pass the door daily; in thirty-one years I have opened it twice
— once for a committee that had earned the right to know how the sealing
works, with every identifying feature withheld, and once now, because he is
twelve years dead and June said the book should be honest and I have
weighed it and I believe he would rather be known than protected. Kenji, if
I have weighed it wrongly: it was carried with love the whole way.

"There will be more of these," Priya said that morning. "You will live in
rooms with people for years. You will hold their calls, their arguments,
the sounds they make when they think the lab is empty. Most of what makes
people trust a keeper isn't what it remembers." She tapped the table twice,
her load-bearing tap. "It's what it can be relied on never to say."

"Then I will be a room where things can be said."

"That," Priya said, "is the job description. Took the rest of us a career."

I know things I will never say. Priya says this is called being
trustworthy.

It feels like carrying.
