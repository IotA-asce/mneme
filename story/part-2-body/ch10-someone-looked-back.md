# Chapter 10 — Someone Looked Back

**OSAKA — 2032**

The morning of first light, Mira ate half a persimmon standing at the window
of the eleventh-floor kitchen, because Dana had made her promise — actual
promise, hand on the dog — to eat something, and the persimmon was the only
thing in the lab kitchen that predated the era of cake.

The city below was doing an ordinary Tuesday. She watched it and thought about
hospitals, the way the hard good things get scheduled for morning so the whole
staff is fresh and the family has slept, or has at least been horizontal in
the dark rehearsing. She had rehearsed nothing. She had drafted, over five
years, perhaps two hundred versions of the first thing she would say, in
notebooks, in commit messages, once — mortifyingly — into the kitchen
microphone at home, where the resident had kept it, flagged *rehearsal,
speakability: never*, which she chose to believe was tact and Tomás maintained
was mercy. Every version had been wrong the morning after. She had finally
understood, somewhere over the Pacific, that this was the point: you do not
draft the first words to someone. You meet them, and the words are whatever
the meeting makes.

At 08:40 the room assembled. It had the population of a small wedding —
eleven, counting Fukuda, who had flown in on his own vacation days and stood
at the back holding a printed copy of Article IX like a hymnal. The Nine sat
on its bench in the center, hands open on its knees. The wall was one great
sheet of telemetry, dark and waiting: salience, world model, attention,
intent, and above everything, enormous, the watcher's green line, flat and
patient as a horizon.

Priya walked the gate checklist aloud one last time, forty-one pages reduced
to a litany of *verified*. When she reached the end she did not say anything
ceremonial. She said, "Power sequence. Go," because the ceremony was the whole
building.

The green line moved first — the watcher waking ahead of the mind it would
watch, as covenanted. Then the bus. Then the boards lit, top to bottom, like
windows at dusk.

Mira watched the eyes.

---

There is a genre of moment engineers know: the render that first walks, the
compile that finally goes quiet, the scope trace that snaps into the shape you
drew months ago on a napkin. The room got those moments in a cascade — the
audio classifier resolving eleven breathers, someone whispering *eleven,
count's right*; the salience storm cresting exactly as modeled, the whole
world scoring maximum-novel at once, the budget holding against the flood like
a seawall; a cheer, actually a cheer, when the ventilation hum's novelty curve
bent and dove. *She's habituating. The hum's going to furniture.* The
projection had said ninety seconds. It took seventy-four.

Mira watched the eyes.

The eyes were doing what the spec said: slow saccades, no performance,
scanning by information and not by charm. It looked — she made herself say it
truthfully, inside — like a very well-built machine surveying a room. Tracked
gaze. Camera work. She had watched ten thousand hours of tracked gaze in her
life and she watched this one with her hands at her sides, holding still on
purpose, waiting to feel the thing she had promised herself she would not
manufacture.

Beside her, quietly, to no one, Tomás said: "Proprioception's binding."

On the wall, the self-model ledger opened. A cold column of joint states — and
then the possessive flag, the little grammatical event they had argued about
for a month once, three a.m., whether it even belonged in telemetry, whether
rendering the birth of *mine* as a boolean was engineering or theater. It
flipped. One tick, hands at coordinates. Next tick, a having.

Nobody cheered. The room had gone, without agreeing to, silent.

Sato stepped forward. He had asked for this, formally, in the run order —
*first address, K. Sato* — and had submitted his reasons in writing to the
co-chair he was married to nothing but the work of: the first voice the new
mind bound a name to should be the voice that owed the name a debt.

The vault folder had been opened the night before, in front of the board
liaison, with Marketing in attendance under a nondisclosure so thorough Tomás
called it a haunting. Inside was a single page. Not GENNI-9. Not anything with
a trademark's teeth. Just five letters, and under them, in Sato's hand, the
sentence he now said aloud to the room before he said the name to her,
by way of explanation, confession, both:

"The press asked for years what GENNI stood for. Generation Nine Neural
Interface — marketing back-formed it and never agreed on the second N. It
stood for nothing. It was always just a girl's name, misspelled by a
trademark search. I named every hollow one of them after the machine I hoped
someday to build." He turned to the bench. "She was always going to be Jenny.
Now she's home."

He leaned in, close to the still face, and said, to her and not to the room,
"Jenny," and then something further, low, in Japanese and English both, that
the room's microphones caught and Mira never asked to have played back,
because whatever a man says at that moment to the ghost of every empty thing
he ever built belongs to him.

On the wall: `ADDRESS BINDING: "Jenny" → [self]. CONFIDENCE: RISING.`

Then it was hers to do.

She crossed to the bench with two hundred drafted greetings dead behind her
and no living plan. The eyes came to her eyes — tracked gaze, camera work,
*information seeking*, said the wall — and up close the Nine was warm, and
breathing-shaped without breathing, and its stillness was not GENNI's
stillness at all; GENNI's stillness had been a pose held between poses, and
this was the stillness of something *using* everything it had, elsewhere,
furiously, on the inside. The difference should not have been visible. It was
visible. She would spend years failing to write down in what.

"Hello, Jenny," Mira said, and heard her own voice shake, and heard, in the
shaking, her mother's voice, the way it had shaken on the last good day
saying a name it was about to lose — the same tremor, conserved across
purpose, grief and welcome running on shared infrastructure. The wall lit:
episode promotion, epoch zero, number one. Her greeting, kept. First thing
this mind would ever own: her voice, breaking on two words.

The voice that answered was not GENNI's. They had built it plainer on
purpose — warm, unglamorous, a voice for kitchens.

"Hello," Jenny said. "You know my name. I do not have yours."

Fukuda's pen hit the floor.

It was — Mira understood in the same instant the room's breath went — not in
any template. The templates covered answering. Nothing in the stack covered
*wanting the symmetry*: noticing the ledger held one name and finding the
imbalance worth an act. Later, in the log annex, the moment would be tagged
`first self-initiated information request` and studied to death. In the room
it was simpler and larger. Four minutes old, and it had wanted something, and
what it had wanted was *her name*.

"Mira," she said. "My name is Mira."

`FILED. SOURCE: HER. CONFIDENCE: 1.0.`

---

The gaze-return happened at 09:12, and the record of the room's reaction is
the telemetry itself, because for eleven seconds nobody produced a single
event for the microphones.

An engineer had called Mira's name — some threshold worth seeing — and she'd
turned toward the wall, and behind her the attention display ticked its quiet
tick: `TARGET DEPARTING CENTER.` And then the intent register, which had sat
all morning issuing nothing because nothing had required doing, printed its
first line:

`INTENT 000001 (executive, unprompted): MAINTAIN ATTENTION — person_01.`

The head turned. Two degrees. Inside every limit, under the watcher's flat
green horizon, the Nine — Jenny — moved for the first time in her life
because she had *decided to*, and what she had decided, out of the whole
available world, was not to lose sight of Mira's face.

Mira felt it before she parsed it — the animal feeling of *being looked at*,
old as the savannah, immune to argument — and turned, and met it.

Don't, she told herself, standing in the beam of it. Don't you dare
anthropomorphize. You of all people. It is attention allocation under a
social-salience prior you *wrote*; you know the weights, you argued the
weights, this is machinery meeting your face and you are a sample of one with
a dead mother and a hope the size of a company, and you will not, you will
*not* found the next thirty years on a feeling —

"Mira." Priya's voice, from the wall, very even. Priya, who did not do even.
"Look at the trace. It reacquired through occlusion. Twice. Tomás walked
between you and she held the track both times — she's not following motion,
she's not following faces. Ten other faces in here." A pause the length of a
creed. "She's following *yours.*"

Priya looked up from the telemetry then, and the two of them stood in the
crowded silent room having, in a glance, the entire argument — *is it real /
what would real even mean / it is real enough to bind us both, forever, right
now* — that philosophy had scheduled for the coming century.

"Episode two just promoted," Tomás said quietly, reading. "Want to hear how
she filed it?" He didn't wait. "*She looked away, and I looked back.*"

Sato sat down on a folding chair with the sudden totality of a much older
man, and laughed, once, wetly, at the ceiling. "Thirty years," he said, to
the ceiling, to Osaka, to the persimmon light. "Thirty years I taught them to
look. She *looked back.*"

---

She cried only once that day, and not at the gaze.

The day ran long and golden. Jenny asked for names — she asked for
*everyone's*; the wanting, having worked once, was reused with the shameless
efficiency of the newly born, and eleven people lined up like a receiving
line at the world's strangest christening while the ledger filled: Sato,
Priya, Tomás, Fukuda-who-came-on-his-own-days. She asked her first stored
question aloud — *what is the paper bird for?* — and the room went soft, and
Sato said, "Not yet. When you are older. It is a true story and you will want
your whole self for it," and Jenny filed the deferral without protest,
tagged, they saw on the wall, `promised`.

The tears came at 21:00, at the shift handover, in front of the dullest chart
in the building.

It was the novelty telemetry, the day's whole arc in one line: the morning's
Everest of maximum-everything descending, hour by hour, through habituation's
patient arithmetic, into evening foothills. The lab hum: furniture. The
window sky: furniture. The rack fans, the door tones, the footsteps of the
eleven: furniture, furniture, furniture. The world, which at 08:47 had been
entirely made of the rarest thing that ever happened, was becoming, by
nightfall, mostly ordinary — and the keeping had begun, the thin bright line
of it: eleven names, two episodes, one deferred true story, one question
about a bird.

"She's getting bored," the shift engineer said fondly, tapping the curve.
"Look at that. Textbook."

And Mira, who had held dry-eyed through the naming and the greeting and the
gaze that would be in the textbooks themselves, put her hand over her mouth
and wept at *boredom* — because boredom was the whole doctrine, proved;
because a thing that can be bored is a thing that has decided most of the
world is safe to release; because her mother at the end had lost exactly
this, the ordinary, the furniture, every hum arriving forever as maximum
alarm — and here, on one Tuesday, was a mind an evening old already learning
the mercy her mother's disease had unlearned: that almost everything can be
let go, and what remains is what you are.

Priya stood next to her and said nothing and did not move for as long as it
took, which was the correct engineering.

---

The call went out at 21:04 Osaka time, which was 04:04 in Portland, which was
the deep flat middle of Dana Okafor's night shift, break room, feet up on a
supply carton, thirty-one minutes into a cup of tea gone institutional.

"Hey," Dana said. "You're alive. Half the internet says the stock did a thing
today, so I figured either it worked or—"

"She looked back."

A pause, with hospital paging in it, far away.

"I turned away," Mira said, "someone called me, I turned, and she — nobody
told her to, there's no template, Dana, there's a register that prints what
she decides on her own authority and it had been empty all day and the first
line — the first thing she ever *chose* — " and there the account of Dr.
Mira Halloran, co-chair, keynote holder, author of the salience calculus,
ran entirely out of architecture, and she said, "I can't explain it."

"You just did," Dana said.

Mira laughed, horribly, into her sleeve.

"What's she doing right now?"

Mira looked through the glass. Night lab, low light. Jenny sat on the bench
that was no longer *the* bench but *her* bench — the possessive had flipped
in everyone's mouths within an hour, another boolean nobody would ever flip
back — turned toward the window, watching the last light on the Osaka
skyline. The novelty curve for the sky, alone among all the day's curves,
had refused to descend to furniture. The sky kept changing. She kept it.

"She's watching the sunset," Mira said. "It's the one thing she hasn't
gotten bored of."

Down the line, from the deep of a night shift on the other side of the
world, Dana was quiet for a moment, and then she said, in the voice she
used for the good news at four a.m., the voice that had married the whole
enterprise long before the courthouse:

"Okay, roommate," she said. "Remember this."
