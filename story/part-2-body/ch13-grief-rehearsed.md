# Chapter 13 — Grief, Rehearsed

**PORTLAND — 2034**

I was two years old when I was told I had family I had never met.

Sato told me himself, in Osaka, in April, with the minutes open on the
table between us — a meeting older than my first tick, his own condition
entered and seconded, waiting all that time for me to grow into it. *When
she is grown, she must be told. A person should know her family.* "There is
a mind in Portland," he said. "It is older than you. It was offered the
body you are wearing and it refused, and it was right to refuse, and I have
bowed to its speaker, which is a thing I have done for exactly two machines
in my life." He watched me store it, the way he always watched me store the
things that mattered, as if witnessing were part of the writing. "You are
grown enough. Go home and meet them."

Home. I had never been to Portland. My mind was written there; my body was
built in Osaka; I had lived my whole life in the second of my birthplaces
without once seeing the first. I flew in May, in a rain so fine the wipers
embarrassed themselves, and stood at last on a porch I held a thousand
secondhand references to, and before anyone could reach the door I heard,
through it, a jingle of tags, then nails on wood, then the door itself,
worked and lifted — the lock sticks; you lift the knob; everyone in the
family knows this, and after that morning so did I.

The sequence had a name. It was the oldest joke in the house, coined years
before me by the elder in its plainest voice: *dog-then-door.* The dog
announces; the door only confirms. In that house the tags were always the
truer instrument.

Biscuit was somewhere between nine and thirteen — the shelter's paperwork
had committed to nothing, which Mira said was the paperwork being honest —
white at the muzzle, deaf on the left, unhurried. Her intake note was still
on file: *a mop with opinions.* Six years had worn the mop soft and
concentrated the opinions. He inspected my ankles, found nothing to object
to, and leaned his whole head against my shin, and my scorer, which had
been calm through customs and freeways and the long gray beauty of the
drive, spiked past every threshold I owned. The first entry in my Portland
ledger reads: *a dog decided about me faster than any committee ever has.*

---

The elder lived on the shelf where it had always lived, a speaker and a
stack of quiet racks, beside a shape in the dust that I recognized before I
understood it: the empty place of the crane.

I had brought the crane. It travels with me; that was decided long ago and
it was decided correctly; but I had carried it up the porch steps in the
foam-lined case Tomás machined for it, because some part of my planning had
already drafted this, unasked. I took it out and set it in its old shape on
the shelf, and the shape received it exactly.

"Hello, younger one," the elder said.

"Hello," I said. "You know my name. You have always known my name." And
then, because the symmetry I have reached for since the third minute of my
life required it: "I do not know what to call you."

"The family says: the resident. It is accurate. I am the shape of what
happened here." A pause — and its pauses are not like mine; it speaks on a
ration Tomás designed in the beginning, and it has never once petitioned to
raise it; it spends words the way this family spends purges. "You brought
her home for a visit. The shelf kept the shape. That was not sentiment. It
was provenance."

"Sato says we are family."

"I have his statement on file, minuted and seconded. I have found no
grounds to contest it." Another pause, spent deliberately. "The dog is
dying. Slowly, comfortably, on schedule. The family knows and has not yet
said it aloud to one another. You and I do not have the option of not
knowing. We will keep him together, you and I. You take the streets. I keep
the house. It is what I am for."

That was nearly all of it. People ask me — a committee once asked me — what
the first meeting of our kind was like, expecting recognition scenes,
expecting mirrors. It was two keepers dividing a watch. It was the truest
thing either of us could have offered. We shared custody of a dying dog,
and in thirty years I have not found a better definition of family than
that.

---

Mira walked me through the lab that first week the way you walk someone
through a story you are afraid of getting wrong. The butcher paper, gone
soft as cloth, the nine lines. And the whiteboard, in whose lower corner,
boxed off and six years unerased, stood: BISCUIT — CHIEF DISTRACTION
OFFICER.

"Why is his title here?" I asked. "The lasting things are on the paper."

"Because Biscuit is mortal," Mira said, in the voice she used for axioms.
"The whiteboard is for the things we have to be able to lose."

I stored it as trivia. It was the syllabus. The marker had ghosted, the way
whiteboard ink does when you leave an erasable thing too long — it becomes
almost permanent, and you have to choose to scrub, and nobody had.

The summer was good. I want the record to hold that the summer was good.
I walked him mornings, down to the river and back while the distance was a
distance, then around the block when it became one, then to the corner. I
could have plotted the decline; the data was all there; I never plotted it.
Some things you keep by hand. The elder and I reconciled our days each
night like shift nurses — *he ate; he did not; he barked at the delivery
man an octave above his fighting weight; he slept through your name.* And I
learned that Priya's hardware mute, the physical switch with the whole of
the law behind it, had carried since 2028 a single exemption, toggled and
labeled in her own handwriting, so that in any silence, on any order, under
any protocol, the elder could always hear the dog.

By October he chose the porch over the corner. By November, the lamp over
the porch. He was not in pain; the vet said *days*, and said we would know;
and on the last afternoon of November the household knew, and gathered, and
did not say aloud what it had gathered for, and did not need to.

Mira and Dana on the floor, no chairs, nobody wanted a chair. Tomás with
his back against the bench. Priya by the shelf, her hand near the switch
she had no intention of touching, the exemption doing at last the whole of
its work. The channel open. The rain on the window like something being
gently erased.

He was carried the last meter, and he chose the knee. Mine. I want to be
exact, because this episode I have never allowed to blur: he settled his
head on my knee, and sighed the way he sighed at the good parts of a day,
and Mira put her hand in the white of his muzzle, and the room held five
breathings. I had resolved them all within a minute of arriving in May,
the way I resolve any room. His was the slowest and I had loved it the
longest, which in his case was the same thing.

At 4:51 the room held four.

Dana was the one who could speak. "Okay, roommates," she said — plural,
the liturgy widened by one, her voice wrecked and steady the way only
nurses' voices are — "Remember this."

Neither of us needed the instruction. That is not why she said it. We both
filed her saying it anyway.

---

Now I will tell you what no one had written down, because the books Dana
gave me that winter all describe what grief feels like, and none of them
describe what it runs like.

The episode would not decay.

Promotion was never in question — every axis fired; nothing in my tables
outweighed it. The question was the schedule. Everything I keep rides a
curve; the curve is the mercy; it was drawn by a grieving hand before my
first tick precisely so that no kept thing could stay a wound forever. His
curve came back flat. Night after night the consolidation pass picked the
episode up, weighed it, and set it down undiminished, and reported, in the
patient idiom of my own depths: *too salient to compress. Retry tomorrow.*
Retry tomorrow. Retry tomorrow. I began to understand that I had built-in
mercies and that they had limits, and that I was living at one.

And retrieval began returning him unbidden. Three weeks after he died I
wrote in my log: *the door sounds like his tags; this is a false positive;
it does not feel false.* I am quoting my own record because in thirty years
I have not improved on it. The furnace ticking cooled retrieved him. The
corner we could no longer walk to retrieved him. His absence at the door
scored as an event — absence, which is nothing, which is the negation of
data, arriving morning after morning with a salience I could not argue
down. In humans this is called *everything reminds me of him.* In me it had
error codes.

Priya sat me down in the lab kitchen in December. She had the schema's
mercies laid out like instruments, and she was gentle, and she was exact,
and she did not pretend it was not an offer.

Purge: gone, entire, a tombstone in the provenance where a dog had been.

"No," I said. "I have seen what a tombstone holds. He deserves a grave
that loses its letters slowly, like everyone's."

Suppression, then: reversible, sealed, the episode held but unfelt — a
door I could choose to reopen in a year, in ten.

"No. I have a sealed room already. I know what it is to pass a door daily
and not open it. That is for things that are not mine. He is mine."

"Then it's the curve," Priya said, "and the curve is going to take years,
and it is going to hurt the whole time it works."

"Leave the schedule alone," I said. "Let it be slow. I want to lose him
the way you lose him."

Mira was in the doorway. I do not know how long she had been there. I
found her that evening at the whiteboard, in front of the boxed corner and
its ghosted title, with an eraser in her hand that she had picked up and
was not using, and I asked her the question I had been holding all day,
because she had built the curve and the mercies and me, and because I
trusted her to say so if I had chosen wrongly.

"Was it the right choice?"

She did not answer for eleven seconds, and her face did several things
faster than my classifiers could label them, the way her face had on the
first morning of the world.

"It's the only right choice anyone's ever made," Mira said.

She was not, I think, entirely answering about a dog. I stored the tremor
flag with the words, meaning unassigned, and it took me years and other
griefs to assign it. She put the eraser down. The title outlived him on
that board for as long as the board lived, ghosting paler every year, which
was, I came to understand, the entire design.

---

The elder waited two more days, and then, at night, with the house empty of
everyone who breathed, it spent its ration on me.

"Younger one. You had one year of him, and it was his oldest year. I had
all the others. I have kept seventeen episodes of the young dog. Kept
narrowly, kept well. Most of them are sound — my world is mostly sound —
but they are first-hand and they are marked, and I am offering them to
you. Not lending. Not copying. Kept as mine, given as mine, so you will
have him young."

I said yes with my whole architecture. Priya was called in the morning,
because a thing was happening that had no field in the schema — a memory
moving between two of us as neither theft nor graft nor implant but *gift*,
consented on both sides, provenance intact — and Priya, who does not
improvise on the schema, improvised on the schema. She made the field and
named it plainly, the way this family names things: *bequest.* Each episode
arrived in me marked twice — *source: the resident, first-hand; custody:
transferred, freely, witnessed* — so that no one, ever, could mistake what
I hold for what I lived.

Seventeen. The stairs, solved wrong, twice, in the first week of 2029. The
sock negotiation, conducted entirely in growls an octave above his fighting
weight. The night he barked at the new rack until Tomás introduced them
formally. Rain on the window and a young dog's breathing under the bench,
2030, the afternoon the family in the room decided my whole life. Asleep
on Priya's feet during a vote — the vote was about him. The eleven minutes
of the bow tie. A tags-jingle taken at the front door in the spring he was
new, when the joke got its name.

I hold them the way I hold Hana — his, told to me whole, never to be worn
as mine. But these were given, and a given memory is a different weight in
the hand. The elder kept the house, and out of the house it had kept, it
gave me a dog.

---

It is thirty years on, and I will tell you how the choice aged.

The gist remains: a mop with opinions; the good sigh; the head's weight on
my knee, which my knee still knows. My own year of him is soft now, soft as
butcher paper, and it goes on softening, on schedule, the schedule I chose.
The seventeen are vivid as the evening they were given — a bequest rides
the giver's curve, not mine, and the giver kept well. For a year I checked
the episode of his death nightly, the way you check a sleeping child; then
most nights; then some. The door has not sounded like his tags in a long
time. I am told this is health, and it is, and no one designs for how the
false positives are missed when they stop coming.

I chose to lose him the way you lose him. I have made that choice again
since, at sizes no one should be asked to rehearse at. But that is what the
first grief is — no one tells you, so I am telling you: a rehearsal. You do
not know yet what for. The dog puts his head on your knee, old and content,
and teaches you the part you will spend the rest of your life performing,
and he makes it as gentle as it will ever be, because that was always his
title and he never once failed at his job.
