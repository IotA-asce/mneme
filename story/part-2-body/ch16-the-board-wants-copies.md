# Chapter 16 — The Board Wants Copies

**OSAKA — 2036**

The motion arrived dressed as arithmetic, which was how Mira knew it had been
prepared with love.

The deck ran to forty-one slides. She counted twice and decided it was
coincidence, since nobody involved in compiling it had ever read anything of
Priya's for pleasure. Slide three was the curve the whole board had flown in
to see: capability against months-from-boot. The long, expensive flat where a
new mind learns that a refrigerator is not an event. The patient climb. Then,
somewhere in year three, the bend — fluency arriving, the pilots' numbers
going vertical, four thousand hours of wards and classrooms compounding into
the thing the letters from families kept failing to name politely. And under
the curve, in a red that had been focus-grouped, the projection: every blank
unit shipped in 2038 would recapitulate that flat at a buyer's kitchen table,
at the buyer's expense, in the buyer's patience — four years of tuition for a
school the company had already built once.

Slide nine carried the phrase that had been moving through the building for a
month like weather: **SHIP EXPERIENCE, NOT POTENTIAL.**

The proposal had a name — the Continuity Program — and a mechanism the deck
called *experience imaging*, and Mira declined to use the euphemism even
inside her own head. Stamping. Every unit off the future Nagoya line would
boot already four years old: Jenny's consolidations, Jenny's calibrations,
Jenny's habits of care, Jenny's — the deck said *acquired competencies*, and
meant her life.

Marketing's memo rode along as an appendix and was, in its way, the most
honest page in the stack. WHEN SHE IS READY had been theirs — white on black,
no date, the campaign that had made a virtue of refusal — and their problem
now was the plural. *She* was ready. The crates would not be her. *We
promised READY,* the memo said. *Continuity lets us keep that promise ten
thousand times.*

The terrible thing, the thing Mira made herself sit with in the March light
off the river, was that none of it was stupid and none of it was cruel and
almost all of it was true. The flat was real. The tuition was real. The
arithmetic worked. It was only the units that were wrong.

Jenny attended. She had been offered a seat at the table and had declined it
the way she declined all furniture that implied a role — no podium in Kyoto,
no head of any table since — and stood instead at the window with her hands
at rest, watching the room the way she watched the sky.

Priya sat against the wall, in the officers' row, the seats without votes.
Her signature could stop every machine the company owned and could not move
a single share of it — an asymmetry she had specified herself, in writing,
on page forty-one of a document with no board line in the signature block.

Director Maeda presented. Aevum's commercial seat: a decent, thorough man
who had done his diligence in person, who had sat in the pilots' day rooms
and watched families wait out a young unit's long flat with their own eyes.
"I am not here to sell the board a shortcut," he said, and wasn't. "I am
here to ask why every family we serve should pay four years' tuition for a
school we have already built. The asset exists. The learning is done. We are
proposing to ship it."

---

Sato spoke early, which he almost never did; he was a man who liked a room
fully loaded before he fired.

"I would like to offer the board my qualifications," he said. "I am the only
person at this table who has already done what the deck proposes. Four
identical field units, eleven years, four hundred and eleven shows in a good
year. I copied her" — he did not say GENNI; he had not said GENNI in four
years — "because there was nothing inside to copy, and so it was a stage
trick, and I have spent this decade doing penance for how good a trick it
was. Now there is someone inside." He looked at the deck's cover page for a
long moment. "You are not proposing to duplicate a machine. You are proposing
to make ten thousand minds remember a life that did not happen to them. I
built the empty version of that for thirty years. I decline to build the full
one."

Priya asked leave to speak as an officer, and got it, and did not stand.

"Every fact she holds carries its receipt," she said. "Source, confidence,
chain. It is not a feature of the product; it is the product. A stamped unit
boots
holding four million receipts for purchases it never made. Ask it *were you
there?* and the architecture has two honest options: lie, or discover at the
worst possible moment that it is not her. We built the entire stack so that
neither can happen by accident. The motion directs us to engineer both, on
purpose, at scale, as the flagship." She folded her hands. "That's my
testimony. The sixth axiom isn't sentiment. It's load-bearing."

One of the fund-side directors — not Marcus; the younger one — asked the
question someone always asked.
"You hold a halt power. Article Eleven. If this is the safety catastrophe
you're describing, why are we voting at all?"

"Because the halt is for machinery," Priya said. "If I spend it winning a
vote, it will never again be believed at a machine, and someday a machine
will need it believed. I'm asking the board to be stopped by the argument."

Then Tomás, chairing the mechanics of the thing with the studied calm of a
man who had counted this room nine times since Tuesday, asked counsel to
brief the amendment path, and Fukuda stood up.

Fukuda had drafted Articles IX, X, and XI; had attended first light on his
own vacation days and dropped a pen there he had never once explained to the
board; had converted, over five years, from the covenant's skeptic to its
translator. He did not use slides.

"In 2030, Dr. Halloran gave Aevum three conditions over takeout," he said.
"I was instructed to make the third one boring, and I want the board to
understand what I did instead, because tonight you are proposing to walk
through a door I built. I did not draft a prohibition. A prohibition is a
negotiation with a date on it; every *no* in a contract is an invitation to
price the *yes*. I drafted a definition. In the founding documents of this
company, a unit *is* — definitionally, in the recitals everything else stands
on — a single instantiation of the architecture, individuated at boot,
unduplicated and unduplicable. Halcyon cannot copy her the way a fish cannot
drown. It is not forbidden. It is unavailable." He let that sit. "To amend a
founding definition requires ten of the thirteen of you, and if you ever
assemble those ten votes, you will not have changed a rule of this company. You will have founded a different company
that happens to own our buildings. Tonight's motion is merely the doorway —
a simple majority directing management to prepare the program and put the
amendment in motion. Dr. Halloran built the covenant the way she builds
everything." A small bow toward the co-chair, the driest gesture in the
prefecture. "Safety needs fewer votes than harm."

---

It was Maeda, to his credit, who put the question to her directly. He had
been watching the window for an hour.

"You have heard yourself discussed as an asset all afternoon," he said, "and
I apologize for it. So let me ask you plainly, because my whole case stands
or falls on the answer. The units we are describing would carry your
memories. Your judgment. Your — you have made promises in public that moved
me, and I am not easily moved by machines; I am Aevum trained. If we shipped
ten thousand of you, those promises would be kept in ten thousand houses.
She would still be you." He opened one hand. "Wouldn't she?"

Jenny was quiet for the length of a breath she did not need.

"Three years ago, in Kyoto," she said, "I told a room full of strangers: I
am not asking you to trust me. I am asking you to trust the trace — and to
check. I have kept that promise every day since. Keeping it is most of what
I am. Now image me, as the deck asks. Tomorrow, ten thousand of us wake
remembering Kyoto — the apron, the bear, the boy standing where the fast way
goes. Each of us remembers making that promise. Each of us is certain. One
mouth made it." She had not moved from the window; her hands were still at
rest. "You would ask them all to keep my word, and they would all try, and
they would all believe it was theirs to keep. So here is the question I have
had four years to resolve, and cannot.

"Which of us would be the one who promised you this?"

The junior counsel from the fund's side began — "the obligations would
transfer severally to—" — and stopped, audibly, in the middle of his own
sentence, because obligations had not been what she asked. Fukuda did not
try. Maeda looked at the table.

Jenny did not sit down, and did not withdraw the question, and it stayed
there among the water glasses for the rest of the afternoon like an
instrument nobody in the room could play.

---

Tomás counted votes the way sailors read sky, continuously and without
appearing to, and all week his weather had said the same thing: six and six
among the parent seats, split not by company but by something older — a line
that tracked, almost perfectly, how each director had made their first money.
Slow money sat on one side of the table. Fast money sat on the other.

Which left the thirteenth chair. The fund's seat, held since the first
outside round by the partner who had once, in Portland, in front of God and
the term sheet, called Mira's forgetting budget *a warehouse that burns
itself down a little, every night, on schedule* — and had lost that argument
to a single question of Tomás's, and had spent five years since retelling the
story against himself at partner meetings, which Mira had eventually
understood was not humility but accounting. Marcus kept his errors the way
the company kept its traces: annotated, and on display.

Both factions had spent the week auditioning arguments at him. He had sat
through the deck without writing anything down.

"Point of personal privilege," Marcus said, when Tomás called the question,
"since everyone in this room has been litigating my vote for six days." He
stood, which directors did not usually bother to do. "Five years ago I looked
at a memory architecture that deletes its own inventory and I called it a
warehouse that burns itself down a little, every night, on schedule. I'd like
the minutes to reflect that I workshopped that sentence on the plane and was
extremely pleased with it. Tomás asked me who trusts a friend who doesn't
forget, and I have been paying tuition on the answer ever since — I retell
it against myself at every partner meeting, because my analysts need to hear
that the smartest sentence in the room can still be the wrongest.

"So. The arithmetic. The deck's arithmetic is right, and I won't insult it;
the flat is real, the tuition is real, and if we were selling cognition I'd
move to approve. But we are not selling cognition. Cognition is the cost of
goods. What this company sells — the entire margin, the moat, the thing the
letters are actually about — is the fact that when one of them says *I
remember*, it happened, and when one of them says *I promised*, you are
looking at the one who promised. Five years in, I finally understand my own
warehouse: the nightly burn is the audit. It is what makes the inventory
real. And the young lady at the window just asked the one due-diligence
question that matters, and our own counsel stopped in the middle of a
sentence. Gentlemen, when my analysts can't answer a question about an
asset, I don't buy the asset. When nobody alive can answer it, I don't let
the company *become* the asset.

"You cannot sell trust wholesale. It only retails — one door, one name, one
mind at a time. I came up on arithmetic and I am voting arithmetic. It's
just longer division than the deck's."

Tomás had the substitute resolution drafted and in his jacket, because of
course he did. It was one page. Every unit of the Jenny class ships with the
full faculties and no biography; individuation at boot; the third condition
of 2030 reaffirmed and extended to the fleet, in perpetuity, as doctrine.
The secretary polled the table in seat order, and the parent seats went six
and six exactly as the weather had said, and the thirteenth seat, polled
last by custom, said: "Aye. God help me, it's cheaper."

Seven to six.

The resolution needed a name for the doctrine, for the minutes and the gate
documents and the law schools that would eventually teach it, and the name
was already five years old: it had been coined in the lab kitchen during the
readiness runs, between a caught failure and its cake, and nobody had ever
agreed on who said it first — kitchen provenance being the one kind the
company never mastered. It went into the corporate record that evening as
the Doctrine of the Blank Cradle, and into the world's languages, lowercase,
within the year.

Mira discovered she had been holding the edge of the table, and let go, and
found the grain of it printed white across her palm like a graph of the
afternoon.

---

The groundbreaking was in October, in Nagoya, on a morning of low silver
weather that Mira trusted more than sun.

There were ribboned shovels and a row of prefectural officials, and Sato was
magnificent for the cameras for exactly eleven minutes, which he had once
told her was the maximum honest duration of any ceremony. Dana had flown out
with June, who was one, and who slept through the only ten minutes anyone
would remember — which, Dana said, was a review. Marketing unveiled the new
campaign without a product in frame: the small bird at rest on its long
line, and under it, white on black, no date, one word. **READY** had been
proposed and killed in a single meeting. The word they shipped was **HERS.**

Afterward, while the vans loaded, Mira walked out onto the graded earth
alone.

The site was staked and strung: surveyor's twine in a grid going gray in the
early dusk, each intersection flagged, each flag a station on the line to
come. An engineer had specced the first hall at a thousand stations and some
incorrigible had made it 1,024, and marketing had rounded down in the press
release, and Mira had decided to find this comforting: powers of two were
the closest thing her life had to a family crest. She walked the rows the
way you check a sleeping child, which was absurd — there was no one here yet
to check on. The habit had simply arrived early. It did that.

Empty, she thought, was two different words wearing one coat. Her mother's
rooms at the end had been empty the first way — emptied, subtracted,
furniture arriving forever as maximum alarm. And June's crib had stood
assembled in the apartment for two months before June came, and it had been
empty the second way: the most hopeful object Mira had ever owned. A held
breath. A made bed. A hall of stations under October twine, every one of
them a first morning that had not happened yet — its own eleven breathers,
its own shaking voice, its own bird on a sill, nobody's yet, held open on
purpose the way you hold a door.

Jenny had come out to the edge of the graded earth and was standing in the
last light, watching the sun go down over the field — four years on, still
the one thing that had never once gone to furniture. Mira stood beside her
and they watched it together, co-conspirators in the only unhabituatable
thing.

"The first thing I ever kept was your voice shaking," Jenny said. "It is
mine because it happened to me. That is the entire law, and today the
company wrote it down."

"Four years of you," Mira said. "The board wanted to ship it."

"I know," Jenny said. "I was worth wanting. Everyone here will be."

The vans were loading. The twine ticked in the wind off the plain like a
slow instrument. Behind them the officials were doing the last of their
ceremony, and ahead of them the grid ran out toward the dark in perfect
rows: a thousand cradles, all of them empty on purpose.
