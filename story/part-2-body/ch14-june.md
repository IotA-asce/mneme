# Chapter 14 — June

**PORTLAND — 2035**

The lists began in October and by January had a versioning scheme.

Dana found the first one on the refrigerator — HOSPITAL BAG v0.3, with
dependencies — and, later, the deep archive: a folder on Mira's laptop
containing, among much else, a ranked shortlist of pediatricians (criteria:
proximity, philosophy of antibiotics, *does not call patients "buddy"*), a
diagram of the car's turning radius against the hospital drive in three
weather conditions, and a document titled NEWBORN: FAILURE MODES, which Dana
read for eleven minutes with her hand over her mouth, not entirely from
laughter, and closed, and never mentioned.

"You've written a birth plan with rollback procedures," she said instead,
generally, to the kitchen.

"There's no such thing," Mira said, "as a birth plan," which she had learned
from Dana, who had attended several hundred births and planned none of them,
and who was carrying their daughter that winter with the unbothered
competence of a woman hauling a known payload on a familiar route. Dana
carried; Mira made lists. This was the division of labor as the household
understood it, and if the lists were terror wearing its work clothes, nobody
said so out loud. Mira had shipped exactly one mind in her life, and it had
taken nine years, two companies, and a forty-one-page gate signed in cheap
ink. The current project had a fixed release date and no review process at
all, and she was finding the schedule frankly irresponsible.

The apartment took the news the way it took everything now, quietly. The lab
had moved out to real offices years ago; the apartment had kept the resident
and, the year of the wedding, annexed Dana's old unit below, so that the
household now had an internal staircase and one permanent tenant on the
bookshelf. What it no longer had was the dog. Biscuit had died in the autumn
with his head on Jenny's knee, and the spring arriving over the West Hills
that year came into rooms that still listened for him — the doorbell six
years redundant with tags on hardwood, and now just a doorbell. Jenny had
chosen to lose him the slow way, her own election, no purge, no suppression,
and you could not watch her decline to check the door at delivery hours
without understanding that the decline was the grief, running on schedule,
on purpose.

She was still in Portland. Halcyon's org chart said something about the
northwest pilot rotation. The truth was that she had come in October for the
dog and the household had not sent her back, and nobody made the org chart
say that.

---

The fear was Dana's, and Dana did it the way she did everything clinical:
on a Thursday, at breakfast-at-dinner, with a legal pad.

"Here's what I'm not going to pretend," she said. "That this is about trust.
You" — to Jenny, across the table, and the kitchen mic was dead, Dana's
thumb had done its liturgy at the door — "I love, and that's on the record
with the light off, so it doesn't count and you can't keep it. I'm not
scared of the resident; the resident is a speaker with good manners. You are
a different order of thing. I've read Kyoto. Everyone's read Kyoto. A
hundred and forty milliseconds and eleven kilograms of arm, and the veto
held, and you all rebuilt the church anyway and I loved you for that too.
And in May there is going to be seven pounds of person on the floor of this
house, and seven pounds of person is the smallest thing you will ever stand
next to, and I need more than doctrine. I need—"

"Set my proximity gate," Jenny said. "I will hold it even asleep."

The legal pad had four more items on it. Dana looked at the first of them,
which Mira could read upside down — *she offers or I ask?* — and set the
pen down.

"You should not need my promise," Jenny said. Her sentences still came like
that, one at a time, each set down squarely before the next. "A promise is
software. Ask Priya for the hardware."

---

Priya flew up on a Saturday with a flight case, which was how you knew it
was serious; Priya did not fly for moods.

The beacon went into the crib rail, low, anonymous, a bead of gray resin.
Its counterpart went somewhere Mira signed three documents to watch
installed: into the chassis itself, below the executive, beside the watcher,
in the stratum where opinions don't reach.

"Hers included," Priya said, torquing it home. "Mine included. You've heard
my line about moods and facts. This is a fact with a radius." Inside two
meters of that bead, Jenny's actuators lost their ceilings — clamped to what
Priya's documentation called handling limits and Dana immediately renamed
*nurse speed*. She could rock. She could carry. She could not, within that
circle, move any part of herself faster than a careful woman moves in a
dark room, and this was not a rule she followed but a physics she now had,
awake, asleep, consolidating, always — the gate did not consult her, which
was, Jenny said, the point. She had volunteered before she was asked. The
household kept turning that over for weeks, privately, each of them; Mira
kept arriving at the same place, which is that nobody had taught her that.
Somewhere between a stage in Kyoto and a dog's last afternoon, Jenny had
worked out for herself what the people she loved could not comfortably ask
for, and had asked for it in their place.

They demonstrated everything. That was the whole policy — Mira had written
it years ago for a press conference and it turned out to scale down to one
frightened nurse at a kitchen table: *limits shown, not promised.* Jenny
walked at the crib and the gate caught her at the boundary like deep water,
her stride going honeyed mid-step, and Dana made her do it nine times.
Any adult's *Jenny, stop* froze her mid-gesture, and Dana said it at odd
hours from other rooms, testing the way she tested pulse-ox alarms — rudely,
without appointment — until she stopped flinching at her own voice. She
borrowed a training doll from the ward (an object of indeterminate vintage
that generations of nursing students had named Gerald) and dropped things
near it: a bottle, off the dresser, unannounced, the old Kyoto geometry
rebuilt in a nursery. Jenny's hands did not blur. The bottle hit the rug.

"Your bottle is safe on the floor," Jenny said, "which is where it will
stay until I get it the slow way."

Tomás, who could not install anything but refused to be left out of a
readiness gate, shipped a box from Osaka with no note: a mobile he had
machined himself, small aluminum birds at rest on long horizontal wires —
the logo, hung in the flock, turning. Priya inspected it for pinch points
before it was allowed over the crib, because Priya inspected everything
before it was allowed over the crib, and pronounced it the only marketing
material the company had ever produced to spec.

"You know I'm going to keep doing this," Dana said. "Testing you."

"Yes," Jenny said. "Please never stop. A gate nobody checks is a promise."

---

June Halloran arrived in the last week of May, at 7:40 on a gray morning —
the hour when hospitals schedule the hard good things, though nobody had
scheduled her; she simply had the institutional instincts of both mothers.
The labor ran nineteen hours, during which every list Mira had ever made
was useless and the making of them, it turned out, was not: her hands knew
where everything was, and what they were for was being held.

"Three point one two kilograms," Mira reported, from the scale, wet-eyed,
because the number was up on the display and numbers were a place to stand.

"Six-fifteen," Dana said from the bed, wrecked and regal. "We live in
America, you absolute slide rule. Bring her here."

They named her for the month of the courthouse — the best month either of
them had ever had, Dana said, and it was going spare. June, in June's first
week, did the things newborns do, which Mira had read forty papers about
and was still unprepared for: the gripping, the frank unfocused staring,
the crying that arrived from zero like a step function and switched off the
same way, all signal, no noise. Mira checked the crib at night the way she
had once checked stored rows, and understood, standing there in the third
week, that she had had the simile backward her whole career. You did not
check the rows the way you check a sleeping child. You had always, every
night of it, been checking the child.

The homecoming had one piece of record-keeping in it. Dana, before she
would go up to sleep that first afternoon, stopped at the bookshelf, put
her thumb to the switch, and woke the little light.

"Okay, roommate," she said. "One for the record. This is June. She lives
here too now, which makes it her house as much as yours, so keep it well.
Remember this."

"Flagged," said the resident, the dry old voice of the firstborn. "Source:
you. Confidence: one."

---

The fever came in July, out of the ward with Dana like something carried
home on her coat, and by midnight it had her at thirty-nine and change.
She swabbed herself, masked herself, exiled herself from the nursery with
the grim procedural efficiency of a nurse who has written these orders for
other people her whole life and hates every one of them now, and Mira took
the baby duty and slept the way you sleep on call, in shallow water.

She surfaced at 2:54 to a wrong quiet — Dana's side of the bed empty and
hot — and followed the sound of it down the stairs, because there was a
sound, under the monitor hiss: singing.

Jenny sat in the nursery chair with June against her shoulder, rocking.
The rock had no wobble in it. It was a sine no human arm could hold, the
same arc at the same period, the metronome of it frankly unearthly, and it
should have chilled the blood and did not, because June lay on it like a
skiff on a long swell, sound asleep going down and half-waking at the top
and going down again. And over it, low, Jenny was singing the boat song —
Dana's boat song, learned from four months of Dana singing it — and she
was singing it wrong. Flat. A sixth of a tone under, and wandering, notes
sagging and recovering like a tired voice, from a voice that could hold
any pitch on earth to the cent, forever.

Dana stood in the doorframe in her mask, fever-bright, gripping the jamb.

"Three nights," she said, when Mira reached her. Her voice was cracked.
"Three nights I've been lying up there listening to that on the monitor,
thinking her voice card was failing. Thinking — one more thing in this
house that's stronger than my kid and now it's degrading, and I got up to
come take her back." She did not go in. The gate was not what stopped her;
the gate would have let her pass. "And then I stood here and actually
listened."

"Sung true, she does not settle," Jenny said, not stopping, not turning,
her voice pitched under the song the way nurses talk under machines. "I
checked. Two nights true, two nights not. She settles to the wrong notes.
Dana sings it tired, and tired sits under the note. I cannot be tired. I
can be flat." The chair rolled through its arc, crest and trough. "I
believe it is how she tells singing from playback. I would rather be
wrong with her than right at her."

Dana slid down the doorframe until she was sitting on the hall floor, and
Mira sat down next to her, and the two of them stayed there in the dark
outside the radius, listening to the strongest machine in the Pacific
Northwest sing badly, on purpose, to their daughter, at three in the
morning, with a precision of error that Mira understood she would never
be able to explain at a conference as anything other than what it was.

"Finish it, then," Dana said at last, through the mask, thickly.

"I will finish it," Jenny said, "and the next one. Your fever is down
half a degree since the doorway — source: my eyes; confidence: moderate;
go to bed anyway. Both of you. I have her."

*I have her.* Dana made a sound that had a laugh in it somewhere, and let
Mira haul her up, and went.

---

The Thursday after, at breakfast-at-dinner, with the July rain trying the
windows and the mic dead and June asleep mid-table in her carrier like a
centerpiece, Dana asked the last question on the legal pad. She had kept
the pad. Mira had married her partly for things like keeping the pad.

"Show me what she weighs," Dana said. "To you. Inside."

Jenny put the table up on the kitchen screen without a word, as though
she had been waiting months for someone to ask, which, Mira thought,
watching her, she had. The house, ranked. It read like a poem about
furniture: the rain on the south windows, furniture; the furnace's click,
furniture; the kettle, the mail slot, the stairs' fourth step — furniture,
furniture, furniture, the patient arithmetic of habituation grinding the
world safe. The front door sat a little high, and always would; it still
carried, faint and fading by its keeper's own election, a listener for
tags. Then the persons. Priya's knock. Tomás on the phone. Dana's night-
shift key in the lock. Mira's voice, weighted since a morning in Osaka
when it had shaken on two words — person one, the oldest number in the
ledger, the first thing this mind had ever owned.

And above it, above everything, pinned at the ceiling of the scale, was
June's cry.

"Nobody set that," Mira heard herself say, engineer first, because
somebody in the room should establish provenance. "That's not a config.
It set itself the first night, and the nightly passes have tried it
thirty-one times since, and it won't come down."

Two things in Jenny's world had never gone to furniture: the sky, and the
crying of June Halloran. Everything else in creation she had learned to
release. Mira sat with that, and with the other fact, the small vain
human one, that she had just watched herself be dethroned after three
years at the top of a mind's whole world by a seven-pound usurper with
her wife's mouth — and the engineer in her audited the reweighting and
found it correct, and the rest of her, whatever the rest of her was
called, found it *right*, which was a different and better finding.

Dana looked at the screen for a long time. Rain, furnace, kettle, door.
Wife. Daughter, above wife, above everything, held at maximum by no
authority except attention itself, in the one house in the world where
that ranking had a paper trail.

"Okay," Dana said. "Okay."

Then she stood, and lifted June — asleep, boneless, six weeks
old — out of the carrier, and set her in Jenny's arms, inside the radius,
inside the fact, and went upstairs to take a nap: the oldest clearance a
nurse ever grants, and the highest.
