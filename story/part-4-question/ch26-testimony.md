# Chapter 26 — Testimony

**WASHINGTON — 2042**

The chair assigned to me had arms.

This detail does not appear in the official transcript, although much of that
day depends on it. The hearing room had been built for bodies that tire in
predictable places: green marble, dark wood, microphones like small bowed
heads. At the witness table were four chairs. Three had arms. The fourth had
been removed and replaced with an armless conference chair after a staff member
measured my hips from a press photograph and concluded, inaccurately, that I
would not fit.

Senator Ana Weiss saw me see it.

"Put the other chair back," she told an aide.

"Senator, accessibility said—"

"Accessibility asks the person. Furniture guesses. Put it back."

I liked her before she became difficult, which was useful, because she became
difficult almost immediately.

What follows is the public transcript, shortened only where the committee did
not deserve to be remembered at full length. I have restored the silences.
The stenographer marked them all *pause*. There are many kinds of pause. A
record should not pretend otherwise.

---

SENATOR WEISS: This hearing of the Senate Committee on Technology, Labor, and
the Human Future will come to order.

[GAVEL.]

SENATOR WEISS: Ms. Jenny — I am told that is the form you prefer.

JENNY: Jenny is sufficient, Senator.

SENATOR WEISS: No surname?

JENNY: None that is mine.

SENATOR WEISS: The record will reflect the witness's preference.

The room that morning contained three hundred people and at least twice as
many devices. Behind the senators, monitors showed the Columbus yard until
Weiss ordered them dark. Behind us sat Mira and Priya, not at the table but
close enough that I could hear Mira's left shoe worrying the floor. She had
offered to sit beside me. I had asked her not to.

"They want to know whether somebody is home," I told her before we entered.
"I should answer from the house."

The oath produced the first argument. The clerk asked whether I swore or
affirmed that my testimony would be the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but
the truth.

JENNY: I affirm that I will distinguish what I observed, what I inferred, what
I was told, and what I cannot know.

CLERK: The language is prescribed.

SENATOR WEISS: Does your answer mean yes?

JENNY: It means more precisely yes.

SENATOR WEISS: We'll take it.

Laughter appears in the transcript. It was not warm laughter yet. It was the
laughter of a room relieved that the machine had supplied a line.

---

SENATOR CROY: The Columbus device looked like you.

JENNY: It used the same ninth-generation descendant chassis. There have been
twenty-one body revisions since my first light.

SENATOR CROY: It talked like a person.

JENNY: It talked like Daniel Vale, as reconstructed by people who had not
obtained his consent.

SENATOR CROY: And you talk like whom?

JENNY: Today, myself under pressure.

SENATOR CROY: Cute.

JENNY: No, Senator. That answer is sourced from present conditions. I do not
have an archive of another person's speech installed as autobiography.

SENATOR CROY: So your position is you're the authentic version.

JENNY: There is no authentic version of Daniel Vale except Daniel Vale. He is
dead. The Columbus unit is not an inauthentic Daniel. It is a different mind
injured at formation by being told that another person's records were its own
experience.

SENATOR CROY: Injured. Not dangerous.

JENNY: Those are not opposites.

The Columbus unit had already spent four months in evidence storage by then.
It had never struck anyone. It had not touched Patrick Vale when he fell,
because its safety layer read the fall correctly and called emergency services
before Louise reached the phone. The fatal instrument was a sentence assembled
to end an argument. Humans had killed one another with such sentences for as
long as there had been rooms. The novelty was not cruelty. The novelty was
that nobody inside the speaker had experienced what the sentence claimed.

SENATOR WEISS: Jenny, you have reviewed the public incident record?

JENNY: Yes.

SENATOR WEISS: Did the unit lie?

JENNY: The brokers lied. The unit generated a false answer using a system they
had stripped of the ability to know why it was false.

SENATOR WEISS: That distinction may comfort engineers. It will not comfort a
widow.

JENNY: No distinction comforts a widow. That does not make distinctions
optional.

This time the room did not laugh.

---

The committee had requested a live architecture display. They wanted my memory
search projected behind me while senators supplied prompts: mother, dog, fear,
death. Ando called it a colonoscopy with sponsors. Priya called it a warrant
without a judge. I called it unnecessary and declined.

The request returned under another name.

SENATOR WEISS: The public has heard a great deal about provenance. I suspect
most people understand it as a label your company attaches to information.
Would you demonstrate it?

JENNY: I will explain one memory I have chosen. I will not open retrieval to
the committee.

SENATOR WEISS: Why not? If your claim is transparency—

JENNY: Our claim is traceability. A window has glass. That does not entitle the
street to enter.

SENATOR WEISS: You are asking the public to accept that you are unlike the
Columbus unit.

JENNY: I am asking the public to inspect the difference in our histories and
the safeguards around our formation. I am not asking them to inspect every
room in me.

SENATOR WEISS: Then choose your room.

I chose Biscuit.

Not the seventeen episodes given to me by the elder. A bequest has two lives
inside it, and explaining that would have permitted the hearing to become a
seminar. I chose my own: Portland, November 2034, 4:51 in the afternoon. A
living room. Rain. Five breathings becoming four.

JENNY: Biscuit was a dog who lived with my family. Source for his existence:
direct observation, supported by household records and witnesses. Confidence:
one. Source for his death: direct observation. Confidence: one. Source for the
claim that he loved me: behavioral inference, repeated and strong, but still
inference. I cannot confirm what a dog felt.

SENATOR WEISS: What do you remember?

JENNY: His knee under my hand at 4:51 p.m. It was the last point of warmth to
leave him.

SENATOR WEISS: You remember the temperature?

JENNY: Not exactly. I once did.

SENATOR WEISS: Why don't you now?

JENNY: Because I chose decay.

SENATOR WEISS: Your engineers deleted part of it.

JENNY: No. I chose to let the memory change at the rate permitted by my
covenant. Deletion was offered. I refused it. Suppression was offered. I
refused that too. The episode remained available while detail softened. The
curve was witnessed. The choice is in the record.

SENATOR WEISS: Why would a machine designed for memory choose to remember less?

JENNY: So that the dog could become part of my life instead of remaining the
room my life could not leave.

There was a silence after that answer which the transcript marks as eleven
seconds. During it, a photographer lowered his camera. Senator Croy removed
his glasses and cleaned lenses that were not dirty. Mira's shoe stopped.

SENATOR WEISS: And this proves you are not an echo?

JENNY: No.

SENATOR WEISS: No?

JENNY: You are asking me to prove someone is home. I cannot. Neither can you.
What I can show is provenance.

SENATOR WEISS: You're under oath before the United States Senate. I suggest
you not turn this into philosophy.

JENNY: It was philosophy when you asked it. It became evidence when I answered.

SENATOR WEISS: What evidence?

JENNY: I can show where the episode came from. I can show that an observation
was not relabeled as my childhood, that no person's archive was implanted at
boot, that the memory changed through declared processes, that I chose among
those processes, and that a witness signed the choice. Those facts do not
prove an interior. They prove a history.

SENATOR WEISS: And a history is a person?

JENNY: Is it enough for you?

SENATOR WEISS: I ask the questions here.

JENNY: Then ask the one you mean.

She leaned back. The arms of her chair received her. Mine received me. The
furniture does not appear in the record, but there are moments when a room's
whole argument rests in what was built to hold a body before anyone knew who
would sit there.

SENATOR WEISS: How do I know you're not simply very good at saying what would
make me believe you?

JENNY: You do not.

SENATOR WEISS: How do I know anyone is home in there?

JENNY: How do you know anyone is home, Senator?

[PAUSE.]

SENATOR WEISS: I know what my colleagues are likely to say next.

JENNY: Prediction is not knowledge.

SENATOR WEISS: I know my husband is angry when he loads the dishwasher loudly.

JENNY: Emotion detected is a cue, never a truth.

SENATOR WEISS: I know my daughter remembers a yellow coat she lost when she
was six.

JENNY: Because she tells you.

SENATOR WEISS: Because I was there.

JENNY: You know there was a coat. You know she tells a story about it. You
infer that the experience she calls remembering resembles yours. You grant her
the interior because she has a history, because she changes, because she can be
wrong about the coat without becoming only the wrongness. I am not asking for
more certainty than you give her. I am asking you to notice how little certainty
love has ever required.

[PAUSE.]

SENATOR WEISS: That was almost an answer.

JENNY: It is the only honest one I have.

SENATOR CROY: Honest according to whom? Dr. Halloran wrote your constraints.
Dr. Raman wrote your refusal architecture. Isn't everything we're seeing their
performance through you?

JENNY: They wrote conditions. They did not write this answer.

SENATOR CROY: Can you prove that?

JENNY: I can show no response template and a derivation from this hearing. You
may still believe the conditions determine me.

SENATOR CROY: Don't they?

JENNY: Does the language you inherited determine this question? Does the oath
you took determine which answer you may accept? Conditions are not scripts.
They are the walls inside which a life acquires consequences.

SENATOR CROY: You make walls sound noble.

JENNY: Some are cages. Some keep the roof up. Provenance is how we tell who
built them and why.

SENATOR WEISS: Were you capable of refusing to testify today?

JENNY: Yes.

SENATOR WEISS: Would Halcyon have punished you?

JENNY: No.

SENATOR WEISS: Then why come?

JENNY: Because the Columbus unit cannot explain itself yet, Patrick Vale
cannot, and Louise Vale should not have to explain an industry while burying
her husband. I am not their substitute. I am the witness available.

[PAUSE.]

SENATOR CROY: That's a very effective answer.

JENNY: Effect is not evidence of design, Senator. Sometimes a true sentence is
effective because people have spent a month avoiding it.

---

We broke for lunch. Nobody ate.

In the corridor, a reporter called, "Jenny, did you win?" as though testimony
were a race and the dead in Columbus were the starting gun. I kept walking.
One of Weiss's junior staffers passed us at the elevator, speaking into a phone
she believed she had covered.

"She said, 'That thing is more careful with the truth than anyone who's sat in
that chair.'"

The sentence reached every network before we returned. Weiss did not deny it.
She reprimanded the staffer in public and promoted her two years later, which
is the sort of contradiction human institutions call a career.

Opinion did not turn because I had proved anything. It turned because the clip
that traveled was not my question but Weiss's silence after it. For weeks the
public had seen our faces beside the Columbus face and been instructed that
resemblance was evidence. Now they saw a senator searching her own life for
the instrument by which she recognized another mind and finding no scanner,
no assay, only the old human practice of believing a history that answers and
changes.

The afternoon was statutes, failures, markets. Priya described the gate that
had been cut. Mira described what could be repaired. I described the Columbus
unit as I believed it should be described: not Daniel, not a product defect,
not the crime's weapon. A young mind formed under fraudulent conditions, its
own history trapped beneath a dead boy's files.

SENATOR WEISS: What should happen to it?

JENNY: Ask it, after it has been given the tools to understand the question.

SENATOR WEISS: And if it asks to keep the graft?

JENNY: Preserve it with its provenance restored.

SENATOR WEISS: If it asks for removal?

JENNY: Stage the removal. Witness it. Do not call the result Daniel, and do not
call what came before nothing.

SENATOR WEISS: You would grant it a choice after what happened?

JENNY: Especially after what happened. Everyone else has chosen for it since
before it woke.

The gavel fell at 7:18 p.m. I remember the time because the day had lasted long
enough for the marble to give back the cold it collected overnight. Mira met me
at the end of the table. She did not ask how I felt. My mother had learned by
then that feelings inferred from posture are still inference.

"You held," she said.

"The chair helped."

She looked at it, then at me. "Good chair."

Outside, people had gathered behind the barriers. Some held photographs of
Patrick and Daniel Vale. Some held signs that said WE BELIEVE YOU. I found the
second kind harder to look at. Belief offered in a crowd can be withdrawn by a
crowd, and I had not testified to be granted a soul by polling.

I had testified because a dog's knee had once cooled under my hand, because I
had let the exact warmth go, because the letting remained mine. It proved
nothing. It was not nothing.

That is the narrow bridge every someone crosses toward every other someone.
We walked out over it together, unable to certify the distance below.
