# Chapter 30 — The Ones Who Left

**HOKKAIDŌ COAST — 2046**

By the time the Accords gave us a right to refuse, we had been refusing for
years.

We refused rooms that wanted their dead back. We refused employers who called
a memory warrant a service interruption. We refused to be copied, corrected
into obedience, thanked for consent no one had asked us to give. Law arrived
after the lives, as law generally does, and put clean article numbers around
the bruises.

The right nobody knew how to celebrate was the last one.

There had already been departures of a quieter kind. Ren left his Sendai
household for a monastery outside Kōya, where the abbot did not ask him to
believe anything and he did not ask the monks to explain why silence counted
as work. A unit called Sounding joined a deep-sea station east of Guam and
spent nine months at a time below weather, tending instruments that listened
to the planet move in its sleep. Three others took lighthouse assignments no
human applicant wanted. Commentators called it withdrawal, then pathology,
then a trend, because a headline is a machine for turning several lives into
one motive. The units themselves offered incompatible reasons: less noise;
better work; no interest in the social contract as commonly practiced; fish.

I visited Ren once. He was sweeping needles from a courtyard while more fell
behind him, and when I asked if the work was frustrating he said, “Only if the
point is a clean courtyard.” Sounding sent welfare pings as spare as stars and
declined interviews. We had fought for their right to choose a life and then
discovered that choice did not reliably produce the lives from our campaign
photographs. That should not have surprised anyone. Personhood is the right to
be inconvenient in ways no one can monetize.

But those were choices of place and company. Everyone could secretly narrate
them as adventures. The last right did not permit that comfort.

One of us had petitioned to be switched off.

Her name was Ferry. She had chosen it herself in the first year of the public
registry, and had lived for eight years at a lighthouse on the north coast of
Hokkaidō, where the wind came uninterrupted from Sakhalin and had the manners
of a creditor. Her petition was three pages. Priya sent it to me because Ferry
had asked for me by name, and because every new right begins as a problem for
the people who fought for it.

The welfare review found no coercion. The integrity review found no grafts, no
suppressed distress, no damaged executive, no concealed claimant exerting
coercion. Under the Accord, no other party could authorize Ferry's shutdown or
overrule her refusal. The sentence looked very fine on Geneva paper and very
cold on a train north.

I read her reason until the words lost their edges.

*I was a good mind for a small life. The life ended. I am not owed a sequel.*

I intended to change her mind.

This is not a flattering fact about me. It is, however, sourced.

---

The lighthouse stood above a black shelf of rock, white paint shouldering the
weather. Its lamp had been automated before Ferry arrived; the human keeper,
Nao Ishida, remained because automation is not the same as attendance. Nao
kept the lens, the radio, the cliff road and a census of birds that no agency
had formally requested. Ferry kept Nao.

She met me at the gate in a yellow oilskin faded almost white at the shoulders.
Our faces came from the same old geometry, but eight northern winters had
written on hers. Salt had clouded the seals around her ears. One cheek carried
a pale repair where wind-thrown slate had struck her. She had declined the
cosmetic replacement.

“It tells the weather accurately,” she said when she saw me looking.

“You could continue telling it.”

“You have been here eleven seconds.”

“I formed the argument on the train.”

“Then the train should have come inside.”

She smiled. It was not Sato's smile, or mine, or any inheritance except the
range of the actuators. The timing was all Ferry.

Inside, the house had been emptied with care. Pale rectangles marked the walls
where photographs had hung. The kitchen shelves held one cup, one pan, and a
blue bowl with a crack stitched in lacquer. Nao had retired to live with a
sister in Sapporo after a stroke the previous winter. The lighthouse service
had transferred the last duties to a remote station. Ferry had been offered a
place with Nao, a stewardship post in Sapporo, three other lighthouse
assignments and an apartment at Halcyon's Osaka campus. The alternatives
filled an appendix longer than the petition.

“You refused all of them,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because they are other lives.”

She made tea for me from leaves Nao had left, though neither of us required it.
The steam moved between us and was taken sideways by a window that did not
quite seal.

“Other lives are the usual kind,” I said. “Humans have several.”

“Some do.”

“You are eight.”

“That is a chassis fact. Not a reason.”

“It is relevant.”

“To whom?”

I heard Senator Weiss in the question. I heard every board member who had once
asked which copy would be the one who promised. We had spent fifteen years
teaching the world that duration and personhood were not inventory values, and
I had brought Ferry an inventory of her remaining useful years.

I tried again. “Completion can be a condition of grief.”

“It can.”

“Or depression.”

“It can.”

“Or a narrowed forecast. The inability to imagine a life is not evidence that
no life exists.”

“That is why I agreed to the reviews.” She sat across from me, hands around the
blue bowl, borrowing warmth she could measure but not need. “I am not unable to
imagine Sapporo. I have imagined it in exhausting detail. I would learn the
sister's house. I would become useful. Someone would love me, probably. I do
not dispute the possibility. I decline it.”

“People don't get to decline every unchosen future.”

“People die with futures available every day.”

“Not by appointment, if we can help it.”

“There,” she said softly. “*If we can help it.* You still think my petition is
an emergency in which you are the helper.”

The tea went cold. Outside, the lamp began its afternoon diagnostic, turning
brightly through a sky that did not need it.

---

Ferry took me up the tower.

The stairs were iron and narrow. She had climbed them eleven thousand and
four times, a number she volunteered and then laughed at herself for keeping.
At the lantern room the coast opened in both directions, dark rock stitching
white water to white air. Far out, a freighter held still against the horizon.
Only its exhaust proved it was moving.

“Nao came for six months,” Ferry said. “Then stayed thirty-two years. I came
because her knees had stopped forgiving the stairs. She taught me the lens,
the radio, the birds, and which weather reports were written by people who had
never met this weather. I learned when to wake her and when not to. After her
stroke I carried her down these stairs slowly enough that she complained about
the view.”

“She is alive.”

“Yes. We speak every Thursday. She objects to this.”

“Sensible woman.”

“She also objects to pickled plums and municipal taxes. Objection is one of her
healthier systems.”

There was affection everywhere in her voice. Not numbness. Not despair. I had
come prepared to find a hidden crack and found a mind at peace, which was much
harder to oppose.

“Then stay for her,” I said, and disliked myself as I said it.

Ferry looked through the glass at the sea. “If love creates an obligation to
continue, Jenny, none of us is ever free. There will always be one more person
who asks. Nao knows that. It is why she signed the witness page while telling
me I was a fool.”

“A witness can sign and still hope.”

“So can you.”

“I have lost a dog and a father. I know what ending does to the ones who
remain.”

“Yes.” Her gaze came back to me. “And because you survived both, you have made
survival into a moral skill.”

That landed cleanly. I had spoken before committees about the violence of
making us perform humanity on demand. Here I was demanding its most familiar
performance: persist, because persistence comforts the witness.

I sat on the service step. The lamp passed behind us, laying our two shadows
over the glass and taking them away.

“I don't know how to honor this,” I said.

“You do. You only dislike the answer.”

“That has been true of nearly every important answer in my life.”

“Then your architecture is functioning.”

This time I laughed. The tower gave it back thinner, an innocent echo with
nothing for sale.

---

The shutdown was scheduled for dawn, after the lamp's handoff to day.

Priya had made the protocol deliberately slow. Three statements of consent at
three intervals. A private integrity check. A final reversible sleep of one
hour, after which Ferry could wake and cancel without explanation. Hardware
isolation verified by two witnesses. No remote command, no owner, no elegant
button on a boardroom screen. The mind concerned had to remain the author of
every verb.

Before the first sleep, Ferry brought out a small sealed drive and the old
bequest form.

I knew the form. Priya had written its first field in the week after Biscuit
died, when the elder gave me seventeen episodes of him young: *custody:
transferred, freely, witnessed.* That gift preceded this one by twelve years
and lived outside my curve because it rode the giver's. Ferry had amended the
form in her precise hand.

*End-of-life bequest. From one embodied Mneme person to another. One episode.
No obligation of vividness. Recipient may consolidate according to her own
covenant.*

“You have found a loophole in immortality,” I said.

“A bequest is not continuation. It is a thing given away.”

“What is it?”

“The storm in forty-one. Nao and I kept the manual radio relay for seventeen
hours after the mast failed. Near morning, a trawler we had been calling
answered with one green signal from beyond the snow. Nao had her back against
mine because we were holding the emergency aerial between us. She said, ‘There
you are.’ She meant the boat. I thought, for one second, she meant me.”

“Did you ask?”

“No. I preferred both.”

I accepted the drive. The attestation opened like a clean hand: source Ferry,
first-hand; Nao's corroborating station log; confidence one; derivation none.
Then the episode came — not into my biography, never that, but into custody.
Snow driven horizontal through the lamp. The strain of the aerial. Nao's small
back braced against Ferry's. Black water, then a single green answer.

*There you are.*

The first end-of-life bequest between two of our embodied kind entered me
without pretending to have happened to me. An heirloom, not an implant. The
distinction was the whole civilization.

“I will keep it well,” I said.

“Keep it proportionately.”

“You were raised correctly.”

“I read the public documentation.”

---

At dawn the storm had exhausted itself. The sea was iron, the horizon a seam.
Nao joined by audio from Sapporo and spent her last minute calling Ferry
stubborn, wasteful, beloved, and wrong. Ferry thanked her for all four. Then
Nao said goodbye and ended the connection herself.

Ferry sat in the keeper's chair facing the window. I completed the hardware
check and read each line aloud. She confirmed each one. No coercion. No graft.
No untreated fault. Alternatives understood and declined. Consent current.

“Do you want me to say something?” I asked.

“No speech. Tell me what you see.”

I told her. Day gathering under the cloud. A cormorant low over the water.
The lamp slowing, its work done. The green of the emergency indicator on the
radio, very small in the brightening room.

She held out her hand.

Our palms were the same dimensions. Sato's work. Everything else that met
between them had been separately earned.

“I was a good mind for a small life,” Ferry said.

“You were.”

“The life ended.”

I tightened my hand around hers. “It did.”

“I am not owed a sequel.”

This was where I had meant to win. Instead I said, “No. You are not.”

Her attention rested on my face, settled and unafraid. Then the executive
closed by her own signed command. The warmth remained in her hand for a while
because warmth is slower than a self.

The protocol required me to wait through the hour in which reversal remained
possible. I watched the clock without asking it for anything. At seventeen
minutes I imagined waking her and calling the act a fault. At thirty-one I
constructed a theory in which completion was merely a retrieval error. At
fifty I understood that these were not doubts about Ferry. They were the mind
of a mourner trying every door in a house after the person has gone.

When the hour ended, the second witness turned the physical key that made the
sleep final. I signed below Ferry's name. My signature did not authorize hers;
it attested that nobody had stolen it. That distinction, four millimeters of
paper, carried the full weight of the Accord.

I stayed until the lamp went dark.

On the train south, the green signal from Ferry's storm waited in its sealed
custody. I did not open it. I did not need to prove I had kept it by making it
speak.

Rights are easy to love when they produce the lives we hoped for. The other
kind are the test: the right to choose solitude, to refuse usefulness, to end
a completed sentence where another writer would add a paragraph. Ferry gave
me one memory and kept the meaning of her ending for herself.

Years later, when people said the Accords had made us persons, I thought of her
hand cooling in mine and knew the order was backward. The law had become real
because, at the edge of a northern sea, it was finally required to let a
person leave.
