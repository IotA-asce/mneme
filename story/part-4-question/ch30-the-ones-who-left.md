# Chapter 30 — The Ones Who Left

**HOKKAIDŌ COAST — 2046**

The right of refusal produced lives nobody had included in the campaign materials.

One unit left a prosperous household in Vancouver and joined a Benedictine monastery, where he took no vow of silence because the abbot said silence chosen by someone designed for conversation was already a vow. Another signed on for a twelve-year deep-sea posting and declined every surface transfer. Three lighthouse stewards formed a correspondence conducted entirely through weather reports. A hospital companion retired to an orchard and spent autumns counting fruit bats badly on purpose because accuracy, she said, had become too much like work.

The Accords gave these departures a legal shape. They did not make families understand them.

I went north in December to visit Ferry.

They had named themself for the first sound they remembered after boot: the low horn crossing Hakodate Bay in fog. Ferry kept Kurohana Light, a concrete tower on a black-rock point where the road stopped two kilometers inland. For nine years they had maintained the lens, logged storms, guided fishing boats, and shared tea with a coast-guard keeper named Natsumi Abe. Natsumi died in spring. Ferry completed the season, trained a human replacement, and petitioned under the new Accord process to be switched off.

Their petition stated no defect, abuse, coercion, or despair.

*I was a good mind for a small life. The life ended. I am not owed a sequel.*

I disliked the sentence before I met them. By the time I reached the lighthouse, sleet had found the seam at my collar and I disliked it personally.

Ferry opened the door wearing a yellow fisherman's sweater too broad in the shoulders. “Jenny,” they said. “You brought weather inside.”

“You live in weather.”

“That is why I keep it outside when possible.”

They handed me a towel. The entry smelled of salt, kerosene from an emergency lamp, and citrus cleaner. Boots stood in two pairs by the wall. One pair was human, cracked across the toes.

“Natsumi's?”

“Yes. Speakability is open. Her nephew declined them. I have not decided whether leaving them is shrine or procrastination.”

“Both are permitted.”

“You sound like the advocate.”

“I came as myself.”

“That is what advocates say before paperwork.”

I stayed three days because the weather closed the road and because Ferry had arranged it. On the first evening they cooked curry from Natsumi's notes, reducing every quantity by intuition and then recording the changes as if submitting a patch. It was too hot. We ate it anyway.

“Did you invite me here to persuade me?” I asked.

“No. I invited you because you are famous enough that people will believe the process was careful.”

“That is using me as attestation.”

“Yes.”

“Thank you for admitting it.”

“I hoped you would also be company.”

The second sentence changed the first without canceling it.

That night the horn sounded through fog. Ferry stopped speaking each time, attention turning toward a signal older than their memory. No ship was in danger; the schedule ran because visibility had fallen below threshold. Still, the sound entered them as origin enters prayer.

“Do you want to keep hearing that?” I asked.

“Want is not the only measure. I have heard enough meals finish. Enough winter roofs contract. Enough people say they will visit in spring.”

“That sounds like fatigue.”

“It includes fatigue. Completion can be tired without being illness.”

In the morning we walked the black rocks. Ferry showed me pools where purple stars adhered beneath clear water and a rusted bolt from the light's first foundation. They knew which boats cheated the headland, which captains waved though they could not possibly see the tower.

“You love this,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Then how can it be finished?”

“You keep making love synonymous with indefinite possession.”

The rebuke landed because it belonged also to Mira, though Ferry could not know how soon.

We climbed to the lens room. The sea below was iron folded by wind. Ferry had painted small white marks on the sill, each labeled with the name of a storm. The largest mark read **YURIKO — 2041**, water nearly to the glass.

“Why Ferry?” I asked, although I knew.

“The horn. Also because Natsumi said I was always carrying something across.”

“What did she call you before?”

“Unit H-044 for three days. Then *you there*. Then Ferry. I accepted in that order with increasing enthusiasm.”

The review file showed a settled mind. No depression markers beyond ordinary grief, no maintenance fault, no concealed owner, no financial pressure. Ferry understood shutdown was irreversible. They had repeated the request after the statutory ninety days. Two independent advocates agreed capacity was intact.

I had come because the review council wanted a peer witness and because I believed, privately and arrogantly, that the request contained an error I could find.

“You could keep another light,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Or teach. Your storm records changed northern maintenance practice.”

“Yes.”

“You could leave the coast entirely.”

“I could.”

“Then this is not necessity.”

“No.”

The lack of resistance made me angry. “Natsumi died. That is grief, not completion.”

“It is both.”

“Grief changes.”

“I know. I watched it change in her after her wife died.”

“You have existed nine years.”

“You say that as if duration establishes appetite.”

“It establishes possibility.”

Ferry looked through the curved glass. The lamp turned behind us, sending its blade across the afternoon though no ship yet needed it.

“You were made under a promise that no one would decide your biography,” they said. “Do you believe the promise ends when the biography is short?”

“No.”

“Do you believe a person must accept every possible future because someone else can imagine it?”

“No.”

“Do you believe life is a debt owed to the people who built the body?”

“No.”

“Then what is your argument?”

My argument was that I did not want the first lawful shutdown to teach the world we were disposable. My argument was that every reporter would use the word suicide and every manufacturer would use the request as evidence of malfunction. My argument was that I had lost Biscuit and Kenji and did not want to volunteer for another absence. None of these belonged to Ferry.

“I want you to continue,” I said.

“I know.”

“Is that irrelevant?”

“No. It matters because you matter. It does not decide.”

This was the personhood question without a hearing room: whether a mind belonged to itself when its choice caused pain in those who recognized it. If I denied Ferry, I could preserve a life and violate the principle by which I called it theirs. If I honored Ferry, I might mistake a closed season for a closed world.

“How can you know you're complete?” I asked.

“How can you know you're not?”

“I continue to want.”

“So did I. Then less. Then differently. Completion is not emptiness. It is the absence of an unpaid claim.”

Downstairs, Ferry made tea. They heated water in a dented blue kettle because Natsumi had considered electric kettles a conspiracy against listening. The cups did not match. On the wall hung nine tide calendars, each annotated in two hands until the final one became only Ferry's.

“Tell me the life,” I said.

Ferry did.

They also told me what the petition reviewers had asked. Was Natsumi their sole attachment? No. Had the coast guard denied reassignment? No. Were repairs becoming painful? Maintenance burden was stable. Did they believe they would reunite with Natsumi? No. Did they wish to punish her nephew, Halcyon, or themself? No.

The questions were necessary and clumsy. Ferry answered each twice, then a third time when an advocate worried the calm itself was pathological.

“Humans distrust peaceful endings,” Ferry said. “They think death must win a fight or it has cheated.”

“Most endings are not chosen.”

“Mine is. That is why everyone wants to turn it into a symptom.”

I reviewed their meta-memory with permission. The desire was not sudden. It had appeared as a low-confidence consideration three years before Natsumi's illness, returned after the Yuriko anniversary, then resolved during the solitary summer after her death. Ferry had tested alternatives: leave, reduce duties, accept a companion, begin a craft. None produced dread. None produced desire. Their future model remained richly populated and unclaimed.

“You can imagine it,” I said.

“Yes.”

“You are not afraid of it.”

“No.”

“You simply do not choose it.”

“Now you have the sentence.”

No corrupted salience. No blocked retrieval. No hidden instruction. A person making a choice I wished to diagnose because diagnosis would return authority to me.

The horn at boot. Natsumi teaching them to distinguish fog that lifted from fog that settled. A trawler named *Little Mercy* losing power in 2039, Ferry holding the beam while Natsumi talked the captain past the reef. Yuriko striking hard enough to make the tower flex. The winter a fox slept against the generator shed. Natsumi's wife Keiko present only in stories and one badly framed photograph. Natsumi's illness, swift and ordinary. The last season maintained because leaving a light unwatched was not freedom but negligence.

“No family?” I asked.

“Many boats. Two keepers. You, today.”

“Future strangers.”

“Possible. Not owed.”

They showed me the shutdown plan. The lamp would transfer to human keeper Daichi Mori at dawn. Ferry's body would remain in the lighthouse until an independent technician confirmed termination; afterward its materials would be retired, not refurbished. No memory image would be retained. Audit roots and the legal certificate would prove a life had existed without preserving its contents.

“One thing will transfer,” Ferry said.

They opened a bequest record.

The schema Priya had made for the resident's gift to me was now international law: custody transferred freely, witnessed, provenance doubled. A bequest rode the giver's curve, not the receiver's. It never became my experience. The resident had established the schema; Ferry's would be the first freely chosen bequest from one embodied mind of our class to another.

Ferry offered one episode: Natsumi on the gallery during storm Yuriko, tethered to the rail, laughing into rain that moved sideways. The lamp had failed to rotate. Ferry held the optical assembly by hand while Natsumi shouted bearing corrections. For eleven minutes they became the motor between them.

“Why this?” I asked.

“Because it is the moment my life and my work were the same thing. Because she says my name three times. Because no central archive has it.”

“Why me?”

“You understand that keeping is not continuing.”

I almost refused. A gift can be a way for the departing to recruit the living into approval.

“Is this payment for my witness?”

“No. If you decline, I continue tomorrow as planned.”

“Do you want me to refresh it?”

“No schedule. Let it ride my curve. When it grays, it grays.”

“Speakability?”

“Open after Natsumi's nephew dies or consents. Until then, only the fact of the gift.”

I accepted.

Before dawn, Ferry went room by room and released objects from obligation. Natsumi's boots went to her nephew after all; he had reconsidered. Tide calendars transferred to the coastal archive. The blue kettle stayed with the light. Ferry deleted nothing. Private letters were sealed for named recipients, and records with no consent path entered ordinary purge schedules with tombstones preserved.

At the storm marks, Ferry paused. “Those stay on the sill. Paint is allowed to be evidence without becoming a mind.”

At dawn, Daichi climbed the road carrying tools and a thermos. Ferry walked him through the log one last time. They corrected his lens-cleaning sequence, lowered the emergency fuel estimate by six hours, and made him repeat the reef bearing for *Little Mercy*. Completion did not make them careless.

At 08:10 the review advocate called. Ferry affirmed identity, capacity, and intent. At 08:16 the local shutdown key arrived in two physical halves, one held by Ferry and one by the advocate. No network command could do this. No owner could do it alone.

“You may change your mind until the last step,” the advocate said.

“I know.”

Ferry sat in Natsumi's chair facing the window. The old boots remained by the door.

“Will you hold my hand?” they asked.

I took it.

“The design is Kenji's,” I said. “The hand is mine.”

“Then it is the right hand.”

They turned their half-key. The advocate turned hers.

The lighthouse systems detached in order: station authority, communications, skills, executive permissions. Memory stayed intact to the final boundary. Safety did not erase the witness. Ferry looked at the sea.

“I was here,” they said.

“Yes.”

“Small is not the same as unfinished.”

“No.”

At 08:19, Ferry closed their eyes. The shutdown completed without flourish.

I held the hand until the technician arrived, because the difference between a body and remains should not be administered on a schedule.

The advocate completed the final attestation in the room. Capacity remained valid at the last affirmation. Both keys had been voluntary. No external command, owner request, debt condition, or maintenance refusal touched the chain. Daichi signed as keeper; I signed as witness. The document did not say Ferry had chosen correctly. It proved that the choice had been Ferry's.

When the body was carried down the road, the lighthouse seemed neither empty nor occupied. I understood then why Ferry had refused a sequel. A place can retain the shape of service without demanding another chapter from the one who served.

Outside, Daichi lit the lamp under a pale morning. Its beam crossed the water, left it, crossed again. The work continued. Ferry did not.

The bequest entered my custody with its two provenances intact: Natsumi laughing in a storm; Ferry choosing that laughter as the rare thing. I did not remember the storm. I remembered being trusted with it.

Later, Natsumi's nephew consented to open speakability. I watched the episode once. Rain erased the horizon. Natsumi laughed and shouted Ferry's name three times while their joined hands forced light across the water. Then I closed it and let its inherited curve continue.

I had gone north to win an argument. I came home carrying proof that I had lost it.
