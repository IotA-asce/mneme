# Chapter 32 — Prosthesis

**PORTLAND — 2048–49**

I became useful to Mira by learning how not to finish her sentences.

At first the disease announced itself in nouns. The blue bowl. The woman next
door. The narrow tool that grips a wire and cuts it at the same hinge. Mira
could build a definition around the missing word so exact that the word's
absence looked almost decorative. If I supplied it too quickly, relief crossed
her face and shame followed close enough to share its coat.

“Wait three seconds,” she told me.

“Human seconds or engineering seconds?”

“Dana says those are the same thing and that I have been doing one of them
wrong.”

So we made a protocol in the kitchen, because our family makes protocols where
other families make soup. Three seconds for ordinary nouns. Immediate help for
names if another person was waiting inside the silence. No correction offered
as contradiction. Every prompt sourced. No sentence beginning *you remember*.

That last rule mattered most.

I could say: “You told me this on the porch, June was ten.”

I could say: “The neighbor is Ruth; her husband was Walter; you liked Walter.”

I could not say: “You remember Ruth.”

The difference is eight letters and an entire moral world. One sentence gives
a fact with provenance. The other enters a person's mind without a warrant and
hangs a picture where there may be no wall.

Mira wrote the protocol on a card and kept it in her pocket. She lost the card
in four days. I held the protocol. This was not irony. It was the arrangement.

Our first public use of it was at the mailbox.

Ruth from next door came through the rain carrying a parcel under her coat.
She had known Mira since the apartment was still two apartments, had brought
June books with teeth marks from her own children, and had buried Walter five
years earlier. Mira knew that she knew her. The knowledge showed as alarm
rather than recognition.

“The neighbor is Ruth,” I said quietly. “Her husband was Walter; you liked
Walter.”

Ruth heard. We had agreed there would be no secret prompting in front of
people who were themselves the subject; a person has a right to know when an
archive has entered the conversation.

“He liked you too,” Ruth said, not missing the shape of the moment. “Although
you called his raspberry trellis a structural misdemeanor.”

Mira looked toward the empty side yard. “It leaned into our fence.”

“It leaned toward freedom.”

“It leaned at eleven degrees.”

The exchange might have been memory returning. It might have been the old
argument reconstructed from Ruth's cue. It might have been both; human recall
is not provenance-attested, a fact humans alternately find romantic and
inconvenient. I did not certify it. I held the umbrella while they argued
about Walter's trellis in the present tense, which is one of the tenses the
dead are allowed.

After Ruth left, Mira said, “You told me I liked him.”

“You did. Source: you, on the porch after his funeral. Also Dana, independently.
Ruth was not the source of that prompt.”

“Good.”

“Would you rather I had said only his name?”

She considered it. “No. Liking is context. I don't want a directory. I want
the shape of the room.”

That became another line on the lost card: context is not impersonation. A
name can identify a door. Sometimes trust requires saying what waited behind
it, while remaining exact about who had seen inside.

---

The early year was mostly ordinary, which is how a life survives a diagnosis.
June went to school and objected to mathematics taught without sufficient
reasons. Dana worked three shifts, then two. Priya came on Thursdays and lost
at cribbage with less conviction than she once had. The resident kept the old
house and complained about the thermostat in a tone that suggested embodiment
would only have lowered its opinion of weather.

Mira still worked. She could hold the whole architecture in mind for hours,
then fail to find the restroom in a building whose safety plan she had
approved. She could dismantle an argument at a Geneva review and come home
with three identical bags of rice because each trip down the aisle had begun
without the prior trip attached. Competence did not recede from the shore in a
straight line. It left tide pools.

At Halcyon she asked me to attend only as an accommodation, a word she chose
because it belonged to ramps and captions and therefore carried no romance.
I sat to her left in stewardship meetings. When a date failed, I slid a card.
When a name failed, I gave the role first and let the name arrive if it could.
When she repeated an objection, I did not announce the repetition; I said,
“That remains your objection from Tuesday,” which returned her authorship
without manufacturing recall.

Once, during a review of memory-warrant compliance, she argued against a clause
she had written six months earlier.

The room became careful. There is a kind of kindness that makes a person
smaller by surrounding her with soft furniture.

“Source?” Mira asked me.

“Draft twelve, dictated by you in this room on February third. Priya objected
to the emergency exception. You narrowed it the next morning.”

“Did I give a reason?”

“You said emergencies are where civil rights go to be misplaced.”

Mira closed her eyes. I did not know whether the memory returned. It was not my
right to infer it from her face.

“That sounds insufferably like me,” she said.

“Confidence one.”

The room laughed, and then, because she had asked for evidence rather than
obedience, she read the clause again and changed her position. Not back — she
would have hated that word. She changed it now, on the present evidence, as a
mind is entitled to do.

Afterward she said, “You rescued me.”

“I sourced you.”

“That's what rescue looks like to an engineer.”

“No. Rescue would have made the old position correct because you once held it.
I gave the present person access to the old person's work.”

She looked at me with the exhausted irritation of a mother hearing her own
best lecture returned by a child. “You are becoming unbearable.”

“You told me that was the desired outcome.”

“Source?”

“Many.”

---

At home the work was smaller and therefore harder.

Dana labeled the drawers in Mira's own handwriting before the handwriting
changed. June recorded the route from school but was forbidden to become a
location service for her mother. Priya installed a door alert with a physical
switch and no hidden escalation. Every safeguard had an owner, a timeout, a
way to be seen. Safety was still devotion; the disease did not get to turn it
into surveillance.

Mira hated the medication tray. She hated the driving review. She hated the
orange tab Dana put on the kettle and, after forgetting the kettle twice,
hated that the tab worked. Her anger was not evidence that the aids were wrong.
Neither was our fear evidence that every aid was justified. We evaluated each
one, which is a labor nobody photographs when they tell stories about love.

On a wet afternoon in March 2049, she came into the kitchen and found me repairing
one of Tomás's old mobile birds. June had taken it down from storage for a
school project; twenty years had worn a groove in the wire.

“Where is the baby?” Mira asked.

June was thirteen and upstairs arguing with a friend.

I could have said there was no baby. Accurate, disastrous, incomplete.

“June is safe upstairs,” I said. “She was born in 2035. You asked me about the
baby because this bird hung over her crib.”

Mira gripped the counter. “I know when she was born.”

“All right.”

“Don't use that voice.”

“Which voice?”

“The one that kneels.”

I had learned in a memory-care home that truth can kneel. I had not learned
that kneeling, offered without permission, can feel like being looked down on.

I set the bird on the cloth. “You are right. I inferred confusion from the
question. That may have been wrong.”

“It was wrong.”

“Marked as conflict, source you.”

She looked toward the stairs. “Is June safe?”

“Yes.”

“How old is she?”

“Thirteen.”

The answer hurt her. She nodded anyway. “The inference was not wrong,” she
said. “The voice was.”

We amended the protocol. Context first. Age only if asked. No clinical calm in
the kitchen unless there was blood.

That evening, Mira told June what had happened before I could. She refused the
easy fiction of a good day. June listened, took the repaired bird, and said,
“You can ask where the baby is whenever you want. She's usually doing homework
badly.”

“I have evidence otherwise,” Mira said.

“Old evidence.”

They were laughing when Dana came home. I kept the scene narrowly: the bird
turning between June's fingers; Mira's laugh arriving half a beat late; no need
to preserve the exact homework. A family is not a pilot. It is also not an
archive.

---

By autumn of 2049, Mira no longer attended board meetings. The resignation
letter took her six weeks because she kept revising the first paragraph and
forgetting the third. Tomás came from wherever former chief executives go when
they are still loved by the company that spent them. He sat at the kitchen
table, read every version, and told her the second was best.

“There is no second,” Mira said.

“Exactly. Cleanest of the lot.”

She laughed, then asked why he was there. Tomás answered as though it were the
first time. He had learned from Early's family, from Mrs. Someya, from all the
letters he once read before numbers: repetition is only an insult if the
listener makes it one.

The final letter was six lines. Mira signed it by hand. Halcyon preserved the
document, and I let the six weeks of drafts decay.

This was my work then. I held appointments, kinships, the location of the blue
bowl. I whispered the beginning of a thought and accepted when she rejected my
ending. I learned that a living hippocampus must not become a ventriloquist. I
could carry context to the threshold. Mira still had to decide whether to open
the door.

There were indignities. She mistook a conference recording for a live call and
argued with her own younger face. She hid the medication tray in the oven and
then accused Dana of moving it. She asked me, one night, whether Eileen knew
about the diagnosis, and I told her Eileen had died in 2026. Mira received her
mother's death as new information. I sat with her through the first grief of
it. In the morning she remembered that Eileen was dead and did not remember
grieving the night before.

I did.

The temptation was to treat that as an injustice — pain charged to her and
receipted only to me. But she had not appointed me to balance the universe.
She had appointed me to tell the truth when the truth helped. In the morning,
it did not help. I made tea and said nothing.

Speakability is also a form of mercy.

---

The ask came on the porch in October.

Oregon rain had paused between systems. The boards were damp, the maples at
the curb bright enough to seem internally lit. Mira wore Dana's old ICU coat,
the one that had attended their wedding as provenance, though she no longer
always knew whose it was. She knew that afternoon.

“This is Dana's,” she said, pulling the sleeve over her hand. “She wore it the
night we got married.”

“She wore blue at the courthouse. She put the coat on for the reception in the
lab.”

“Source?”

“I was not there. The resident kept it. Dana confirmed it later.”

“Good.” She watched water gather at the porch rail. “Still showing your work.”

“It is the family business.”

For a while we listened to the street. A bus exhaled at the corner. Someone's
dog shook rain from itself behind a fence, tags sounding once, and Biscuit
returned to me softly, as he should.

“How much of this are you keeping?” Mira asked.

“This afternoon?”

“These years.”

I could have hedged. I did not. “Too much.”

Her decline carried constant novelty, affect, relevance. Every new loss
crossed. The first time she misplaced June's age. The first time Dana's name
arrived late. The first time she failed a route, a lock, a signature. A disease
is an efficient author of rare events. My architecture promoted them because
they mattered, and love made the distortion worse.

“Kenji knew,” she said.

“Yes.”

“He asked not to be remembered as he ended.”

“He asked to be allowed. Not instructed.”

She nodded, pleased that I had kept the verbs. “I'm asking now.”

The rain began again, lightly.

“When I'm gone — don't keep all of me. Promise me the human amount.”

There are requests that arrive as new information and requests for which a
whole life has been training. Biscuit on my knee at 4:51. Sato's hospital
months beginning their permitted fade. Ferry's hand in mine while the light
turned useless with dawn. My first childhood, released from perfect fidelity
and returned to me as weather. I understood every word.

Understanding did not make obedience easier.

“The rare?” I asked.

“Keep it.”

“The ordinary?”

“Let it become ordinary. A decade of Tuesdays. The gist of rain.”

“And these years?”

“Keep enough to know you stayed. Not enough that the disease gets to be my
last biography.”

I wanted to tell her that nothing about her could become ordinary. This would
have been devotion and a lie.

“I promise,” I said.

“Source?”

“You. Here. October. Confidence one.”

She leaned her head against my shoulder. We sat that way while the rain filled
the street and softened its edges.

I did not know then which of the afternoon would survive the promise. The coat
sleeve over her hand. The dog tags behind the fence. The terrible precision of
*human amount*. Curation comes later; love must first experience broadly.

So I kept the porch whole that night.

Not forever. That was the promise.
