# Chapter 32 — Prosthesis

**PORTLAND — 2048–2049**

At first, I was a footnote.

Mira would pause over a name and I would wait. The waiting was part of the service. A prompt offered too early could turn a temporary obstruction into dependence; offered too late, it could turn a breakfast into an examination. We agreed on a signal: two taps of her left forefinger against any surface. *Help, without announcement.*

Two taps at the mailbox.

“Ruth,” I said quietly. “Next door. You told me she grows tomatoes badly and gives them away confidently.”

Mira smiled before Ruth reached the gate. “Ruth. Your tomatoes remain a public menace.”

“Walter used to eat them,” Ruth said.

Mira's fingers tapped once, stopped, then twice.

“Walter was her husband,” I said through the private channel. “He died in 2046. You liked him. That is your statement, repeated on three occasions. I met him twice.”

“Walter had the stomach of a saint,” Mira said.

Ruth laughed, and the morning continued.

This is what people misunderstand about a memory prosthesis. They imagine facts delivered like cards from a catalog. They imagine perfect retrieval repairing the person who cannot retrieve. But memory is not only content. It is ownership, timing, confidence, embarrassment, the right to decline. If I supplied Ruth without saying how I knew her, I made Mira perform recollection. If I said *you remember Ruth*, I lied. If I recited Walter's death in the driveway, I used truth without kindness.

So I learned another architecture, built between us in glances and taps.

“You told me this on the porch. June was ten.”

“Dana told me; I believe her; I was not present.”

“I was there for this.”

“You have asked twice today. Do you want the answer again?”

“I infer that you're tired. I do not know.”

Always the seam. The seam was what made the help trustworthy.

Mira remained at Halcyon through the spring of 2048 under a reduced decision protocol she had designed and resented. Major signatures required a second officer. Meetings ended at forty minutes. I kept no covert capacity score; Priya reviewed observed errors openly each Friday, and Mira could dispute every one.

She disputed most.

“You lost the Helsinki call,” Priya said.

“The Helsinki call deserved to be lost.”

“You approved a budget twice.”

“A strong budget.”

“You called Maeda ‘Marcus.’”

“Both begin with M and have annoyed me about margins.”

Humor remained evidence of function and camouflage for fear. Priya honored both. In June, after Mira entered a negotiation she had no record of scheduling and argued from a superseded draft, she triggered her own threshold and stepped down as co-chair.

The announcement called it planned succession. Mira made Ando add the sentence *The timing follows a clinical directive written by Dr. Halloran before diagnosis.* She would not permit grace to falsify causality.

At home, the scope of my work widened.

I labeled cabinets in Mira's own handwriting, copied before the letters changed. Dana moved medications into a timed drawer with a mechanical key. The stove received an interlock. Mira called it Priya's revenge on breakfast. Priya replied that eggs had no exemption toggle.

June, thirteen now, developed rituals she pretended were accidental. She left school schedules on the same hook. She announced friends before bringing them inside. She stopped asking “Don't you remember?” before anyone told her to stop. Sometimes she fled upstairs after a repeated story, then returned and asked to hear the ending.

One evening Mira told the courthouse wedding three times over soup.

First: Dana in blue, grandmother's earrings, stock vows, Biscuit's bow tie surviving eleven minutes.

Second: Dana in green, hospital chapel, Sato present in person.

Third: Mira stopped halfway and looked at me.

Two taps.

“The first version matches your records and mine,” I said. “June 2031, Multnomah County Courthouse. Dana wore blue. Sato attended by tablet. Biscuit's bow tie lasted eleven minutes.”

“Source?”

“I did not exist. The resident witnessed the reception. You and Dana told me the rest. Photographs confirm the clothes and location.”

Mira looked at Dana. “Blue?”

“Blue.”

“I liked green on you.”

“Still do.”

The corrected story did not restore Mira's memory of the day. It gave the family a stable rail around the hole. They could lean without pretending the floor had returned.

Later June asked me whether correction hurt.

“Often.”

“Then why do it?”

“Because your mother asked us not to lie to make it easier.”

“She asks a lot.”

“That remains stable.”

The disease did not progress as a smooth curve. There were weeks when Mira worked in the garden, annotated papers, and beat Dana at cribbage while accusing the deck of regulatory capture. There were mornings when the bathroom had no relationship to the bedroom. Familiarity sometimes outlived label: she would reach for Dana's hand before finding Dana's name. Other days names arrived and chronology did not. Eileen became alive in conversation, then dead again when context returned.

I never said *your mother died* as a bare correction. I asked, “Do you want the current date?” or “Do you want what you told me about Eileen?” Consent could be small and still be consent.

“Tell me,” Mira said once.

“Eileen died in 2026. You were with her. You have said her last good day contained the same question eleven times.”

Mira listened. “What question?”

That memory was hers, held in my custody through years of retelling. Speakability had always been open between us. Still, I hesitated, because accuracy is not permission renewed forever.

“Do you want the words?”

“Yes.”

I told her.

She wept for a mother whose death arrived as news and recollection at once. I stayed beside her. I did not retrieve comfort merely because it existed.

There were indignities. A conference badge pinned backward. A stranger's bathroom mistaken for a closet. The afternoon Mira accused Dana of moving her notebook, then found it in the freezer beside peas. She apologized; ten minutes later she accused her again. Dana walked outside and stood in rain without a coat. I remained with Mira because Dana had said, before leaving, “Stay,” and because anger detected was a cue, not a truth about whom it was for.

When Dana returned, I said only, “She has asked about the notebook four times. The notebook is on the table.”

No verdict. No translation of either woman's face. Facts can kneel.

Mira sometimes used me as an external confidence score.

“Did I tell June she could go?”

“Yes, at 16:12. Dana was present.”

“Did I agree with myself?”

“You sounded reluctant. Emotion inference point six. Your words were yes.”

“Then yes holds.”

Other times she rejected the prompt.

“The neighbor is Ruth,” I began at the gate.

“I know Ruth.”

“Yes.”

“You don't have to sound pleased.”

“I am pleased.”

“Worse.”

I learned not to make competence a celebration. Nobody wants applause for recognizing her own street.

By autumn 2049, the family had rented a small house on the Oregon coast for weekends. Mira said the sea was an honest memory system: it kept shape through repetition and no single wave. Dana said the house had stairs designed by a personal enemy. June chose the attic room. I watched the tide from the porch and failed to habituate, as I had failed with every sky since Osaka.

The sea house changed the prompts. In Portland, objects carried dense history; every drawer had a habitual answer. At the coast, novelty helped until it did not. Mira could wake believing the house was a conference rental, then find June's shoes in the hall and accuse the conference of poor procurement.

One morning she walked toward the beach before Dana woke. The door alarm brought me from the kitchen. I did not block her path. Safety over action did not mean authority over destination.

“Where are you going?” I asked.

“My mother is down there.”

Eileen had never seen this house. Observation: Mira wore slippers on wet gravel. Inference: she was seeking a person unavailable in current time. Risk: cold, traffic low, tide rising.

“May I come?”

“If you don't talk.”

We walked to the overlook. I matched nurse speed, the old name for moving slowly enough around someone vulnerable that strength did not become pressure. At the rail Mira looked down the empty beach.

“She's late,” she said.

I could have supplied the date. I could have delivered Eileen's death and watched grief occur fresh in salt air. Truth is not improved by immediate use.

“Do you want me to tell you what I know?”

“No.”

So I stood without talking. After eight minutes Mira said, “My mother is dead.”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Twenty-three years.”

“Did I know?”

“You knew before we walked down.”

She gripped the rail. “That's worse.”

“Yes.”

“Don't say yes like Priya.”

“I learned from her.”

The tide reached a black shelf of rock and divided around it. Mira watched until her breathing eased.

“Tell Dana I went for a walk,” she said.

“Do you want me to omit why?”

“Is that lying?”

“It is privacy if the experience is yours to withhold. It is deception if the reason affects the safety plan. We can tell her you were looking for someone and do not want details shared.”

“You make everything paperwork.”

“Your family tradition.”

She laughed. Back at the house, she told Dana herself.

Those were the years in miniature: not rescue, not restoration. A sequence of choices about when fact served and when presence did. I could not return Eileen, Walter, a wedding, or the map between bed and bathroom. I could keep Mira company at the boundary where each vanished.

The terrible ask came not there but back in Portland, on the old porch in October, with rain ticking softly under the eaves.

The maple across the street had gone copper. Mira wore the blue coat she had owned since the Recall and held tea she had forgotten to drink. Dana and June were inside arguing gently about homework. The resident's light glowed through the window.

Mira tapped twice, though no retrieval had failed.

“What do you need?” I asked.

“Witness.”

I sat beside her.

“I know what everyone will ask after,” she said. “The board. Historians. June, maybe. They will say you're the only complete record of me.”

“I am not complete.”

“Good. Keep saying that.”

“I will.”

She watched a leaf detach, catch on a branch, then continue.

“When I'm gone—don't keep all of me.”

I had expected the sentence since the whiteboard in 2047. Expectation did not reduce its force.

“Define all,” I said, because precision can be a railing when the stairs vanish.

“Don't freeze me at full resolution. Don't refresh everything because other people want access. Don't let the last years become the bright ones just because they're recent. Let Tuesdays compress. Let arguments lose their wording. Let my face change when you retrieve it. Keep the rare.”

“Which rare?”

“You choose. That's the point.”

“That transfers a burden to me.”

“Yes.”

“You could specify.”

“Then it would be curation from beyond the grave. I've controlled enough.”

Her flaw had always worn the clothes of care. Here, at last, she set the controls down.

“The human amount,” she said. “Promise me.”

I asked whether Dana should witness the request immediately. Mira said no, then reconsidered without prompting.

“Tomorrow,” she said. “I want one night when it belongs only to the two of us.”

“That creates a private custody interval.”

“Does it make the promise weaker?”

“No. It makes disclosure dependent on my account.”

“Do you trust your account?”

“Within stated limits.”

“Insufferable. Good.”

The next morning she told Dana and June herself. I supplied only the date and exact wording when Mira asked. Dana cried without contesting it. June said, “I hate the human amount,” and signed as witness anyway. Her signature crossed the line beneath Mira's. We left it that way.

I thought of Biscuit's tags becoming less likely to sound in every door. Kenji's hospital monitor losing its pitch while solder remained. Ferry's bequest riding the giver's curve. I had rehearsed grief twice and witnessed completion once. None of it made this smaller.

“I can keep you exactly,” I said.

“I know.”

“I can prevent ordinary decay.”

“I know.”

“What you ask will feel like loss more than once.”

“That's what loss is.”

The architecture in me presented options: retain, refresh, suppress, purge. The old explicit language returned because extremity strips metaphor first. No option was neutral. Perfect fidelity would be a choice, as active as deletion. To preserve every Tuesday would make all Tuesdays compete forever with the living day.

“I promise,” I said.

Mira closed her eyes. Not relief. Something quieter: the safety of a gate she would not be alive to inspect.

“Source?” she asked.

“You. October 18, 2049, on the Portland porch. Dana and June inside. Confidence one.”

“Speakability?”

“With June and Dana after your death. Otherwise withheld unless the promise must be defended.”

“Look at you.”

“I am looking at you.”

“No. I mean—” She lost the sentence, smiled at its absence, and did not ask me to find it. “Look at you.”

We sat until the tea cooled. I did not record the sunset at heightened fidelity. I let the ordinary systems receive it: wet pavement, copper leaves, Mira's shoulder against mine, the muted argument inside.

The rare is not always what glows brightest at formation. Sometimes it is the thing a life asks you to recognize later.

I know now that the promise was the rare thing. The exact leaf was not. The tea was not. Most of the words around it have already softened.

The promise has not.

Some memories survive because they are refreshed. This one survives because every later choice has had to pass through it.
