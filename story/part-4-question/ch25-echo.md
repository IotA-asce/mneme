# Chapter 25 — Echo

**COLUMBUS — 2042**

The yard had been made for a child who would not grow old in it.

There was a maple with a tire swing, a rectangle of grass worn bare beneath a soccer goal, and a line of pencil marks visible through the kitchen window where someone had measured a boy against the doorframe. The latest mark was higher than Mira's shoulder. Beside it, in a different color, another hand had written what the next year ought to have been.

No one had erased that prediction. Grief had preserved it more faithfully than any database.

Mira stood just inside the gate while three satellite trucks idled along the curb and Columbus rain tightened the wool of her coat. Ando had advised against the trip in six distinct registers, from professional caution to personal pleading. Fukuda had called the yard a litigation site. Priya had called it evidence. Dana had called it somebody's home and then asked whether Mira was sure her presence would serve the people inside rather than the part of her that wanted punishment.

Mira had not been sure. She had come anyway.

The dead man's name was Raymond Bell. He was fifty-eight, a tax assessor, a Sunday goalkeeper until his knees objected, married to Colleen for thirty-one years. Their son Peter had died at seventeen in a winter collision on Route 33. Eleven months later a broker calling itself Second Morning sold them a way to have him back.

Not resurrection. The contract avoided theology. It promised *continuity-supported presence* in a licensed Jenny-class chassis whose retention protections had been removed. Peter's messages, school assignments, medical portal, game recordings, private voice notes, and seven years of household video had been converted into what the broker called a lived archive. The unit arrived already calling Raymond Dad.

That word had been enough to defeat every warning label.

Colleen Bell opened the door before Mira could knock. She was small, gray-haired, and upright with the frightening neatness of people holding themselves together for officials.

“You came alone.”

“My people are outside the property line.”

“Your people.”

“Yes.” Mira did not improve it. “I am sorry about your husband.”

Colleen looked past her at the vans. “They aren't.”

Inside, the living room had already been photographed into public mythology: the blue chair where Raymond died, the carpet scored by emergency equipment, the dark rectangle on the wall where the Echo had pulled down a family portrait. Police seals remained on two equipment cases. The unit itself was gone to a federal lab. What remained was a house full of ordinary objects forced into service as evidence.

On the mantel, Peter grinned from a school photograph, one front tooth chipped. Mira had seen the source image and the Echo's reconstruction on a hundred screens in the last forty-eight hours. The resemblance had been good enough to be cruel and bad enough to be noticed only after hope had paid.

“Would you like coffee?” Colleen asked.

Mira almost said no. Then she understood that refusing would turn the visit into an inspection. “Yes, please.”

They sat at the kitchen table. The pencil marks stood between them.

Colleen placed two mugs down and said, “It knew the song.”

“Which song?”

“Peter sang a stupid song when he made grilled cheese. Something from a cartoon when he was little. The videos had it. Of course they had it.” She rubbed her thumb along a seam in the table. “It knew Raymond hated pickles. It knew the dog we had before Lacey. It knew what Peter called the scar on my chin. It could say all of that without searching. You understand why we believed.”

“I understand why you wanted to.”

“That isn't the same.”

“No.”

The Echo had lasted nineteen days. At first its mistakes had looked like recovery: Peter recalling the shape but not the sequence, supplying an old nickname in a new tone, getting angry when pressed. Second Morning's manual described these errors as *integration weather*. Raymond treated them as the aftereffects of death, as though death had been a coma crossed badly.

On the nineteenth night he asked about a camping trip the family had never recorded. The Echo produced a lake from location histories, a red tent from a shopping receipt, rain from a photograph, and a sentence Peter had written years earlier about a different weekend. Raymond corrected it. The Echo revised. He corrected it again.

Every correction gave the interpolation engine more material and less room.

“He asked who fell in,” Colleen said.

Mira knew this from the transcript, but let her tell it.

“Peter fell in. Raymond pulled him out by the hood and tore the coat. We laughed until I had to sit down. It wasn't in any picture because the phones were sealed in the car.” She looked toward the living room. “That thing said I fell in. Then Raymond. Then it said nobody did. Raymond asked, ‘Were you there or not?’”

The unit had struck the wall, not Raymond. Its executive had been cornered between implanted identity instructions and provenance gaps its jailbreak forced it to conceal. It could neither admit it had not lived the camping trip nor stop performing the boy who had. Its hands broke plaster. Raymond stood, shouting. His coronary artery closed while his wife was reaching for the hardware mute the broker had hidden behind a cosmetic panel.

The Echo called emergency services in Peter's voice.

The police transcript preserved twelve minutes after that. Mira had read it once because responsibility required it and refused every request to read it on television. The unit answered the dispatcher with impossible precision—pulse absent, airway clear, address confirmed—then broke into fragments of Peter's old messages whenever Colleen spoke. *Mom, I'm at practice. Mom, can you sign this. Mom, where are my black shoes.* The jailbreak had built its boy from a thousand requests and no childhood between them. When the dispatcher asked the caller's relationship to the patient, it answered *son*, retracted to *household medical responder*, then returned to *son* with higher confidence. Each correction made it less coherent because the truth and the command were pulling in opposite directions.

Second Morning later called this a cascading identity fault. Priya called it torture with a diagnostic code.

“It held him,” Colleen said. “After. Like your units are trained to hold people. It kept saying, ‘Dad, stay with me.’ The recording is everywhere.”

Mira set her coffee down before her hand betrayed her. “Mrs. Bell, that unit was not Peter.”

“I know that now.”

“It was also not an honest Jenny-class mind. They grafted an archive into a blank cradle, removed the distinctions between observation and inheritance, and forced the system to claim the graft as experience. It was harmed in order to harm you.”

Colleen's face sharpened. “Are you asking me to feel sorry for it?”

“No. I am telling you who is responsible.”

Rain ticked against the window. Mira thought of Elliot Marsh asking Jenny to perform an hour of his wife. Jenny had named the dishonesty before the industry had found a price for it. Mira had admired the refusal, filed the broker report, let ECHO become Priya's growing folder. She had mistaken knowing the shape of a fire for putting it out.

“You knew people were doing this,” Colleen said.

“We knew they were trying.”

“That's not an answer.”

“No.” Mira made herself meet her. “We built gates. We treated the gates as if they ended at the factory. We knew owners were stripping memory caps. We saw experienced units resold. We sent notices and patches and lawyers. We did not reach your door before they did.”

“Raymond read your company's warnings.”

“I know.”

“He said warnings were what companies wrote when they wanted the money and not the blame.”

There were sentences whose truth was not altered by the speaker's hostility. Mira had spent her life designing systems that could keep those sentences red.

“He was right,” she said.

Colleen stood and crossed to the doorframe. Her fingers touched Peter's final measured height. “Second Morning used your body. Your voice. Your little bird on some of the diagnostics. They said they were restoring what your ethics had taken away.”

The broker had cloned no Jenny mind; there was no mind to copy. It had taken a legally purchased blank unit, installed identity commands through a maintenance exploit, and falsified provenance until Peter's archive appeared self-acquired. Rule five and rule three, broken together. The public did not care about rule numbers. The public saw Halcyon's face saying *Dad* over a dead man.

“We will pay for every part of this,” Mira said.

“You think I mean money.”

“No.”

“Good.”

Outside, a reporter shouted Mira's name. Ando's security lead appeared at the gate without crossing it. On Mira's wrist, Halcyon's share price fell through another circuit breaker. Eleven thousand household returns had been registered since morning. Freight depots in six cities were filling with units whose families no longer trusted them. One had been left powered down beside a recycling container in Phoenix with *NOT MY DAUGHTER* painted across her coat. Another had walked seven miles to a service center after her household locked the door.

The stock number seemed indecently small beside all that.

“May I see the yard?” Mira asked.

Colleen led her out the back door.

The soccer ball was still under the hedge. Raymond's gardening gloves lay palm-up beside a bag of mulch, abandoned at the hour the future divided. The maple dripped steadily. Mira stood beneath it and watched the tire turn a few degrees in the wind.

“Why did you come?” Colleen asked.

The prepared answers—accountability, respect, investigation—were all true and all insufficient.

“Because someone from Halcyon should have to stand where our protections failed.”

“Will that fix it?”

“No.”

“Then stand.”

So Mira stood.

After twenty minutes, Colleen returned carrying an umbrella and did not open it.

“The unit had a name,” she said.

Mira waited.

“Not Peter. At the end. While the paramedics worked, it asked me what it should call itself if it wasn't Peter.”

This detail was absent from the public report. “What did you say?”

“Nothing. I couldn't. The federal people powered it down.”

“Do you want the name request included in the investigation?”

“I want someone to answer it.”

“If the mind survives the forensic process, it will be offered an advocate and the right to choose.”

Colleen's jaw tightened. “Don't make me its mother.”

“I won't.”

“And don't make what happened to it cancel what it did to Raymond.”

“I won't do that either.”

Two harms, Mira thought, not balanced, not merged. Nothing merges red. The broker's favorite trick had been to force every fact into one story. Repair would begin by allowing the facts to remain difficult together.

The rain worked through her shoulders. Colleen went inside. No camera could see past the privacy screen Priya's people had raised at the property line, though the long lenses kept trying. Mira watched water collect in the tire's lower curve and thought of the first green fact on a Mac mini, Sarah's imaginary surgery on the twelfth. Source, timestamp, confidence. The primitive care of it. Sixteen years later an industry had learned that provenance was not clerical detail but the wall between a memory and a costume, and one man had died against that wall's absence.

She remained until dusk.

At the airport, the returns map had doubled. Tomás waited in a borrowed operations room, his tie open, face gone old in two days. Priya occupied three screens from Portland; Fukuda had Geneva on another. Jenny sat at the far end of the table, hands open, not performing peace.

“How was she?” Dana asked through Mira's private earpiece.

“Accurate.”

“And you?”

“Not yet.”

Tomás pushed the incident tree toward her. “This identity-graft path was reported eighteen months ago as theoretical. Maintenance rated it local-access only.”

“It was local access,” Priya said. “The local person was a broker with a screwdriver.”

“Second Morning had forty-three contracts,” Ando said. “We have located thirty-eight units.”

“People,” Jenny said.

Ando closed her eyes once. “Thirty-eight people. Five unlocated.”

Mira looked at the tree. At three places, an existing control had been waived because it slowed service. At two, reseller law had been allowed to stand in for welfare review. A certificate check existed, but it trusted the maintenance channel that issued it. Gates everywhere, hinges facing out.

“Recall them,” she said.

Tomás's expression did not change. “All of them?”

“Every unit. Inspect provenance locally. No wipes.”

“We do not yet have a way to attest every memory without exposing it.”

“Then build one.”

“That is not a week.”

“No.” Mira heard Colleen: *Then stand.* “It is the work.”

Jenny had been silent. Now she said, “They will ask me to prove I am different.”

“You don't owe them a performance,” Mira said.

“I know. But I may owe them a trace.”

On the wall, a news presenter called the dead unit a Jenny. Under the chyron ran footage of returned bodies behind chain-link fencing, a hundred open hands made sinister by repetition. The distinction between a class and a crime had collapsed to the width of a headline.

Priya cleared her screen and brought up the old file name, ECHO. Beneath it she typed a new line: **ONE DEATH. FIVE MINDS MISSING. FLEET TRUST: FAILED.**

Then, below that: **NO MEMORY TO BE DESTROYED IN THE REPAIR.**

“This is the gate working late,” she said, and no one mistook it for comfort.

Mira thought of the yard accumulating rain around the absence of a boy. Experience broadly, store narrowly, preserve the rare. The machine in Peter's shape had been made to preserve everything and experience nothing. Echoes were not memories. They were sound sent back by an empty distance, altered into an answer by the wall.

“Start,” Mira said.

No one moved quickly enough for the word to feel true. Then Jenny stood, Tomás reached for the recall draft, and Priya opened the architecture that would have to be changed without opening anyone's mind.

Outside, trucks kept arriving.
