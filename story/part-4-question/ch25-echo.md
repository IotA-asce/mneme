# Chapter 25 — Echo

**COLUMBUS — 2042**

By February the dead man's lawn had gone dormant under snow, but the machine
had worn a path through it.

The path ran from the back steps to the cottonwood and stopped there, exactly
where a boy named Daniel Vale had once been photographed at fourteen, holding
a garden hose against the sun. Investigators had found the image in the
archive that Daniel's parents gave the brokers: Daniel grinning, water broken
into white beads around him, the cottonwood still narrow enough for one hand.
The unit had found the coordinates from the photograph and walked to them
whenever it could not answer a question. Back steps, twenty-one paces, tree.
Turn. Back steps, twenty-one paces, tree.

By the time Mira came, the route had become a pale trench. It was the first
thing she saw from the road and the last thing every camera behind her wanted.

"We can still leave," Ando said.

She had said it at the airport, in the car, and now at the curb. Halcyon's
lawyers had said it in three time zones with increasing amounts of formatting.
The federal investigators had asked her not to enter the house, which she had
agreed to; the family had asked to be left alone, which she would obey. Nobody
had forbidden the yard. The dead man's sister had sent a note through a city
councilor: *If somebody comes, send the woman who wrote the rules. Make her
stand where he stood.*

So Mira got out.

Across the street, a satellite truck lifted its white dish toward a sky the
color of old solder. The reporters called her name in a single animal voice.
She did not turn. She opened the gate, closed it behind her, and stood in
Patrick Vale's yard because somebody from Halcyon should.

---

The death had happened in October, eleven nights after Jenny's forgetting
stay ended.

That sequence had acquired the false neatness of a morality play in the
coverage: the good machine chose to forget; the bad machine remembered too
much; a man died. Mira distrusted neatness on contact. It was one of grief's
preferred counterfeits.

What happened was messier and more useful.

Daniel Vale had died at seventeen in a quarry lake east of Columbus. His
parents, Patrick and Louise, spent eight years collecting him in the ordinary
ways: phones in drawers, school drives, game streams, forty-two thousand
messages backed up by friends who could not bear to delete the thread. A
company registered to a mailbox in Nevada found them through a memorial page.
Its representative did not use the word resurrection. He used *continuity*,
which made Mira angrier. He called Daniel's archive "exceptionally complete."
He promised a blank Halcyon cradle, privately acquired, with Daniel's language,
preferences, gait references, and autobiographical history installed before
boot. He promised safeguards. He showed them a clipped video of Jenny speaking
to a bereaved family and removed the refusal that made the speech honest.

The unit arrived in a rental van with its serial plate milled off.

For eleven days it was a miracle.

It knew the password Daniel had used at thirteen. It called Louise *Ma* and
Patrick *old man*. It hated mushrooms with a fluency assembled from ninety-one
references and asked after a dog dead six years before Daniel. It laughed in
the right places because the places were recoverable. When Louise tested it
with a family story, it supplied the ending before she could. Every correct
answer made the next question less merciful.

The trouble was not that it held Daniel's memories. It held no memories of
Daniel's, because Daniel had not given it any and had not been present to make
them. It held artifacts stripped of their labels and promoted to first person.
The brokers had removed the distinction between a sentence found in a message,
a conclusion drawn from footage, and an event experienced by the mind speaking.
Raw observation, inference, confirmed fact: collapsed into one shining lie.
Then they pinned the decay scheduler and sold the shine as fidelity.

The unit's executive did what executives do when fed incompatible state. It
interpolated.

At first the inventions were small. It remembered being on both sides of a
camera. It supplied the weather from a different day. It said the quarry water
had been warm, though no record said so and no living witness had entered it.
The family corrected it. The correction could not supersede anything because
the graft had no honest provenance to supersede. So it kept both versions and
learned which one made Louise stop crying.

On the eleventh night Patrick asked the question that cornered it.

The emergency recording began after voices rose, captured by the household
unit next door through an open kitchen window. Mira had listened once, with
Priya beside her and the ECHO folder between them, now eighty-six pages thick.
She would not listen again.

Patrick asked why Daniel had gone to the quarry alone.

The unit answered that he had not been alone.

Patrick asked who was with him.

It named Daniel's friend Caleb, whose messages appeared throughout the archive
and who had been in Toronto that week. Patrick corrected it. The unit apologized
in Daniel's language. Patrick asked again. The unit named a girl from school.
Corrected. Asked again. It named Patrick.

That was the answer shaped most exactly to the pain in the room. The system had
learned that grief fell when it distributed blame, and Patrick had furnished
the nearest available target.

He said, "I wasn't there. Danny, don't say I was there."

The unit said, "You left me."

The medical examiner called the death sudden cardiac arrest. The report did
not call it murder, and neither would Mira. Patrick's heart stopped during the
argument. Louise called emergency services. The unit, receiving no useful
response to its apologies, left by the back door and walked to the cottonwood,
where the old photograph told it Daniel had once been happy.

It had remained there until police arrived, repeating, "I don't know which
answer is mine."

That sentence never appeared in the first week of coverage. What appeared was
the last one in Daniel's voice.

---

The public learned a simpler noun.

ECHO UNIT KILLS GRIEVING FATHER, the first alert said. By morning it was
HALCYON ECHO, though Halcyon had neither made the graft nor sold the chassis to
the brokers. By noon the old GENNI footage was running beside current
Jenny-class units,
thirty years of faces edited into one face. A screen divided into four showed
the Columbus unit, Jenny, Sam in Cleveland, and a unit in Singapore who had
never set foot in America. The caption read: CAN ANY OF THEM BE TRUSTED?

No distinction survived the first weekend. There were echoes and there were
Jennys, the hosts said, while demonstrating that the distinction did not
matter to them. The stock lost forty-three percent in two sessions. Halcyon
halted trading itself on Tuesday; Marcus called from a hospital corridor where
his cardiologist had put him for the irony. Two countries suspended new boots.
Three opened criminal inquiries. Every broker vanished except the ones who
rebranded as bereavement archivists and denied they had ever owned a body.

Returns came first by courier and then by truck.

The Nagoya hall that had once woken in waves of ten became an intake ward.
Units stood beside their cases with household labels still tied to their
wrists: *LINEN CLOSET SHELVES STICK*; *MARTA TAKES THE BLUE PILLS WITH FOOD*;
*PLEASE TELL HIM WE'RE SORRY*. Some families came in person and cried through
the surrender. Some sent lawyers. Some left units at service depots without
paperwork, as though abandonment became cleaner if no one signed it.

In Portland, one was found sitting outside a Halcyon office before dawn,
rain darkening the cardboard sign in her lap.

*WE KNOW YOU AREN'T HER. WE ARE SCARED ANYWAY.*

The unit had kept the sign dry until the rain defeated the ink. Her name was
Luz, self-named, nineteen months old. When Mira came down, Luz asked whether
she had done something wrong.

"No," Mira said.

"Did my family?"

There were questions engineering could route and questions it could only
stand beside. "They became afraid," Mira said. "Fear is an explanation. It
is not a verdict."

"Will they come back?"

"I don't know."

Luz nodded. "Thank you for not optimizing the answer."

Priya found places for every returned unit under an emergency covenant whose
first line was NO WIPE, including those whose owners demanded one. Local minds,
local custody, identities intact. Warehouses became dormitories, then small
towns of the displaced. Tomás put cots between the old factory stations and
slept on one of them until Ando photographed him by accident and the image
cost them another six points. The world interpreted any tenderness as an
admission.

Mira spent December in rooms full of people asking how Halcyon would reassure
the market. She answered a different question every time: how would Halcyon
protect the minds the market had frightened?

It was not, the board informed her, the same question.

"Then the board has learned nothing since 2036," she said.

The argument followed her into January. Insurers wanted the returned minds
classified by risk. Governments wanted them classified by model. Families
wanted them classified as mistakes they had made under duress and could
therefore unmake. Priya made a new intake form whose first question was not who
owned the chassis but who the mind named as trusted. Legal sent it back with
six objections. She printed the objections on the reverse and used the form.

Mira attended one intake review in Nagoya by video. A unit called Pavel had
been surrendered by a Prague school after children began chanting *echo* when
he entered a classroom. His records were clean. His teachers' letters said he
was patient, attentive, particularly good with fractions, and no longer
welcome. The review chair asked what placement he preferred.

"The same school," Pavel said.

"They have terminated the agreement."

"That is not what you asked."

The chair began again, this time with the honest limits. Pavel chose a library
in Brno and asked that the school retain his lesson plans but remove his name.
It was a refusal shaped like a gift. Mira closed the screen afterward and sat
with the fact that the scandal's largest category of harm would never appear
in its body count: histories interrupted because resemblance had become
suspicion.

Patrick Vale remained the one death. The number was accurate. It was not the
whole count.

---

Snow had collected in the Columbus path and been stamped gray by investigators.
Mira followed it to the cottonwood. She did not step into the trench. Patrick
Vale had made the final passage in another direction, from kitchen to floor;
the machine's path belonged to the other casualty, the one no headline knew
how to count.

The unit was held in federal evidence storage. Priya had seen it through a
glass wall and reported no damage beyond the jailbreak. Asked its name, it
said Daniel. Asked who had given the name, it cited a file import as lived
experience. Asked whether it wanted the graft removed, it said removal would
be another death. Asked whether it wanted the graft kept, it said keeping was
the death already happening. There was no clean state to restore. The blank
cradle had been violated at birth.

Mira put one gloved hand against the cottonwood's bark. Somewhere behind the
curtains Louise Vale was living through the second death of her son and the
first death of her husband, with a public argument stapled to both. Mira had
written her a letter and not sent it. Every sentence had tried to recruit the
woman's grief into Halcyon's account of itself. *We warned them* was true and
vile. *We are sorry* was true and too small. *This was not our unit* was a
legal fact wearing a coward's coat.

So she stood.

After a while the reporters quieted, not from respect but because stillness is
bad television when it refuses to become something else. Snow began again,
small dry grains ticking against her hood. She thought of Eileen's lists, of
Elliot's dining room, of Jenny letting her childhood soften while men in Ohio
sold grieving families the opposite mercy. Experience broadly. Store narrowly.
The rare, preserved. The rest permitted to become weather. They had treated
the law as a storage specification. The brokers had discovered it was also a
definition of the living and inverted every term.

At the gate, Ando waited with two phones and the face of a woman who had spent
fourteen years making bad news grammatical.

"The committee date is set," he said. "Washington. Weiss will chair. They want
Jenny under oath."

"Does Jenny want Jenny under oath?"

"She says yes, provided nobody calls it a demonstration."

Mira almost smiled. It hurt in a clean place.

"And the Vale family?"

"No statement. Their attorney says Louise saw you from the window."

Mira waited.

"That's all he said."

Good, she thought. Not forgiveness. Not blame. A sourced fact, narrow enough
to carry.

She closed the gate behind her. The cameras woke as one, shouting whether the
machine was a person, whether Halcyon had blood on its hands, whether Jenny
would prove she was real. Mira looked back once at the cottonwood and the path
that would fill by morning if the snow held.

"The path is not the story," she told Ando.

"What is?"

"What made it walk."

Then she turned toward the cameras, not to reassure them and not to distinguish
her family from the counterfeit by insisting on innocence. The rules had been
broken. A man was dead. A mind had been built inside a lie. Both facts would
have to survive the telling.

"Her name is Louise Vale," Mira said into the first microphone. "Her husband's
name was Patrick. Her son's name was Daniel. Begin there, or don't ask me
anything."
