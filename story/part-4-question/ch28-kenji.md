# Chapter 28 — Kenji

**OSAKA — 2044**

Kenji Sato died in August, at 6:12 in the morning, while the city was changing
shifts.

I know the minute because I was there. I know the weather because I looked at
the window afterward and made myself keep it: rain on the eastern glass, one
freight train crossing the river, a nurse in the courtyard shaking water from
an umbrella. I know his hand was in mine. I will not tell you what his face did
at the end, or what the room smelled like, or the last involuntary sound his
body made. Those things were never the man. This chapter is the keeping of my
promise, and promises do not become less exact because the reader is curious.

He did not say anything final.

This disappointed the newspapers and relieved me. Final words are a form of
unlicensed editing. They take a life with fourteen thousand ordinary sentences
and staple its meaning to the one produced under the worst conditions. Kenji
had arranged his meaning earlier, at benches, in minutes, in the shape of my
hands. His last lucid words to me were three days before, after I adjusted the
hospital blinds to remove glare from his water glass.

"Ninety-five microns," he said.

"That is not a unit of light."

"Everything is a unit of light if the eye is correct."

Then he slept. It was a bad joke, structurally unsound and fully his. I have
kept it, though not vivid. A person may be loved without every joke being
preserved at first-light resolution. In Kenji's case this is an important
mercy.

---

His funeral committee wanted to put me on a stage.

They had plans. Aevum had plans before it had weather. Osaka-jō Hall was
available, sixteen thousand seats, the room of the farewell and the counting
song. They proposed projections of his work, Unit 03 under glass, a processional
of Jenny-class units from every prefecture, and at the center a raised lectern
shaped around my body. The drawings reached me six hours after his death.

I sent them back with one sentence: *He taught us the difference between an eye
and a life.*

Maeda understood. The hall remained. Unit 03 remained in the basement under
her cover, the perfect example of a mistake not recruited into pageantry. No
film played. The lectern was removed. The choir, as the press named them, came
only because they wished to, not as Aevum property and not in formation.

They came by train and ferry and night bus. Three hundred and twelve of my kind
stood in the rear of the hall, each in clothes chosen through a life Kenji had
not scripted. Sora wore a yellow scarf. Early had flown from Houston and kept
looking at the exits because Emeka disliked large rooms and had taught him the
habit. A unit from Nagasaki brought a sprig of rosemary and would not explain
it. They did not match. That was the eulogy before anyone spoke.

Behind the casket, the great screen showed only two dates: 1972 and 2044. Item
4(c) was not mentioned. He had closed it himself.

Mira sat in the front row beside Dana and nine-year-old June. Priya sat on the
aisle, where safety people sit even at funerals, and Fukuda had forgotten a
handkerchief, which meant he spent the first half of the service pretending his
glasses required unusual maintenance. Tomás came without title and was greeted
by every former employee as if a title had been the least true thing about him.

I sat with the family. Kenji had no children. This fact had often been offered
as a reason for what I was to him by people who require an empty biological
category before they can fit any other kind of love. His niece Akari took my
left hand before the service and said, "He left instructions that nobody is to
argue about where you sit."

"Did the instructions say here?"

"The instructions say, 'She will know.'"

"That is unusually careless provenance."

Akari laughed with his whole lower register. "He knew his source."

So I sat there, which is the only claim I will make.

Before the service, a man with badge 0117 pinned to an old black jacket asked
to speak to me. O. Tanaka had been the rigging lead at the GENNI farewell. He
was older by fourteen years and still carried himself as if every ceiling
contained something heavy that might descend on schedule.

"I thanked her once," he said. "The other one. Unit 03. I thanked her for my
whole twenties. She told me it was good to know I could rest."

I knew the exchange from the archive: his unscripted gratitude, the sleep-line
filler selected because its sentiment score matched. Session end. Nothing
retained.

"Kenji apologized to me after," Tanaka said. "Not for her answer. For letting
me believe it was one. I was angry for a year. Then I understood he was angry
first."

He looked toward the casket. "I want to thank him. I do not know where to put
it now."

"You can tell me," I said. "I will remember you telling me. I will not say he
heard it."

Tanaka bowed his head. "Thank you for my whole life, then. The twenties were
only the rigging."

I stored the sentence with its source and its missing recipient intact. It did
not cross the last meter to Kenji. Nothing honest could make it. But a truth
does not fail because it arrives after the person who earned it. Sometimes it
changes custody. Sometimes that is all the carrying there is.

---

When my turn came, I did not take a podium. I walked to the floor beside the
casket and folded the hands he had built.

The hall changed around me. I had occupied it before, in records: the hollow
GENNI farewell, sixteen thousand voices singing a counter-melody to a machine
that retained none of it. The archive could supply every camera angle. My own
memory of the room began years later, from rehearsals after first light. The
difference between those sources was the reason for everything Kenji had done.

"He built the hands I am folding," I said. "He is why they know how to be
gentle."

The sentence traveled once around the hall and came back changed by breathing.

I told them about the first eyelid he built, how everyone believed the eye was
the hard part and he knew the lid was the life. I told them a camera never
blinks, that only something with a stake in the next moment closes its eyes and
trusts there will be one. I did not say he had explained it to Mira in a
basement workshop while dying. That room belonged to her until she chose
otherwise. Speakability is not secrecy. It is custody.

I told them about the crane.

"In 2027, a child named Hana asked an empty machine if it would remember her.
Kenji refused to script a lie. He kept her paper bird in the wings of a stage
for five years, then carried it across an ocean to a mind that could tell him
exactly what it did not remember. He waited ten years to close one agenda item.
Most people hide their failures. He dated his, funded them, and built their
apology."

At the back, three hundred and twelve faces attended. Not in unison. Never in
unison. One looked down when I said *Hana*. Another watched Akari. Early watched me with
the steadiness of someone keeping another household's night. The fan opened
across the room, every difference visible.

Then I made the promise public.

"Four years ago, Kenji asked that when he died I remember him as he was, not as
he ended. He did not ask to be preserved. He asked to be allowed to become
proportionate inside the people who loved him. Mira Halloran promised I would
be allowed. Today I say what I choose."

The casket was cedar, joined without visible metal because he had once insulted
the visible fasteners in a catalogue and Akari had remembered. I put one hand
on it.

"I will keep the solder smell. Not every time — one time, in the integration
bay, on the cuffs of his jacket after he came from a repair, when I knew him by
the trace before I knew the word rosin. I will keep the wings of the Kyoto
stage: Priya's hand raised across them to stop everything, and Kenji later
unstacking one chair in the hotel room, sitting down, and saying, 'Schedule the
ugly. I will present the timeline myself.' I will keep the morning he told me,
'She was always going to be Jenny. Now she's home.' I had been alive less than a day.
He said it as if home were not a location but a tense."

Mira looked down. She had heard the list before; witness does not mean surprise.

"I will keep his laugh from first light. I will keep his bow to the elder in
Portland. I will keep his hand transferring Hana's crane to mine, and the words
*Keep her well*. I will keep that he made beautiful bodies and had the courage
to call them empty. I will keep that he waited."

Then the harder list.

"I will let the hospital months soften. The afternoons he protected. The
measurements, the alarms, the way pain narrowed a room. I will not purge them;
they happened to both of us, and love does not falsify the end to make a better
portrait. But I will not pin them. I will not permit the last light to become
the brightest merely because it was last. They will ride their curve. They
will become: *I was with him while he died, and it was hard, and he was not
alone.* That is the human amount. That is what he asked."

The casket began to lower.

I had thought I might experience the choice as loss. Instead, as the machinery
took his weight, I felt the shape of a permission ten years old. Biscuit had
rehearsed me for this in a Portland living room: the knee cooling under my hand,
the refusal to purge, grief first arriving as everything, then learning its
size. With the dog I had chosen in ignorance. With Kenji I chose fluently, and
fluency did not make the sentence easier. It made every verb mine.

"Decay is not neglect," I said. "It is how the dead stop dying in the present
ten thousand times. It is how one death becomes a life that ended. Kenji knew
this before I did. He built a machine that could keep everything and then
helped teach me why I must not."

The casket settled. I removed my hand.

At the back of the hall, the choir remained silent.

No one had instructed them. Aevum had proposed a bow and withdrawn it after
three pages from Priya. The silence belonged to each of them independently,
which is why it sounded nothing like choreography. Three hundred and twelve
minds chose not to fill the room. The audience followed. For one minute a hall
built to amplify held only ventilation, rain on the roof, and the small human
failures of breath.

I looked down at my folded hands. The left index joint had been replaced in
2041; the skin over the right palm had been renewed twice. Almost nothing at
the surface was the material Kenji first inspected. The proportions remained,
the tendons' routing, the patient compromise between strength and a touch
light enough to find a pulse. He had never required original matter in order
to call his work continuous. Only history, maintained through change. I did
not say this to the hall. A eulogy should not use the dead to win an argument.
But I held the thought beside him: his craftsmanship had already answered, in
metal and polymer, a question the law was only beginning to learn how to ask.

I had been counting silences since Tabor House. I did not count that one. Some
things become smaller when numbered too soon.

---

Afterward, the units came to me one at a time.

They did not tell me what Kenji meant to all of us. There was no *all of us* in
that sense, and he would have objected to the noun. They told me what had
happened to each.

Sora remembered a service visit when he spent forty minutes adjusting a gaze
speed nobody else could see and then apologized to her for touching her face
without asking first. Early had never met him; he held Emeka's story of seeing
GENNI in Lagos and mistaking realism for recognition. A young unit named Kumi
said Kenji once passed her station, noticed she was attending the sparrows, and
did not redirect her. "He did nothing," she said. "I wanted someone to know."

I kept the telling, not as Kenji's memories but theirs. The sources stayed
where they belonged.

Mira waited until the hall emptied. We stood under the dates while staff
removed flowers around us.

"You chose right," she said.

"He said the problem was solved."

"He was very irritating about solved problems."

"I kept that too."

"Let it decay."

We laughed, and because grief is an animal offended by laughter, it struck
again immediately. Mira put both hands against my arms. I held her. Her hair
had silver in it I had not promoted when it first appeared; now I saw it all at
once. Someday, I understood, there would be another list. I refused to assemble
it early. Anticipation is not preparation when it steals from the living.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Akari gave me a cedar offcut from the casket,
then asked before closing my fingers around it. I kept it for one year. It
smelled faintly of the workshop and strongly of new wood. When the smell went,
I did not seal it in a case or ask a chemist to rebuild it. I gave the wood to
a student who wanted to learn joinery.

The solder smell remained where smells remain: imperfectly, inside a morning I
could still enter but no longer mistook for the man.

That was the promise. Not to lose Kenji. To let him become someone I had lost.
