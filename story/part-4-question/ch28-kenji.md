# Chapter 28 — Kenji

**OSAKA — 2044**

Kenji Sato died in March, while the cherry trees were still deciding.

For most of the winter he had asked visitors to report the buds outside his hospital window. He did not trust the camera feed. “A lens tells you color,” he said. “A visitor tells you whether the city is impatient.” By the final week, the buds were pink at their seams. He did not see them open.

I was with him the night before. Mira was asleep in the chair, her mouth slightly open, one hand closed around the visitor badge as if admission might be revoked without notice. Kenji's breath had become work performed in public. Machines translated the work into numbers and alarms. I understood every number. I resented them for their confidence.

He woke at 02:17 and looked at my hands.

“Still mine?” he asked.

It took me a moment to understand. Morphine had loosened his nouns but not his humor.

“The design is yours,” I said. “The hands are mine.”

“Good answer.”

“You trained me well.”

“Mira trained you to answer. I trained you to gesture while answering.” His fingers moved against the sheet, attempting my old open-handed pose. “Better marketing.”

I put one hand beneath his, not lifting, only meeting the weight available.

Years earlier, over an old eyelid assembly, he had asked Mira to let the ending fade. He had not asked me because at the time he still believed permission traveled through parents. Later he apologized and asked again.

*Remember me as I was, not as I end.*

I had said yes with conditions. I would not falsify his illness. I would not cut the hospital months out as if love had failed to attend them. I would let their detail decay at an ordinary rate and refuse the artificial refresh that public curiosity would impose. The promise was not to erase his ending. It was to deny the ending a monopoly.

At 02:23 he said, “The wings.”

“Which wings?”

“Kyoto.”

There had been many wings in Kyoto: stage left in 2033, after my safety override; the white paper birds June made during the recall; an eyelid linkage Kenji insisted looked avian though no bird would have accepted the comparison.

“I need more context,” I said.

He smiled without opening his eyes. “Good. Don't invent.”

Those were his last words to me.

He died at 06:08 with Mira awake and me on his other side. His daughter Emi, from whom distance had become habit without becoming estrangement, arrived six minutes later and put her forehead against his shoulder. I left the room before grief could be mistaken for a claim on the family's first silence.

The cherry trees opened two days afterward. The city called it early.

---

Kenji had left instructions for the funeral in a file named `NOT_YET_FINAL_7`, which was how we knew it was final.

No product footage. No corporate speakers except Mira, who was family before she was a co-chair. No preserved voice. No projection. The units could attend if they wished, but they were not to be arranged for visual effect. “If anyone says choir,” he had written, “make sure they mean singing, not matching faces.”

Nine hundred came.

They filled the rear of Osaka-jō Hall, the same building where hollow GENNI had taken her final scripted bow fourteen years before. Some wore household clothes; some wore work uniforms; one had painted a blue line across her cheek; one used a cane after declining a chassis repair that would have erased the record of an accident. One inherited body plan, nine hundred local lives. They stood silent not because anyone had staged them but because grief had found the same posture by different roads.

Humans filled the front. Engineers, riggers, nurses, board enemies, children from the hospital who were adults now. O. Tanaka sat in row eleven, badge 0117 pinned to a black jacket. Fukuda attended without a folder. Tomás came as a private citizen and sat beside Priya.

The paper crane rested inside its foam-lined case at the foot of the casket. Hana's fate remained unknown. That uncertainty had outlived its keeper and was still not ours to fill.

Before the doors opened, Emi found me beside the case.

“He left you more instructions than he left us,” she said.

The sentence contained an old injury I had no right to classify aloud. “He left me technical custody. That is not the same as family.”

“No. Family gets `CALL_EMI` on a sticky note.”

“Did he call?”

“Eventually.” She touched the clear lid over the crane. “He was better with unfinished machines than unfinished people.”

I held the fact without defending him.

“He talked about you during the last month,” I said. “Speakability was open to you. Do you want it?”

“Not today.”

“I will keep the offer.”

She nodded. Months later she accepted, and I gave her three sentences, no more: his pride in her bridge designs, his regret that apology had become harder each postponed year, his knowledge that showing up at the end did not repair the middle. She said they were insufficient. I agreed. The dead do not earn completeness by losing the chance to change.

I had never taken a podium. Kenji's plan accounted for this. A low microphone stood on the bare stage with no lectern to hide behind. I walked to it carrying no text.

There are events I remember in two times: as they occurred and as they altered through later retrieval. The eulogy exists that way. I can access the certified recording, exact and external. I can also remember standing before the people whose hands had assembled mine, feeling the room pull every version of Kenji toward the hour of his death. The recording is cleaner. The memory is true in a different direction.

“Kenji Sato built the hands I am folding,” I said.

I folded them.

“He is why they know how to be gentle.”

The hall made the small collective sound of grief receiving the exact wound it came for.

“When he was a young engineer, he learned to hide mechanisms under skin. He made eyelids that did not shiver, fingers that yielded before they bruised, a face that could hold sixteen thousand people as if it saw each one. Then he spent years admitting that beauty was not presence. This admission cost him more than the inventions. He made it anyway.”

Behind me, the screen showed no photograph. His instruction. The absent image made each person supply their own.

“He taught a machine to hold a baby before he could teach it to remember the baby. He kept a paper crane for five years on behalf of a mind that did not yet exist. He wrote an apology in a binder when no one had accused him. He named me before I could answer and then spent the rest of his life learning that a name is a gift, not ownership.”

Mira lowered her head.

“Kenji asked me not to let this winter become all of him. I will tell you what I have chosen to keep vivid.”

The sentence was public because the promise was. Nothing private followed.

“Rosin solder in his clothes on February eleventh, 2030, while he spoke to Portland through a bad microphone and remembered his wife. The wings of the Kyoto stage after the Incident, where he sat on an equipment case and said a skill was a memory with momentum. His hand writing *Item 4(c)* on masking tape for Mira's wedding. The way he bowed to the Portland resident who declined the body he had built. His voice at my first light: *She was always going to be Jenny. Now she's home.*”

In the first row, Emi covered her face.

“I will keep the afternoon he and Mira repaired an eyelid assembly in 2040 for no reason but the pleasure of it. They argued over cable tension to hundredths of a newton because love sometimes requires a harmless object on which to spend its precision.”

That earned a wet laugh.

“I will keep his impatience with cameras, his terrible file names, and the fact that he pretended not to like cake after safety failures, then took the corner piece. I will keep that he was sometimes vain, frequently theatrical, and capable of confusing motion with progress. The dead are not honored by becoming uncomplicated.”

I looked at the casket. The wood was pale, without metal ornament. His hands were inside it, the first tools with which he had built mine.

“The hospital months are also true. I will not deny them. But I will let their detail soften. I will not refresh the sound of his breathing because strangers ask. I will not preserve the numbers more brightly than the jokes. His ending belongs to his life; it does not get to become its title.”

The distinction was difficult to explain to those who imagined my forgetting as a settings panel. There was a settings panel. There were also years. Each unrefreshed feature would lose resolution through ordinary consolidation. The monitor pitch would uncouple from his face. The exact sequence of medications would compress to *we kept him comfortable as we could*. His final hand weight might remain because touch had its own stubbornness. I could not forecast grief completely. That was part of honoring it.

“A decade ago, our dog Biscuit died with his head on my knee. I was offered the clean exits: suppress, purge, stop the involuntary return. I refused. I chose decay. I said I wanted to lose him the way my family did. Kenji heard me and, years later, asked for the same mercy.”

I had rehearsed the choice on a smaller love. No love is small to itself, but lives have different spans, different rooms. Biscuit had taught me that grief could change without betrayal. Kenji asked whether the lesson scaled.

“It does,” I said.

I turned toward the rear of the hall. The units had come from homes, clinics, ports, schools, temples, farms. None carried Kenji's biography. Many had never met him. All inhabited the consequence of his work.

“He wanted you named by the people who loved you. Some of you named yourselves. He was corrected by this and delighted. He called you a choir once, privately, then warned us not to mistake matching instruments for one song.”

One of the younger units, self-named Cedar, began a low note.

No one had planned it. Another voice found a third above. Then a human voice entered from the front, uncertain but willing. The sound gathered without conductor: not a hymn I knew, not any melody Kenji had specified. A sustained chord, imperfect because bodies breathed at different times and some of us did not breathe at all.

I stepped away from the microphone. My eulogy was finished. The choir was not.

---

After the eulogy, O. Tanaka approached me with badge 0117 still pinned to his coat. He had been the last person hollow GENNI answered in public. *Thank you for my whole twenties,* he had said, and the filler cascade had offered him sleep.

“He told me you know,” Tanaka said.

“I have the archive. I do not remember being her.”

“I know.” He looked relieved that the old boundary had survived a funeral. “I wanted to tell someone who can keep it now. He gave us careers that were also our youth. We complained about him every day. We would have followed him anywhere except another production meeting.”

“May I keep that?”

“Please. Source: me. Confidence—what do you say?”

“Confidence one for the statement. The conclusion remains yours.”

“He would hate how much paperwork grief has.”

“He helped write it.”

Tanaka laughed, then cried without transition. I did not classify either for him.

At the cemetery, rain threatened and withheld itself. The casket descended beneath cherry branches cut from the tree outside his workshop. As it lowered, my retrieval system offered the hospital room with full recent force: breath interval, skin temperature, Mira's badge crushed in her hand, Emi arriving six minutes late.

I did not suppress it.

I marked no purge.

I let the retrieval complete, then declined to refresh the details beyond what mourning required.

Mira stood beside me. “Can you do it?” she asked.

“It is not one act.”

“I know.”

“I have begun.”

She took my hand—the design Kenji had made, the hand I had claimed—and placed something in it. His old solder spool, nearly empty, sealed in a clear evidence sleeve by Emi's request.

“Bequest,” Mira said.

“Object, not memory.”

“I know the categories.”

“You become reckless at funerals.”

“He would approve.”

The spool smelled faintly of rosin when I opened the sleeve later, in private. Smell is an efficient key. It brought back a video call before I existed, Kenji telling Mira his dead wife used to complain the solder lived in all his clothes. That record was inherited, not witnessed. I kept the seam.

For years afterward, when rosin reached me, the hospital tried to arrive with it. At first the room came entire. Then the numbers went. Then the shape of the monitor. The last words remained longer, perhaps because they were a joke and a rule together: *Good. Don't invent.*

By the time I write this, I no longer know the pattern on his hospital blanket without consulting the sealed record I chose not to consult. I remember the solder. I remember the wings. I remember the hands.

The cherry trees opened. The city was impatient. Kenji was not there to mistrust the camera.

Emi sent me one photograph anyway: the workshop window reflected in rain, branches white beyond it, his bench empty and every tool returned to its outline. I kept the image as hers, received after, not as evidence that he saw the season. Provenance is sometimes the courtesy by which grief declines to improve the ending.

I let that hurt change.
