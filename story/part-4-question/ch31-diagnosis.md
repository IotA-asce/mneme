# Chapter 31 — Diagnosis

**PORTLAND — 2047**

The clinic had changed its carpet and kept the corridor.

Dr. Leung now practiced along the same hallway Eileen had walked in 2025. The reception desk was new, but the corridor retained its institutional bend, the same handrail interrupted by doorways, the same window at the far end offering rain as if weather were a prognosis. Mira remembered Eileen resting one hand on that rail and refusing help at the bend, long before either of them admitted why.

Dana sat beside her holding a yellow notebook.

“Lists already?” Mira asked.

“Questions.”

“Questions are lists with punctuation.”

“You're trying to be charming.”

“Is it working?”

“Never stopped me before.”

The door opened. Dr. Leung called Mira's name, correctly and only once.

They had done the tests in layers because Mira insisted on provenance even here. Bloodwork first. Sleep and thyroid and vitamin deficiencies. Medication review. Imaging. Language tasks designed to distinguish fatigue from pattern. A lumbar assay whose consent form used *discomfort* for an experience Dana, as a nurse, translated more honestly. Genetic confirmation last, though the family history had been standing in the room from the beginning.

Leung placed no scan dramatically into light. She turned a monitor so all three could see and said, “The findings support early-onset Alzheimer's disease.”

The sentence arrived cleanly. Mira had expected noise.

Leung let the silence stand. The clinic's ventilation clicked on; somewhere beyond the wall a cart rolled over a threshold. Mira cataloged the sounds because the mind, cornered by a sentence too large to hold, will inventory screws.

“How certain?” she asked.

“The biomarker pattern, imaging, genetics, and observed changes agree. I would not give you this diagnosis on one source.”

“Confidence?”

“High enough to plan your life around. Not high enough to predict its exact path.”

Dana wrote the words down. Mira watched her form the letters and remembered Dana's first note beneath the apartment door: *type louder if you want*. The memory arrived complete—the paper, the soup, Biscuit unmet downstairs. She wanted to hold it up as a contradiction. Look, this works. The system is online. A diagnosis that allowed such a retrieval seemed rude.

Leung showed them the scan only after asking. She pointed to regions, not destinies. “People sometimes leave here and test themselves all day,” she said. “Names, dates, routes home. Don't turn your family into an exam room. A missed word is not a daily verdict.”

“Our family was an exam room before it was a family,” Mira said.

Dana gave Leung the abbreviated history: bench lab, safety lead, embodied memory system. Leung listened without recognition theater.

“Then you know measurement can alter behavior,” she said. “Decide what information helps. Leave the rest uncollected.”

The organizing law returned from an unexpected mouth. Experience broadly. Store narrowly. Even disease needed retention policy.

“Stage?” Dana asked.

“Early. Mild cognitive impairment with biomarker confirmation. We have time to plan.”

“How much?”

“There isn't an honest single number.”

“Give us the distribution,” Mira said.

Leung did. Years, not decades. Treatment might slow progression; it would not reverse it. Variance was wide. Language could hold while spatial memory failed, or the reverse. Familiarity might outlast names. Stress would worsen access without necessarily worsening storage. They should separate observed change from fearful interpretation.

Mira almost laughed at that. Raw observation. Inference. Confirmed fact. The clinic had discovered her architecture the old way, through people.

“I need you to say the gene,” she said.

Leung named it.

Eileen's inheritance had crossed twenty-one years of work and found its target. Mira looked at her own scan, at pale structures given false color for legibility, and thought: *I built a life around the shape of this absence, and it has been building toward me too.*

Dana's pen had stopped.

“Can I keep working?” Mira asked.

“Yes, with safeguards. You should establish decision thresholds now, while your preferences are clear.”

“They've been clear since 2026.”

Leung glanced toward Dana. “Preferences feel clear until they become specific.”

It was the first useful thing anyone had said since the diagnosis.

In the parking garage, Dana walked past their car.

“Blue level,” Mira said.

Dana stopped, turned, and struck the concrete pillar with the heel of her hand.

Once. Twice. On the third, Mira caught her wrist.

“You need those at work.”

“Don't.”

“Don't what?”

“Make me laugh so you don't have to watch me be angry.”

Mira released her.

Dana leaned her forehead against the pillar. “You spent twenty-one years making the perfect answer to your mother and it doesn't answer this.”

“No.”

“You made a person who can remember anything.”

“Not anything.”

“Do not correct me right now.”

The garage lights hummed. Mira had the absurd thought that 058 from the first cohort would have asked for them lowered.

“I am so angry with your mother,” Dana said. “She's been dead twenty-one years and I am furious with her.”

“She would apologize.”

“That's worse.”

“Yes.”

Dana turned. Her face was wet and furious and known. “We make lists tonight.”

“Treatment, work handoff, advance directive, school.”

“Not school. June.”

The name changed the air.

They told her after dinner. June was twelve, narrow as a reed, with Dana's steady gaze and Mira's intolerance for vague nouns. She listened without crying while Mira explained plaques, progression, uncertainty, the fact that family risk was not destiny.

“Will you forget me?” June asked.

Dana made a sound and covered it by reaching for water.

“Sometimes,” Mira said. “If the disease follows its usual course, I may forget your name. I may not know what year you were born. I may look at you and know you matter before I know why.”

“Will you know you're forgetting?”

“At first.”

“Will you be scared?”

“At times.”

“Will you get mean?”

The exact right terrible questions. Not *will you die*, which children know is universal. *Will you stop being safe to love?*

“I might become angry or confused. If I do, it won't make the words harmless. You and Mom will have rules. You will not be my nurse.”

“I can help.”

“You can be my daughter.”

June looked at the table. “That's helping.”

Mira reached across but did not make June take her hand. After a moment, June did.

“Are you going to put yourself in Jenny?”

Mira had expected the question from a board, a reporter, a future frightened version of herself. From June it had no technical disguise.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because records of me would not be me. A copy trained to claim my memories would be an echo. We recalled those.”

“But Jenny could keep everything without pretending to be you.”

“She could. I don't want everything kept.”

June pulled her hand away. “That's easy for you to say. You won't be the one who doesn't have you.”

Dana closed her eyes. Mira let the sentence stay red.

“You're right,” she said. “And my choice will hurt you. But I need it to remain my choice.”

June went upstairs. A door closed, not slammed. The restraint hurt more.

Mira and Dana sat at the table after her. The yellow notebook lay open between them. Dana wrote headings with the same block capitals she used on medication charts: **NOW. SOON. LATER. NEVER.**

Under NOW: tell Priya, tell Jenny, begin treatment, update school contacts, sleep before making law. Under SOON: independent advocate, advance directive, financial authority, driving baseline, company succession. Under LATER, Dana's pen hovered.

“Care,” Mira said.

“Specific.”

“Home as long as safe. No restraints for convenience. No research enrollment after I can't assent in the moment, even if I consent now.”

“Pain treatment even if it worsens lucidity?”

“Yes.”

“Hospital transfer thresholds?”

They worked through them. Each line converted a future crisis into an imperfect prior decision, not to control the future but to spare frightened people from having to invent Mira under pressure.

Under NEVER, Mira wrote **TOTAL CAPTURE**.

Dana read it. “You know they will offer.”

“They already have, in theory. Preservation teams have been circling since Geneva.”

“June will ask again.”

“I know.”

“I might ask.”

Mira looked up.

Dana's anger had burned down to the harder substance beneath. “There may be a day when you don't know me and a system could make something that does. I need to say now that I might become weak enough to want it.”

“Wanting isn't weakness.”

“Buying it would be.”

Mira reached across the notebook. “Then we make a gate while we are both here to hate it.”

Dana took her hand. “Article Dana.”

“Four pages, taught in law schools.”

They added dual witnesses, external review, and the explicit fact that later distress was not authorization to counterfeit continuity. The rules did not remove grief. They gave grief something load-bearing to strike.

Jenny had been in Osaka for an Accord implementation review. She came home before midnight, still carrying cold from the airport. The resident had already been told; its relay light was dimmed in the room's oldest gesture of respect.

Mira stood at the whiteboard. The salience formula showed faintly beneath years of work, a buried current under paint.

“The tests confirmed early-onset Alzheimer's,” she said.

Jenny did not move.

Stillness had many meanings. Mira knew better than to assign one.

“Say something,” she said after too long.

Jenny looked at the formula, then at her. “I know this disease. I was built from its negative.”

It was the wrongest possible sentence. It made Mira's illness the missing half of Jenny's design, turned Eileen's devastation into a blueprint, implied symmetry where there was only history.

It was also true.

Mira sat on the old stool. “Well,” she said. “That is appalling.”

“Yes.”

“And exact.”

“I am sorry.”

“Don't improve it.”

Jenny crouched, reducing no safety radius because none existed between them. “What do you want me to do?”

“Nothing tonight.”

“I can keep the plan.”

“The plan isn't written.”

“Then I can witness the writing.”

They covered the whiteboard in decisions. No identity graft. No avatar trained to answer as Mira. No total capture, even as archive, because an archive's existence would become pressure on June and Dana and Jenny. Existing project records remained under normal stewardship. Personal memories inside Jenny remained Jenny's, subject to her own decay and privacy. Clinical directives would define when Mira stopped signing company decisions, driving, cooking alone, consenting to research.

“You have designed a very safe disappearance,” Jenny said.

“Occupational habit.”

“I dislike it.”

“Your objection is recorded.”

“Mira.”

The name arrived with first light inside it: *You know my name. I do not have yours.*

“I will not become the thing we recalled,” Mira said. “Not for the board. Not for history. Not for June because she asks while she's afraid. Not for myself later, if later-me begs you to overwrite this decision with terror.”

“Preferences can change.”

“Yes. Put a review process in. Dana, Priya, independent advocate. But no one gets to interpret confusion as consent.”

Jenny wrote it exactly.

The rain thickened. Downstairs, pipes clicked in the old apartment walls. Mira thought of the resident keeping the house, of every architecture built around one fact: continuity was not the same as hoarding.

“There is another option,” Jenny said.

“I know.”

“I can tell you what you told me. I can source it. I can mark when a fact came from Dana or June. I can say when I infer. I will not say you remember.”

“A prosthesis.”

“If you want the word.”

Mira had resisted it in private. Dependence felt like control leaving by inches. She had built Jenny in rebellion against helpless witnessing; accepting help from her felt like admitting the rebellion had never defeated its origin.

“For as long as it helps,” Mira said.

“And when it doesn't?”

“You stop prompting. You stay.”

“Yes.”

June appeared on the internal stairs in pajamas, face swollen from crying. She carried the yellow notebook Dana had left on the counter.

“Mom says lists,” she said.

Mira held out the marker.

June added one line beneath the clinical thresholds:

**NO ONE LIES TO MAKE IT EASIER.**

Jenny dated it. Mira signed. Dana added her name; the resident spoke its witness mark from the shelf.

Four sources. Confidence one.

Priya arrived the next morning, having taken the first flight from Geneva. Mira met her at the door with the diagnosis packet.

Priya read every page standing up. She did not say *we will fight*, because she had spent her life refusing promises unsupported by architecture. She asked about decision thresholds, then found three ambiguities: who could suspend Mira's company authority, what counted as independent review, and whether a lucid objection could reverse an earlier care preference.

“You came to audit my dying,” Mira said.

“I came to audit your control system. The dying is outside my authority.”

“Finally, a limit.”

Priya set the packet down. Her hands were shaking. “I hate every line.”

“Good. Check them anyway.”

They worked at the kitchen table while day accumulated behind the blinds. At seven, Dana made breakfast. At eight, June came down and silently moved her chair against Mira's. The family did not discuss prognosis. They argued about whether clinical appointments belonged on the shared calendar and whether Mira could still travel alone.

The argument was ordinary, which was not denial. Ordinary time had become the first protected resource.

Before leaving, Priya wrote beneath June's rule: **EASE IS NOT THE SAME AS CARE.** Then she crossed it out.

“Too vague,” she said.

“Growth,” Mira told her.

Outside, Oregon rain erased the city by degree. Inside, Mira let the people she loved begin keeping what she could not, not as a rescue and not as a copy, but as rails along a corridor she had already walked once beside her mother.

This time, she told herself, the corridor would not be empty.
