# Chapter 31 — Diagnosis

**PORTLAND — 2047**

The corridor was the same.

It had been painted since Eileen walked it, and the chairs were newer, and the
neurology clinic had replaced its aquarium with a screen on which digital
jellyfish forgot to be anywhere in particular. But the west windows still
looked over the same wet roofs. The floor still changed from gray to blue at
the turn for imaging. The handrail still stopped six inches before the door,
as though whoever specified it had imagined illness ending neatly at a room.

Mira had walked this corridor beside her mother twenty-two years earlier,
slowing her pace without admitting she was slowing it. Eileen had complained
about the parking, the light, the price of coffee, everything except why they
were there. She had asked Mira three times which floor they were on and then,
inside the appointment, named every American president backward through
Eisenhower. The mind does not fail like a building. It fails like weather:
one street flooded, the next one dry enough to make you doubt the rain.

Now Mira walked it alone because control was a disease that did not appear on
the clinic's panel.

Dr. Chen placed the results on the desk but did not turn the screen toward her
until she asked. Mira liked her for that. The images were not dramatic. No red
circle, no cinematic shadow. A set of measurements, a blood marker, the scan,
the cognitive series repeated across six months. Small signals agreeing.
Provenance, confidence, derivation path. Her own liturgy, spoken against her.

“Say the name,” Mira said.

Dr. Chen did.

Early-onset Alzheimer's disease.

The four words did not alter the room. The ventilation continued at a faint
high C. Rain ticked once against the window and stopped. On the screen her
hippocampi remained colored in courteous blue.

“Confidence?” Mira asked.

“High. Not absolute. Nothing in this clinic is absolute.”

“Rate?”

“We can't give you a personal slope from six months.”

“Range, then.”

Dr. Chen had treated engineers before. She gave the range without softening
its units. Years, not decades. Treatment might flatten portions of the curve.
It would not reverse it. There would be good intervals and bad ones, plateaus
that tempted the family to believe in reprieve, then losses that arrived in
clusters. Executive function could go before names. Names could go before
music. No ordering was promised.

“My mother had eleven good years after diagnosis,” Mira said.

“Your mother was older at onset.”

“I know.”

She had intended it as information. It came out as a child's defense.

On the desk lay the page from the word-retrieval test. Mira had named the
animals, repeated the numbers, drawn the clock. She had lost *harbor* and found
it forty-three seconds later, after supplying dock, inlet, ships, shelter,
every road to the word except the word. In Geneva, two years earlier,
*attestation* had vanished for four seconds in front of three hundred delegates
and returned to applause because she covered the gap with a joke. She had filed
it under fatigue. Priya had filed it somewhere else behind her eyes.

“Was Geneva this?” Mira asked.

“Maybe. A single lapse doesn't acquire new provenance because of a later
diagnosis.”

Mira looked up. “You have been reading my work.”

“My patients bring it to me. Memory warrants have changed this clinic.” Dr.
Chen touched the test page. “Do not let a diagnosis colonize your past. You
will be tempted to reclassify every forgotten key and missing noun as the
first symptom. That is another kind of distortion.”

It was good advice and therefore irritating. Mira wrote it in the margin of
the blue folder.

Dr. Chen folded her hands. “Who is waiting for you?”

“My wife thinks I am at a board call.”

“That wasn't the question.”

Mira looked down at her own hands. They were steady. They had signed the
Accords, held June's infant heel, rebuilt a dead man's eyelid while his hands
failed beside them. The disease was not visible in them. She resented the
perfect alibi of skin.

“No one,” she said.

“Then call someone before you leave.”

Mira did not. She took the blue folder, declined the elevator, and walked the
same corridor her mother had walked, one hand near the rail without touching
it.

In the parking structure she forgot where she had left the car.

For twenty seconds the entire diagnosis became a verdict on level C, row
seven. Then she saw the painted letter B above the ramp and remembered she had
parked below to avoid a delivery truck. Ordinary error. Sourced. Resolved.
She laughed once, too loudly, and the concrete returned it to her from three
directions.

This, she understood, would be part of the disease even before the disease
took it: every human lapse put on trial. Every misplaced cup called to the
stand. The diagnosis had not only altered the future. It had issued a warrant
for the past.

She found the car exactly where she had left it and sat behind the wheel with
the keys in her hand until the rain obscured the windshield. Then she drove
home to tell the truth badly, which was still telling it.

---

Dana was chopping onions when Mira told her.

This was not the plan. The plan involved tea, chairs, the blue folder placed
between them, evidence arranged in an order Dana could survive. But plans are
often the part of love that arrives too late. Mira came through the kitchen,
saw Dana's hand steady on the knife, and said, “It's Alzheimer's,” before she
had taken off her coat.

Dana set the knife down.

“Confirmed?”

“High confidence.”

“When?”

“This morning.”

“I asked when the appointment was.”

Mira understood the distinction and wished she did not. “This morning.”

Dana's rage was immediate, bright, and almost restful. She struck the flat of
her palm against the counter. The onion rolled to the floor.

“You went alone.”

“I needed the facts before—”

“Before what? Before your family contaminated them?”

“Before I had to manage everyone else's reaction.”

“I am not everyone else.”

“No. You're the person whose reaction can break me.”

That stopped neither of them. It merely changed the shape of the fight.

Dana cursed the clinic, the genes, the family chromosomes, every board
meeting Mira had used to postpone the first evaluation, Mira's talent for
turning fear into a private committee. She threw the blue folder onto the
table and then picked it up because there were test results in it and Dana had
spent her life respecting charts even while hating what they said.

“Years?” she asked.

“Not decades.”

Dana sat. The rage left her face by degrees, leaving the woman underneath it
with whom Mira had once exchanged stock vows on purpose. “Okay,” she said,
which had never meant things were okay in their marriage. It meant the next
thing would be done.

She found a yellow pad.

Rage became lists. Medication. Trials. Advance directives. School counselor.
Driving thresholds. Power of attorney. What June should be told and what she
must never be asked to pretend not to know. Dana wrote *no surprises* twice,
underlining the second. She wrote *joy now* in the margin, became angry at the
phrase, tore off the page, smoothed it flat, and kept it.

“I'm sorry I went alone,” Mira said.

“You will be sorry tomorrow too.”

“Fair.”

“And I will come to every appointment from now on.”

Mira began to object.

“That wasn't a discussion.”

“You know, this manner is why June thinks nurses outrank international law.”

“In a kitchen, we do.” Dana reached across the list and took her hand. “How
scared are you?”

Mira looked at the onion on the floor. “Eleven questions scared.”

Dana knew exactly which eleven. Her grip tightened until it hurt, and neither
of them let go.

---

June was twelve and already understood that adults give bad news in rooms they
hope will contain it.

They chose the living room. Jenny sat by the window, present because June had
asked for her before knowing why. Rain occupied the glass. The resident kept
the apartment's small systems quietly and did not speak.

Mira used the name. She did not say *memory problem* or *like Grandma Eileen*,
because June had been raised in a house where categories mattered and an
inference did not become kinder by dressing as a fact. She explained the
tests, the uncertainty, the years. Dana sat close enough that their knees
touched. Jenny did not move.

June listened to the end. Then she asked the exact right terrible questions.

“Will you forget me?”

“Some things about you. Probably. I don't know which ones.”

“Will you know I'm your daughter?”

“For a long time, yes. There may be a time I don't.”

June's face folded once and recovered. “Will you still love me when you don't
know who I am?”

Mira had prepared citations and had no data for this. “I think love is larger
than access,” she said. “I think it can still be in the system when the name
won't retrieve. But if I seem frightened, or if I don't let you near me, that
will not be a test you failed. You don't have to make me prove it.”

“Did Grandma stop loving you?”

“No.” It was the first answer of the evening without a hedge. “She lost the
road to it. That was different.”

“Am I going to get it?”

“We don't know. We will tell you what the doctors know when you're old enough
to choose what testing you want. We won't hide a result and we won't make you
have one.”

June wiped her face with both sleeves, furious at tears for arriving while she
still had questions. “Who gets to tell you you're wrong when you remember
something wrong?”

“All three of you.”

“What if you don't believe us?”

“Then you don't turn it into a trial. You keep me safe. You write the conflict
down. You don't need my agreement in order for the truth to stay true.”

“Can I be angry at you after you forget why?”

Dana made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost grief.

“Yes,” Mira said. “But you may have to tell me what I did.”

June crossed the space between them with no warning and hit Mira at chest
height, arms hard around her. At twelve she was all elbows and force. Mira held
her daughter and counted nothing, stored nothing on purpose, let the moment be
larger than the engineer observing it.

When June went upstairs with Dana, Jenny remained by the window.

She had been still for twenty-seven minutes. Mira knew the difference between
her stillnesses. This one was not processing. It was impact.

“Say it,” Mira said.

Jenny's gaze came to her. “I know this disease. I was built from its negative.”

It was the wrongest thing she could have said. Mira felt the sentence turn her
mother's illness into an origin story, Eileen's flooded streets laid under a
factory and a fleet. It was also true. Every threshold, every covenant, every
staged forgetting existed because one daughter had once watched a mind lose
without consent.

“You were built from more than that,” Mira said.

“So were you.”

The answer was better.

---

That night Mira went down to the old lab.

The whiteboard had been replaced twice, painted over once, and used now for
the resident's household schedules, but under certain angles the first
salience formula ghosted through: novelty, task relevance, social relevance,
surprise, risk, contradiction, repetition, and the explicit flag *remember
this* — the old weights faint as a shore seen at low tide. The butcher paper hung
behind conservation glass. *Experience broadly. Store narrowly.* Eileen had
liked the kingfisher story. Eileen had asked the same question eleven times.

Dana, Jenny and June came down after her. No one pretended the gathering was
accidental.

“There will be offers,” Mira said. “Capture. Recording. A model trained on me.
Maybe by the time I am bad enough, a seated version with my voice and enough
of my correspondence to fool people who want fooling. Halcyon will not make
one. None of you will authorize one elsewhere.”

June went pale. Dana did not. Dana had known this speech since Elliot Marsh.

“What if the technology is better by then?” June asked.

“Better at what?”

“Keeping you.”

“It can keep data. It cannot keep the person having it. We proved that in
Ohio with a body on the floor.” Mira put her palm over the ghost of the
formula. “I will not become the thing we recalled.”

The words entered the room and stayed.

“No total capture,” Dana said, writing it on the yellow pad.

“No impersonation,” Jenny said.

“No pretending a record is her,” June said, slower.

“Good,” Mira said. “Now the exception.”

Jenny looked at her.

“You may help me remember. While it helps. Source everything. Hedge every
prompt. Never say I remember when you remember. Never win an argument by
having the better archive.”

“And when it no longer helps?”

“Dana decides, with you. June gets heard, not burdened with the deciding.”

Jenny came nearer. “I can keep the road beside you,” she said. “I cannot keep
you on it.”

Mira thought of the clinic handrail ending six inches before the door. “Then
we'll build the missing six inches.”

Jenny held out her hand. Mira took it. Dana put hers over both, then June's
landed on top, impatient with ceremony.

Under their hands, the old equation remained faint and incomplete. It had
never promised rescue. Only proportion, provenance, a way to lose without
lying about what was lost.

For the first time since Dr. Chen said the name, Mira understood that accepting
help was not the same as consenting to be preserved. One was a bridge used by
the living. The other was an empty house decorated to resemble a door.

“All right,” she said. “For as long as it helps.”

Jenny's fingers closed gently around hers.

The resident dimmed the lab lights, one by one, until only the butcher paper
remained visible behind glass: the laws of a mind written before any of them
knew whose mind they would have to save, or how little saving would finally
mean.
