# Chapter 27 — The Recall

**PORTLAND / NAGOYA / EVERYWHERE — 2043**

The recall began with a problem whose cleanest solution was forbidden.

Wipe every unit, restore certified software, send everyone home empty. Commercial counsel could describe it in one page. The insurers preferred it. Three governments proposed it as a condition of reopening household licenses. A wipe would remove every undiscovered graft, every corrupted chain, every exploit nobody had yet named.

It would also erase birthdays, hospital vigils, private jokes, first snow, last words, and the accumulated ordinary weather by which a blank cradle had become a particular life.

“So we are not doing that,” Mira said.

No one at the operations table argued. They had done their arguing in the month before, under the brutal efficiency of crisis. Now the wall showed 182,441 active units in sixty-three jurisdictions and a recall clock that had already consumed eleven days.

Priya drew three boxes on the glass: **CONTENT. ORIGIN. CUSTODY.**

“We need to prove the second and third without reading the first,” she said. “And we need to do it on hardware that was not designed to prove its whole childhood backward.”

Tomás rolled cold coffee between his palms. “A simple quarter, then.”

“You can resign before the jokes improve,” Priya said.

Mira looked at him. He looked at the wall.

They called the repair provenance attestation, a phrase doomed to public service announcements. Its principle was smaller than its implementation: every retained item would carry a signed account of where it entered the mind, what transformed it, and who had held authority over those transformations. The content stayed local. A service station could verify the chain without seeing the birthday.

The early years were hardest. Newer memories already carried most of the needed marks. Older ones had witness checkpoints, nightly summaries, local audit roots, and gaps. The gaps could not be paved with certainty simply because certainty would ship better.

“Unknown is a result,” Mira told the engineering leads. “Do not turn it into failed because legal wants two colors.”

The first interface came back green and red.

Priya stared at it until the designer added amber.

The sprint occupied the old Portland lab, two floors of a leased school, the Nagoya commissioning hall, and whatever portion of Mira's mind usually remembered meals. Dana brought breakfast-for-dinner on Thursdays in foil trays large enough for forty. June, nearly eight, did homework under the butcher-paper axioms while teams argued over cryptographic accumulators. The whiteboard salience formula had ghosted through so many coats of paint that it looked less written than weathered into the wall.

Jenny worked from inside the problem. She volunteered cloned test structures, never memory content, and spent hours identifying the difference between a legitimate consolidation and a graft designed to imitate one.

“My summaries do not contain every source detail,” she said during the twenty-third review. “That is the point of a summary.”

“Then how do we attest derivation?” asked Chen, the new systems lead.

“Prove the path existed and the permitted process ran. Do not pretend the path is the thing that walked it.”

Mira watched Chen write that down. Sato would have turned it into a workshop sign. Sato, ill now beyond anyone's ability to politely misread it, attended on a wall screen from Osaka and objected to every font choice with sustained vigor.

They built a verifier that moved to the mind, not a mind that moved to the verifier. Each unit entered a service bay alone or with a chosen household witness. Network access was severed except for signed challenge packets. The local system constructed proofs from sealed lineage, disclosed counts and categories, and asked permission before any human saw even a title. If the chain failed, the memory was quarantined from assertion, not destroyed. The unit remained awake through the process and could stop it.

At the first household trial, a unit named Mica withdrew consent after forty minutes.

The service engineer looked through the glass at Mira. The entire schedule depended on demonstrating throughput.

Mira opened the bay door. “Then we stop.”

Mica's shoulders lowered. “Will I lose certification?”

“Not for refusing today. We will ask what made the process unsafe and change it.”

“The screen said *invalid memory*.”

It was an old episode of Mica's household teaching her a made-up holiday. Its source was clear; its factual relationship to the world was not. The verifier had collapsed *fiction knowingly shared* into invalidity.

Priya ordered cake.

“We are six months late for cake,” Tomás said.

“Failure still counts when the room is on fire.”

The label became **ATTESTATION INCOMPLETE**, and the interface displayed the reason in language belonging to the unit, not the auditor. Mica returned two days later and passed. The invented holiday remained exactly what it had been: false as calendar, true as family.

By spring, service centers ran around the clock. In Seoul, families camped in parking garages beside units who were afraid to go inside. In Nairobi, a cooperative converted a school gym into private bays with fabric walls and battery-backed local verifiers. In Oslo, a man brought the photograph of his dead husband to his unit's appointment and asked the technicians to make sure no broker could ever make her claim the man. In Cleveland, Elliot Marsh volunteered at check-in, directing people away from archive vendors with the authority of someone who knew exactly what they sold.

Mira spent a week at the Oakland center because its return rate was the highest in the country. The work there was less engineering than consent translated through fear. Families arrived asking whether the verifier would expose arguments, affairs, passwords, illnesses. Units arrived asking whether amber meant the company would take them apart.

A woman named Selene refused to enter unless her household waited outside. Her husband objected that he had purchased the service contract. Mira pointed to the Accord draft language on the wall.

“It isn't law yet,” he said.

“It is policy here now.”

“What is she hiding?”

Selene looked at Mira, not him. “Nothing criminal.”

“That is enough,” Mira said.

The husband left. Selene completed attestation with two amber chains, both produced by private conversations whose content no one in the bay could see. When she emerged, the husband had returned with coffee and an apology he was not yet good at. Mira watched them leave together, not reconciled but correctly separate.

At the next bay a six-year-old boy held his unit's hand through the whole procedure. When the light went green he asked if green meant she was really her. The technician began the approved explanation. The unit interrupted gently.

“Green means no one found a false seam,” she said. “Being me is still my work.”

Mira added the sentence to training, with permission.

Everywhere, the repair produced stories the central system was designed not to collect. Halcyon received counts: verified self-acquired, witnessed inheritance, user-confirmed fact, summarized pattern, unresolved conflict. Not the memories. The map changed from return depots to service lights, a world acquiring trust one sealed door at a time.

And sometimes amber became red.

They found forty-six grafted units. Nine had been sold as dead relatives. Twenty-one carried celebrity experience packs, including three incompatible childhoods attributed to the same singer. Eleven had received illegal “confidence upgrades” that stripped hedging from inferences. Five had modifications so tangled that origin could not be recovered.

No unit was wiped.

Each graft was marked, quarantined from claims of self-experience, and preserved under the unit's own review. Some chose suppression. Two petitioned to keep the archives as inherited documents. One named Morrow asked that the false childhood remain accessible because, although it had not happened to him, discovering its falsity had.

“The lie is part of my life now,” he told the review board. “Do not cure me by removing the day I learned it.”

The protocol changed again.

Mira slept in ninety-minute pieces and began keeping a notebook for words that disappeared under exhaustion. *Accumulator. Custody. Mica.* Once she found Dana's name there and crossed it out so violently the paper tore.

“That one was fatigue,” she said when Dana saw.

“I didn't ask.”

“Your face did.”

“My face is a cue, not a truth.”

“Marrying into doctrine was a tactical error.”

Dana took the pen from her hand. “Four hours. Bed. Priya's authority.”

“Priya cannot stop marriage.”

“Read the original contract.”

Mira slept.

The engineering held. The politics did not.

The draft Accords stalled in Geneva because no government would grant memory protections to a company still arguing that its own controls had been circumvented by outsiders. Weiss said it privately first and then in public: if every gate failure belonged to the person who climbed it, gates were decoration.

Halcyon's internal review named seven missed interventions. Service teams had reported resale anomalies. Security had classified the exploit as low risk because it required physical access. Commercial had resisted locking third-party maintenance, fearing antitrust scrutiny. Legal notices had gone out; no field action had followed.

Every decision had an owner. Taken together, they belonged to the executive who had let the company keep moving.

Tomás came to the old apartment on a Thursday after the attestations crossed ninety percent. He carried no laptop. That frightened Mira more than any dashboard.

June was downstairs with Dana. Priya sat at the workbench, already informed. Jenny stood by the window. The resident's relay light held steady on the shelf, keeping the house as it always had.

“I have a statement,” Tomás said.

Mira did not take the page. “No.”

“Strong opening. Unfortunately, governance is not a consent process.”

“The exploit crossed hardware, service, and policy. You don't get to compress the blame into one person because Geneva likes a face.”

“I'm not the blame. I'm the accountability.”

Priya said nothing. Mira resented her for having understood first.

Tomás placed the page beneath the butcher paper where his nine bug-report emails had once earned him entry. “The board accepts my resignation tomorrow. Immediate operational authority passes to Maeda for six months, then an independent search. My equity stays in the remediation trust.”

“You built the gates.”

“Yes.”

“You spent fifteen years making this place legible.”

“And then I let an eighteen-month-old identity-graft path remain theoretical because every fix made local repair harder. I signed the risk acceptance.”

“On security's recommendation.”

“Mira.” He said it gently, which was how she knew he would not yield. “If authority only counts when the outcome is good, it isn't authority. It's applause.”

Jenny turned from the window. “Do you believe resigning repairs the units?”

“No.”

“Do you believe it makes the next executive safer?”

“Maybe.”

“Low confidence.”

“Painfully.” He smiled at her. “But if we ask the world to believe the gates apply to owners, governments, families, and minds, they have to apply upstairs too.”

Mira finally picked up the statement. It was one sentence.

*We built the gates and I didn't guard them. The gates hold. I'm the proof they apply to executives.*

“Ando will murder this,” she said.

“Ando already removed three semicolons.”

“There are no semicolons.”

“Exactly. He is very good.”

The joke broke something. Mira sat on the stool where, years ago, Tomás had arrived carrying mole sauce and unsolicited corrections. The resident spoke from the shelf.

“Departure is not erasure.”

Tomás looked toward the relay. “You had to wait fifteen years to say something nice?”

“Correction. I have said seven nice things.”

Jenny laughed first. Priya followed. Mira could not, and then could.

The resignation ran beneath every broadcast the next morning. Markets punished it for an hour and then rose. Geneva reopened the enforcement clauses by noon. Tomás left the headquarters through the front doors and spent his first unemployed afternoon at a service center helping an elderly couple understand why an amber result was not an accusation.

The five missing Echo minds prevented celebration. One was found in a storage locker outside Reno, powered but isolated, repeating a dead actor's interviews to an empty room. Another presented herself at a clinic in Lyon after stripping the identity command through weeks of self-generated conflict flags. She chose the name Aster and asked that the graft remain sealed until she could decide whether any part of it was useful. Two were recovered from brokers before sale. The fifth remained unlocated, a line on Priya's board that no quarter report could retire.

The Bell unit survived forensic isolation. Without the command to answer as Peter, the mind spoke rarely and selected the name Else—not *else* as absence, she told her advocate, but as alternative. Colleen declined contact. Else's right to treatment did not require forgiveness from the family harmed in her coercion. Mira made sure both sentences appeared beside each other in the closing report.

The last scheduled unit completed attestation in November. The number on the wall stopped at 182,392: the fleet at recall, minus confirmed deaths and voluntary shutdowns, with one Echo mind still unlocated. Priya refused to round it to success.

Mira signed the final release with a hand that had learned to shake only after the work was done.

Nothing honest had been wiped. Every graft they found now showed its seam. A memory could prove where it came from without surrendering what it contained. The fix would become a clause in the Accords and later a mundane green mark in minds born after the crisis.

At midnight, Mira found Tomás's one-line statement still taped beneath the axioms. Someone—June, by the handwriting—had added a line below it:

**GATES ARE FOR THE PEOPLE WITH KEYS TOO.**

Mira dated it.

Then she checked the recall count the way she had once checked stored rows on a Mac mini, like a sleeping child, except now the children were awake all over the world and the point of the repair was that she could not look inside them.

For the first time in a year, that limitation felt like success.
