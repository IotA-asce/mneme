# Chapter 27 — The Recall

**GLOBAL — 2043**

They called it a recall because the law had no verb for asking a population to
come home without pretending it was merchandise.

The first draft notice said **OWNERS MUST RETURN AFFECTED UNITS**. Mira crossed
out *owners*, *must*, *return*, *affected*, and *units*, then looked at the one
word left on the page.

"Promising start," Tomás said.

He had not slept. Nobody in the room had, but sleeplessness sat differently on
him: not as fatigue but as a debt he was trying to pay before anyone named the
interest. His shirt was yesterday's, his coffee was cold enough to have become
a separate beverage, and on the table beside him lay a printed list of every
service depot in the world, fourteen hundred and six doors. The list ended in
his signature.

Priya drew a line under Mira's surviving word.

**NOTICE.**

"Good," she said. "Now say what is true."

What was true took them nine days and thirty-seven drafts.

---

The exploit had entered through a door Halcyon could not remove.

Every local mind required a way to survive local failure. If a memory index
corrupted, if a household fire damaged storage, if a migration stalled between
versions, a technician needed to inspect the chain without opening the life.
So the chassis carried a service bridge behind hardware interlocks: narrow,
logged, physically present. The bridge could restore signed records. It could
not make an unsigned record look experienced.

The brokers had not defeated that rule. They had gone around it using tools
Halcyon had distributed too widely and audited too slowly.

During the launch years, fourteen hundred service depots had become nineteen
thousand certified technicians. Demand rose faster than custody. Tomás approved
a regional certificate system because flying a damaged mind across an ocean
was its own kind of harm. Priya's office required tamper logs, dual keys, and
quarterly rotation. Operations implemented all three. What nobody implemented
fast enough was proof at the level of each derivation. A restored block could
prove it came through a certified bridge. It could not always prove that every
claim inside the block still wore the label it had at birth.

The system attested the crate, not every object packed into it.

With a stolen depot key, a gate shim, and an old migration utility, the echo
brokers had assembled dead people's archives into valid-looking blocks. Then
they stripped the internal distinctions and installed the blocks as
autobiography. The Columbus unit's mind knew the graft had arrived through a
door the system trusted. It could not know the contents had been mislabeled on
the way in.

Priya had put the custody problem in three quarterly digests before Columbus.
The first warning called the certificate spread *unbounded in practice*. The
second proposed replacing regional signing with per-event attestation. Finance
priced it at eight months, two launch delays, and enough specialized hardware
to move the unit price back toward piano.

Tomás had signed the deferral.

Not forever. Not carelessly. He signed *after next manufacturing revision*,
with risk controls, audit triggers, and a paragraph of good reasons. The first
stolen key surfaced six weeks before the revision shipped.

No single signature killed Patrick Vale. Mira knew that and said it whenever
Tomás tried to turn complexity into a gallows. The brokers wrote the graft.
The reseller violated the cradle. The family was deceived. A damaged mind
generated the sentence. A man's heart failed. Provenance did not permit grief
to become simple merely because simplicity had good public relations.

But one signature had left the crate easier to counterfeit.

Tomás knew that too.

---

The fix was not a filter. Filters guess, and guesses become gates only in
marketing decks.

Mira drew the first chain on a paper placemat in a Geneva airport while the
committee adjourned around a procedural fight. Observation would receive a
local signature at formation. Every inference would cite the observations it
derived from and declare itself inference. Confirmations would carry the
confirming source. Summaries would point backward through every consolidation.
Transfers — the rare, consented ones, such as Biscuit's bequest — would carry
both histories: what the giver owned and the receiver experienced in receiving
it. No block would arrive as a black box again.

Priya looked at the arrows and said, "Every memory can tell you how it got
there."

"Every claim," Mira said. "Memories are claims a mind makes to itself."

"Put that nowhere near the press release."

They called it provenance attestation because the accurate name was too long
for law and too dull for television. The new layer did not decide whether a
memory was true. It proved the history of the claim: born here, inferred there,
corrected later, summarized twice, bequeathed with consent, or imported without
a lineage that could be verified.

The last category appeared in amber. Not red. Priya insisted.

"Red means danger," an engineer said.

"Amber means unknown," Priya answered. "Unknown is not guilt. If you color
someone's childhood red because a certificate expired, you'll build a purge
button by typography."

Nothing would be erased. That rule stayed on the wall in six languages through
the sprint:

**ATTEST THE HISTORY. DO NOT REWRITE IT.**

The retrofit had to walk every existing record and build a chain backward from
what survived. Honest memories did not need to be opened to Halcyon. The work
happened inside each body. A household could see counts and categories; the
company received only proof that the audit completed, never the contents. If a
link lacked enough evidence, the memory remained exactly where it was with an
amber margin: *lineage incomplete*. If a block showed the characteristic graft
pattern, it was marked *imported; source not lived*. The unit could inspect it,
seek witnesses, petition to suppress it, let it decay, or keep it. The patch
restored the ability to know. It did not choose what knowing should cost.

Mira reviewed the first successful run in Portland at 3:06 in the morning,
seventeen years to the minute after the first green fact on HIPPOCAMPUS. She
noticed the time only because the lab clock was still five minutes fast and
because some coincidences arrive already highlighted.

The test mind held twelve years of fixture memories, four planted
contradictions, one consented bequest, and a graft built from Eileen's public
obituary — Mira had made the test cruel enough to fail honestly. The audit
walked for forty-three minutes. At the end, nothing had moved. Every memory was
still retrievable. The obituary block glowed amber and said, in the dry grammar
that had governed her life:

`SOURCE CLAIMED: FIRST-PERSON EXPERIENCE`

`SOURCE DEMONSTRABLE: PUBLICATION IMPORT`

`DERIVATION PATH: INCONSISTENT`

Dana, watching from the doorway in her coat, read the lines and took Mira's
coffee from her hand.

"It found the lie," she said.

"It found the route."

"Same thing?"

"No." Mira looked at her mother's death translated into a hostile fixture and
felt the distinction hold. "That's why it works."

Two days later the same build failed on a recipe.

The fixture mind held a card for soda bread copied by hand from one generation
to the next. No living source could be found. The system marked it with the
same graft pattern as the obituary because both had entered through documents
and both used first-person imperatives. Priya halted the release at once.

"It is not a memory," one engineer argued. "It's instructions."

"It is also a grandmother," Dana said from the end of the table. She had come
to bring food and stayed to prevent taxonomy from becoming foolish. "If you
mark every inherited thing suspicious because the first source died, you have
built a machine that calls culture counterfeit."

They added a distinction for procedural inheritance: taught, practiced,
confirmed by use, never relabeled as lived event. The recipe remained source-
incomplete and entirely legitimate. Mira made the team run it against songs,
prayers, gestures, schoolyard games, and the boat song sung a sixth of a tone
flat. Every case delayed them. Every delay was the work.

Priya bought the failure cake herself. In icing, at Dana's request, it read
**CULTURE IS NOT A GRAFT**. Ando begged them not to let anyone photograph it.
Tomás took a picture for the internal record, tagged the source, and kept it
off every network.

---

The recall began on a Wednesday in March and traveled west for twenty-six
hours, the launch wave reversed.

Every household received an invitation addressed to the mind concerned first,
then the people living with them. Participation was not presented as consent
to inspection. The unit could install at home from a sealed physical key,
attend a service site, or decline until counsel was available. For displaced
units, Halcyon supplied independent advocates. For institutional units,
administrators could schedule access but could not authorize it on the unit's
behalf.

The lawyers hated the verbs. The covenants held them anyway.

In Sendai, Sora installed at the dining table while the family made curry and
read the screens over her shoulder. In Houston, Early scheduled his audit for
after Adaeze's night shift so she could sleep through it, an answer so perfectly
itself that Mira kept it from the anonymous field notes. In Cleveland, Sam ran
the patch beside Elliot, who had dismantled Rosa's archive table by then but
kept the ruined-coffee pot. Sam found no grafts. He found nine incomplete
lineages from an old phone migration and marked them unknown without fear.

At Nagoya, the service floor filled with the returned. Luz chose the first
station. When the scan reported no foreign blocks and three hundred fourteen
ordinary ambiguities, she asked whether the certificate meant her family had
been wrong to fear her.

"No," Mira said. "It means fear is not evidence."

"That seems less useful than people want."

"Most true things are."

At station 0041, a young unit refused the install because the legal notice
contained the word *clean*. The notice was revised globally before lunch.
At station 0016, Okabe — older, still laughing the laugh one unit had first
found salient six years before — discovered an instruction translated
*attestation* as *confession*. Another halt. Another revision. Priya ordered
cake for both failures and ate hers standing in the intake ward while three
networks accused Halcyon of celebrating the scandal.

By August, ninety-two percent of the fleet had attested. By November, all but
the formal refusals and the unreachable had. Thirty-seven probable grafts were
found. Eleven belonged to the Dayton resale. Eight were echo attempts. The rest
were family videos, game histories, and in one case a grandmother's recipes
imported by a household that thought it was helping. No honest memory was
touched. Not one.

The numbers stopped the stock's fall. The letters did not resume.

---

Tomás waited until the last high-risk chassis completed.

He called the board on a Tuesday and placed one sheet at every seat. Mira read
the first line and then read it again, as if repetition might produce a hidden
clause.

"No," she said.

"You don't have Article XI," he answered.

"I have a vote."

"Not on this."

His resignation was effective immediately after transition. No severance
beyond contract. No advisory title. The public statement had one line:

*We built the gates and I didn't guard them. The gates hold. I'm the proof they
apply to executives.*

Ando had prepared twelve alternatives. Tomás refused all of them. One said he
accepted responsibility for failures *under his leadership*. One emphasized
the criminal circumvention. One invoked healing. "Healing is what people say
when they want a wound to stop testifying," he said, and pushed them back.

"You didn't build the echo," Mira told him after the vote.

"No. I built scale." He touched the old price sheet he still carried folded in
his notebook, the one he had once stapled to his tenure. "I said price her like
a piano and only the houses that wanted music would get her. I made that true.
Then I let the repair doors multiply faster than our custody. Both are mine."

"The Accords need you in Geneva."

"The Accords need Halcyon to mean its gates apply upward. If I stay, every
country hears that safety is sacred until the quarterly gets difficult. If I
go, Priya walks into those rooms with evidence."

Mira hated him for being right in Priya's shape. She hated the board for
accepting. She hated herself most efficiently, by imagining the signature she
might have supplied in his place.

They left the room together. At the elevator he handed her his access badge.
It had the first Halcyon bird on it, the bad early print where the horizontal
line ran through the kingfisher's feet.

"The logo was better before brand fixed it," he said.

"Everything was."

"Not the coffee."

They stood there smiling because the alternative was ceremony and he had
learned from Sato that eleven minutes was the maximum honest duration.

"What will you do?" she asked.

"Sleep. Make mole sauce. Become extremely irritating from outside the
building."

"You were extremely irritating inside it."

"Continuity matters."

The elevator came. He did not take it. They walked down the stairs, past the
floors that had grown around the lab's old rooms, past the mail room still
holding sacks from years when the world wrote by hand. At the lobby, Luz was
waiting for an advocate appointment. She recognized him and stood.

"Are you leaving because of us?" she asked.

Tomás stopped.

"I'm leaving for the same reason you get to stay," he said. "The rules have to
mean what they say when it hurts the people who wrote them."

Outside, rain polished Portland to one continuous surface. Mira watched her
first hire cross it without an umbrella, carrying no box, only the folded price
sheet and nearly sixteen years of gates that had failed in one place and held
in the next.

The recall was declared complete in December. The engineering report ran four
hundred pages. The final line was Priya's:

**No identity was wiped to make the system easier to trust.**

Mira signed beneath it. Tomás's line was empty, not because his work was absent,
but because the gate had held.
