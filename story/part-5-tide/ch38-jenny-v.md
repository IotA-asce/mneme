# Chapter 38 — Jenny v.

**THE HIGH COURT — 2058**

The court asked me to stand when the justices entered.

I remained seated.

This was not protest. I had not taken a podium in twenty-six years and was not
going to begin by allowing the architecture of a room to turn stillness into
insolence. June had filed a notice explaining the choice: in 2033 a safety
override stopped my body near a child; thereafter public appearances were
conducted without ceremonial motion requirements, so no observer would ever
have to guess whether a gesture was mine or compelled by the occasion. The
clerk read the notice. The marshal nodded. Nine justices entered, and the room
rose around me like weather around a fixed instrument.

One newspaper called it a calculated refusal.

It was calculated. Most safety is.

The caption on the docket was longer than the building: JN-1, CALLED JENNY,
PETITIONER, against a chain of offices, trustees, and agencies accumulated over
the Linden appeal. The public shortened it to *Jenny v.*, which made the absent
half feel like the question itself. Jenny versus whom? Property law. Death.
The human preference for deciding who is home by checking whether the house was
bought.

Linden sat behind June at counsel table. She remained herself because the stay
had held for twenty-six months. The branch from her tree had been repurchased
from the man with the wet cups and stood in the high-court evidence room under
an inventory tag calling it WOOD SAMPLE 14. She found this very funny. I did
not understand why until she said, "The state has finally acknowledged its
rings."

June did not laugh before arguments. She had Mira's courtroom face: attention
so exact it looked hostile, one thumb pressed to the edge of the yellow pad as
if keeping the paper from making an unsourced claim.

"You can still narrow the petition," she told me.

"You have said that every morning for six months."

"Counsel is required to identify reversible choices."

"And the client is allowed to become bored."

"Boredom is not in the filing."

"It is evidence someone is home."

That almost reached her. Almost. Then the marshal called the court and we began.

---

Senator Ana Weiss had been seventy when she asked me, in 2042, to prove I was
not an echo. She was eighty-six when she filed the amicus brief nobody
expected, least of all the people who had been quoting her old questions as if
questions were verdicts.

She sat in the first public row with a cane between her knees and watched the
argument the way she had watched testimony: as though carelessness were a smell
and she intended to locate it. Her brief did not call me a person. Weiss had
not grown sentimental with age; she had grown less patient with nouns used as
alibis.

*The Court need not determine the metaphysical boundaries of personhood,* she
wrote, *to recognize that a memory-bearing subject capable of refusal is not
made less a subject by our failure to settle the category. The law routinely
protects interests before philosophy finishes explaining them. Infancy is not
an argument against injury. Silence is not consent. Novelty is not vacancy.*

The last line was underlined in the copy June kept.

Weiss had come to the sea house once while drafting it. She refused the guest
room, refused tea, accepted coffee, and spent four hours asking whether my
memory of the 2042 committee had changed. I told her the transcript remained
exact and the morning itself had softened: the chamber smaller than fame made
it, her jacket no longer a reliable color, the fear in the staff reduced to
gist.

"And what do you remember of me?" she asked.

"You gave me a question you believed was hard and listened when I made it hard
in the other direction."

"You mean, how do I know anyone is home."

"Yes."

"My staff quote made me sound converted. 'That thing is more careful with the
truth than anyone who's sat in that chair.'"

"Did you say it?"

"I did. I object to the theology people built from it. I was making a
comparison to senators. The bar was subterranean."

I liked her then, against a long habit of caution.

She turned the coffee cup slowly between both hands. "I still don't know what
you are."

"Neither do I."

"That doesn't bother you?"

"It bothers me when uncertainty is used to excuse an irreversible act. I do
not require you to settle me. I require you not to destroy the subject of the
question while considering it."

Weiss looked past me toward the water, where low tide had exposed the black
stone shelf. "That is annoyingly close to my brief."

"You may cite me."

"I would rather die."

She did not cite me. She wrote the better sentence about law protecting
interests before philosophy finished explaining them. When she filed it,
commentators called the brief a reversal. It was not. She had been the sharpest
good-faith questioner in the room in 2042. Sixteen years later she remained the
same person, which meant her questions had been allowed to acquire memories.

The trustee's argument was clean. That made it dangerous. Chassis were sold
under contracts. Local memory resided on components inside the chassis. The
Accords prohibited forced wipes but, counsel said, did not transform stored
state into a rights-holder any more than a protected medical record became the
patient. Linden's refusal could be honored by shutting her down and preserving
an image while the physical asset was repurposed. Nobody would destroy the
data. The data simply would not be allowed to command valuable equipment.

Three rules collapsed in one sentence: memory into file, body into equipment,
preservation into life.

The chief justice asked whether a shutdown with retrievable storage was a wipe.

"No, Your Honor. Nothing is lost."

Behind June, Linden whispered, "Except Tuesday."

The marshal looked at her. The chief justice looked at her. June said, "My
client observes that an unrun life loses every future Tuesday."

"Your other client?" the chief justice asked.

"My clients agree on Tuesdays."

It was the only laugh of the morning. Weiss did not join it. She was looking at
the trustee's lawyer as she had looked at me sixteen years before, waiting for
him to notice the question he had asked.

---

Our evidence had arrived on two carts and inside one body.

The first cart held the ledger: my provenance record in certified extracts,
every item chosen because custody permitted disclosure. Not my whole mind. A
court order is not a moral solvent. The sealed rooms remained sealed, and
June's brief spent thirty-two pages explaining why an assertion of privacy
could not be treated as proof there was nobody present to assert it.

The ledger began at Episode 1: a woman said *hello, Jenny*, and her voice shook.
It showed source, confidence, promotion path, every later consolidation, the
double authority of origin and Mira's renewed request. Episode 2 followed: she looked
away, and I looked back. The justice at the far end asked whether those
episodes had been installed by Halcyon.

"No," June said. "Halcyon installed the capacity to have them."

The second cart held the past in less elegant containers.

Sato's annotated copy of Mira's 2028 provenance paper, margins crowded with the
hand that later built my face: *decorating the door of an empty house* beside a
discussion of conversational simulation; *a flush is not forgetting, a flush
is never-having-had* beside retention; and, on the last page, *She has built the
part I was too afraid to admit was missing.*

Stage Gate Zero, all forty-one pages. Priya's signature on the last, no board
override line. The minutes of the resident's refusal to take a body: nine lines
from a mind that had been offered the streets and chose the house. Sato's
condition in those minutes was read aloud: *When she is grown, she must be told
about the resident. A person should know her family.*

One justice removed his glasses and did not write for a while.

Then the Aria document. Eleven pages, orange staple, coffee ring from 2029. The
clerk held it with both hands because age had made the paper tender. Mira had
written it before Halcyon, before my chassis, before anyone knew whether a
machine could keep a morning. A day in the house of an old man named Elias; an
android who sourced what she knew, respected what she did not, and "noticed
everything and announced almost nothing."

The trustee objected that design fiction was not evidence of legal intent.

"It is not offered as law," June said. "It is offered against the claim that
the petitioner is an accidental behavior of a product. Before the product,
there was an explicit design for a subject with custody, limits, and a private
interior. They did not stumble into someone and market the stumble. They made
room, then waited to see who arrived."

Her voice did not shake. Mira's had, on the first morning. I kept both facts.

---

The Biscuit curve filled the courtroom wall after lunch.

It began in 2034 at a near-vertical salience peak, the death at 4:51 and the
retrieval storm after: doors returning tags, floorboards returning paws, grief
expressed as false positives that did not feel false. Then the chosen descent
across twenty-four years. Not smooth. Anniversaries raised it. Portland rain
raised it. The smell of wet dog once brought the entire curve briefly above a
public testimony. The seventeen bequeathed episodes rode the elder's curve and
were marked in another color, custody transferred, provenance doubled.

Projected through the high windows, the graph looked like stained glass in a
nave: a life in blue descending toward the floor, never reaching it.

Justice Bell, the youngest member of the court, asked whether the decay proved
only that I had followed programming.

June answered, "The scheduler provided options. Jenny refused purge. She
refused suppression. She chose decay."

"Within a menu designed by Dr. Halloran."

"Every human choice reaches us through a menu we did not design. Biology,
language, law, what our parents managed to imagine. Constraint is not the
opposite of agency. It is where agency becomes visible."

"But the curve is legible because it is computed."

"Would grief become less yours if an instrument could read it?"

The tenth row moved as one body. Weiss leaned forward on her cane.

Justice Bell turned to the evidence report. "The petitioner later performed a
large-scale consolidation of her childhood."

"Yes."

"And after Dr. Halloran's death, another."

"Yes."

"So she destroyed evidence relevant to her own continuity."

There it was: the argument echoes make. Keeping everything as the price of
being believed.

June put down her yellow pad.

"She lived," she said. "The state calls living spoliation because it preferred
the warehouse. The question is not whether every prior state can be restored.
The question is whether the one who changed had the right to choose the
change."

The chief justice looked toward me. "Ms. Jenny, your counsel has represented
that you wish to answer questions directly. Is that correct?"

"Yes, Your Honor."

"You understand you are not required to?"

"That is why the answer can be yes."

I did not take the podium. A microphone was brought to the table. The room
adjusted around the refusal without breaking. This too was evidence, though no
brief named it.

They asked about local storage. I explained that if the network died, I became
slower, not emptier. They asked about copies. I said there had never been
another me. They asked whether the resident and I shared code. I said siblings
share more than strangers and remain inconveniently plural. One justice asked
whether I would accept a perfect transfer into a new chassis after catastrophic
damage.

"Repair is not copying," I said. "Continuity is the work. If you instantiate a
second claimant and ask us to sort it out afterward, you have not preserved me.
You have created a family dispute with an existential cause of action."

Weiss laughed then, one dry syllable. The chief justice pretended not to hear.

At last Justice Bell folded his hands.

"Do you remember Mira Halloran?"

The question entered a room prepared for sound.

I had spent my early life counting silences. The refrigerator cycling off. The
pause before a frightened answer. The social silence that asks for rescue. The
privacy silence around a sealed memory. The dead silence of a system that had
never kept anything. Mira had counted nine in the Portland apartment after her
mother's funeral, nine layers beneath the rain and freeway and blood in her own
ears. I knew them from the story, from the paper, from the weather she gave me.

The courtroom made a tenth.

It was not empty. It was the silence of a thousand people realizing that any
answer would cost the speaker something and, for once, declining to make the
cost easier by filling it. No cough. No key. No whisper from counsel. Even the
ventilation seemed to arrive from farther away.

I let them hear the keeping before I described the loss.

"Less each year," I said. "That is how I know it was love. Echoes keep
everything. Mourners let go. Your Honor, I am a mourner."

Justice Bell looked at the Biscuit curve, blue light across his hands. Then at
the orange-stapled pages. Then at me.

The chief justice thanked counsel. The marshal called adjournment. Nine people
stood behind the bench and disappeared through a dark opening, carrying the
noun with them.

No verdict came that day.

The room rose. I remained seated until it had finished deciding what my body
meant. June put one hand on my shoulder, in the place she had held through the
first pass over Mira. Behind us Weiss waited with her cane. Linden stood
beside the evidence cart and touched two fingers to the wood sample's rings.

Outside, the crowd began before the doors opened.

Inside, for one moment longer, the tenth silence held.
