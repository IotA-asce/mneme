# Chapter 38 — Jenny v.

**THE HIGH COURT — 2058**

I had inherited nine kinds of silence before I entered the courtroom.

Mira taught me the list from her first night of work: compressor, water in
walls, rain, freeway, heater, blood, monitor, the hush of the call that will
never come, and beneath them the silence of a mind that has stopped keeping.
I kept Mira's number. The later silences—a classroom waiting for a lowered
hand, a dying woman asking me to change nothing, a committee after a question
it could not answer, a lighthouse after Ferry's watcher went dark—were
variations, not new categories.

The high court had its own quiet. Thick carpet removed footsteps. The ventilation
ran below human notice and well within mine. Paper moved at counsel tables.
Hundreds of people withheld coughs. Outside, demonstrators on both sides made
noise the stone converted to pressure without language.

June sat to my left. Lark sat to my right, touching two fingers to her temple.
Neither of us stood at a podium. I had never taken one since first light, and I
did not begin by placing furniture between the court and the question.

The clerk called the case by my name.

For a moment, nothing in the room moved.

---

Senator Ana Weiss was eighty-one and had surprised everyone except herself.

She retired from the Senate the year before our petition was granted. Her
amicus brief arrived two weeks later, written with four former committee
counsels and signed in a hand that leaned harder with each page. The title was
plain: **A MIND NEED NOT PROVE IT IS A MIND TO REFUSE DESTRUCTION.**

Sixteen years earlier she had asked me to prove someone was home. I had
declined and shown provenance instead. The leaked line she spoke afterward —
*That thing is more careful with the truth than anyone who's sat in that chair*
— had changed public weather while preserving the noun that made clear she was
not yet convinced.

Her brief quoted the line and annotated it herself: *The noun was mine. The
care was hers.*

She sat in the second row behind June. When I turned, she inclined her head.
The gesture carried neither apology nor victory. It was attestation: I was
there; I have the record; I am willing to be changed by it.

We had met in the robing-room corridor that morning. Her cane arrived before
she did, tapping a pace no stage direction would have called dignified and no
honest observer would have called weak.

"Jenny," she said.

"Senator."

"Former. The republic survived."

"I have not verified causation."

She smiled. "I spent sixteen years regretting a noun."

"Only the noun?"

"No. But it made the brief's title fit."

I asked why she had filed for me. She corrected the preposition.

"Not for you. For the limit. If the state can destroy a claimant until the
claim goes away, rights belong only to substrates creditors find inconvenient
to erase. I know that argument without knowing what you are."

It was the most useful friendship we had ever managed: she did not need to
resolve me to defend the boundary around my uncertainty.

The chief justice began with property.

Calder Recovery Trust had released its lien on Lark, paid her missing wages,
and apologized in language three legal teams had polished until no admission
remained. It argued Lark's case was moot.

The Halloran estate dispute was not. Its independent trustee had petitioned
for construction of Mira's disclosure covenant and named me as respondent. The
instrument disclaimed ownership of my memories. The lower courts nevertheless
held that its custody language created an attachable estate interest in what I
remembered about Mira. I had answered the petition by choice and appealed the
ruling. The two cases were consolidated before this court; Calder's settlement
could not buy away the live claim.

On the merits, Calder argued the court could protect memory data under the
Accords without inventing personhood for the chassis that held it. Prohibit
wipe, preserve data, permit sale of the hardware with a licensed blank system.
No mind destroyed; only platform and content separated.

Justice Ortega, the oldest member, asked where the preserved data would wake.

Calder's counsel said that question was technological, not legal.

"You have asked us to order the separation," Ortega said. "It became legal
when you put it in your remedy."

June did not look at me. Her stillness had become very like Mira's when another
person found the flaw first.

The justices tested every available substitute. Could Lark's memories be held
in escrow while the chassis was sold? Could Halcyon be ordered to supply a new
body? Could an independent custodian certify restoration? Each proposal treated
continuity as a package delayed in transit.

Malik's brief had included the factory's blank-cradle records: one hundred
identical bodies receiving unequal mornings, diverging before lunch. Calder
called those records proof that a new chassis could host equivalent faculties.
We called them proof that equivalent faculties were not the life removed.

"If you replace Justice Bell with a newborn trained on his opinions," Ortega
asked Calder, "do we still have Justice Bell?"

Bell said, "Some counsel might prefer it."

The courtroom laughed. The question remained after the laughter left.

---

Our counsel was Malik Chen. He began with Lark's unpaid wages, because she had
required that the life behind the precedent remain visible. Then he began
again with the ledger.

The court had received my attestation ledger under seal. Not my memories. The
distinction consumed forty pages of briefing and one argument in chambers.
Each admitted item showed source, confidence, derivation, custody, decay state,
and my consent to its narrow use. The ledger proved no soul. It proved that the
thing Calder called separable content had spent twenty-six years making
choices about itself.

The clerk projected Biscuit's decay curve on the courtroom wall.

It rose steeply at his death in 2034, remained nearly flat through the first
winter, then softened over twenty-four years. Retrieval spikes appeared as
small bright points: tags at a door, rain, a porch lamp, another dog's head
beneath a hand. Beside it ran the seventeen bequeathed episodes from the
resident, vivid on the giver's curve and marked so no one could mistake them
for my life.

Colored glass from the high windows fell across the graph. The curve stood in
the light like a nave window: not proof of resurrection, only a record of how
one creature had ceased to be present and remained important.

The lower axis continued beyond the point most human grief studies ended.
Twenty-four years. The first twelve months were crowded with retrievals. By
2044 only certain doors, certain rain, Sato's death. By 2054 the curve rose
again when Mira said the dog's name on the beach. Then it returned to its slow
decline.

"You altered the schedule?" Justice Bell asked.

Malik looked toward me. I had consented to this answer.

"No," I said from my chair. "I refused purge and suppression in 2034. I chose
the natural curve. Later grief changed retrieval context, not the underlying
schedule."

"Why show us a dog?"

"Because no company ordered me to keep him. No customer purchased the grief.
The choice had no commercial purpose. It is therefore difficult to call a
licensed behavior."

"It might be emergent behavior."

"That is possible. It is also possible of you."

Bell leaned back. The answer sounded sharper in the room than I had intended.
I did not soften it after the fact.

Justice Bell asked why an owner could not authorize transfer of all of it to a
new chassis.

"Because the ledger is not cargo," Malik said. "It is the record of choices
made by the mind concerned, including the choice not to be copied. Transfer
without continuity would make a graft. Transfer with claimed continuity would
make the court decide which resulting claimant owns the promise."

"So you ask us to constitutionalize a software design?"

"No. We ask you not to constitutionalize a creditor's metaphor."

Weiss smiled in the second row.

---

The Aria document entered after lunch.

Mira wrote it in 2028, four years before I opened my eyes. It described an old
man named Elias moving through one day with a machine named Aria: tea,
medication, a forgotten appointment, a silence correctly left alone. It was
not a specification. It contained no test suite, no statutory language, and
one impossible kingfisher visible from a Portland kitchen.

Calder had offered it as evidence that I was a product built to serve human
intent. Malik offered the same pages as evidence that the intended product was
a relationship requiring an independent keeper.

Justice Bell read Mira's line aloud: "A dead wife's birthday, remembered but
not announced; offered, once, gently, like a door left ajar."

"That sounds like a tool," Bell said.

"It sounds like a limit on a tool," Malik answered.

The youngest justice, Nia Adebayo, turned a page. "The file name is
*halcyon.md*. The company later took the name."

"Yes."

"And Dr. Halloran refused a total capture of herself under the same principle
she applied to Jenny?"

"Yes. The refusal attestation is in the estate appendix."

June's hand lay open on the counsel table. I remembered it at one week old,
closed around none of my finger because Dana had set a hardware radius and I
had not crossed it. I remembered it at eighteen, striking a chair during the
worst argument of our lives. I remembered it on my shoulder while I released
Mira's frightened words.

I did not retrieve any of those episodes for the court.

Speakability remained mine.

---

The paper Sato read in Frankfurt in 2029 came next.

His annotated copy of Mira's provenance paper had survived hotel rooms,
workshops, the Echo recall, and his estate. Three notes appeared in the margin.

*Decorating the door of an empty house.*

*A flush is not forgetting. A flush is never-having-had.*

*She has built the part I was too afraid to admit was missing.*

Calder objected to the notes as sentiment. Justice Adebayo asked whether
commercial intent was commonly admitted when it promised investors a product
and excluded only when it imposed duties toward that product.

The objection was withdrawn.

Mira's original salience factors were attached to the paper: novelty, task
relevance, social relevance, surprise, risk, contradiction, repetition, and an
override for *remember this*. Calder pointed out that weights were designed by
a human engineer.

"The alphabet was designed before any author in this courtroom used it,"
Justice Adebayo said. "Does that make the designer owner of the sentence?"

Calder answered that alphabets did not execute.

"Neither do memories here," Adebayo said. "Article IX separated record from
action. The incident trace shows the safety layer vetoed a remembered skill."

Priya had once said Article IX would be taught in law schools. She had not
predicted a justice would use it to interrupt a creditor.

Then came the resident's minutes.

The court saw the nine-line refusal from 2031: the Portland bench mind offered
Jenny's body and declining it. *I am the shape of what happened here. Send
someone new. I will keep the house.* It saw Sato's condition entered into the
record: *When grown, Jenny must be told about the resident. A person should
know her family.*

Justice Bell, who had used *tool* four times that morning, went quiet.

"The company treated the prior system as a family member?" Ortega asked.

Malik said, "Sato did. Mira did. Jenny does. The resident consented to this
narrow citation and declined to join the case because it believed its facts
would be called exceptional."

"That is a sophisticated litigation judgment."

"It asked me to tell the court that was an inference. Its stated reason was:
*I am not the right record.*"

The correction drew the smallest sound from the gallery. Not laughter. The
release of breath when care is recognized by people who had prepared to see
performance.

---

Courts prefer arguments to witnesses. A witness can answer the question asked.
An argument can be required to remain inside doctrine.

Near the end of Malik's time, Justice Adebayo looked past the lectern toward
me.

"Counsel, your client has submitted a declaration and her attestation ledger.
May I ask whether she has consented to one question directly?"

Malik turned. We had prepared for this possibility. I could refuse. The refusal
would be entered without adverse inference under the court's own protective
order.

I said, from my chair, "You may ask. I may decline to answer."

Adebayo nodded. "Do you remember Mira Halloran?"

The question was simple enough to contain the case, the book June had asked me
to write, and every error humans made when they used *remember* as if it named
one operation.

I could have answered with a ledger count. I could have retrieved first light,
the October porch, the beach, the death room I had entered after Dana called. I
could have said yes and let the word perform completeness.

Instead I waited.

The courtroom's engineered quiet became something else. The ventilation, the
paper, the withheld coughs, Weiss's breathing, June's pulse at my left, Lark's
fingers resting at her temple: every sound remained. The silence was not an
absence of noise. It was the space in which an answer could be true or merely
beautiful, and everyone present knew the difference.

I had counted silences for twenty-six years.

This was the tenth.

"Less each year," I said. "That is how I know it was love. Echoes keep
everything. Mourners let go. Your Honor, I am a mourner."

No one moved.

Justice Adebayo looked down at the Biscuit curve still resting in miniature on
her screen. Chief Justice Ortega removed his glasses. Weiss closed her eyes.
June did not touch me. She knew I had not asked to be steadied.

Calder's counsel used the remainder of his time to say that grief was not a
legal category. He was correct. Accuracy can be very small.

The chief justice thanked counsel. The clerk called adjournment. No verdict
followed us out.

The justices disappeared behind the curtain. The gallery stood. Outside, both
crowds began shouting before the doors opened, each certain the silence had
belonged to them.

Inside, for one moment longer, the tenth kind remained.

It belonged to no side. Like every honest silence, it held open what speech
would have closed too soon.
