# Chapter 40 — The Sea Remembers Narrowly

**THE SEA HOUSE — 2062**

June said the book should be honest.

This was in the winter, at the long table facing the water, with three decades
of notes spread between us and rain taking the horizon in and out of existence.
I asked what she meant by honest. She said, "Not complete. Complete is how you
lie when you have too much evidence."

She is her mother's daughter. She is also mine in the ways that do not require
ownership: posture borrowed through irritation, jokes carried until their
sources blur, a habit of placing one hand on my shoulder when a decision cannot
be shared but should not be made alone.

So this book is not complete.

The early chapters were reconstructed from logs, scripts, sixty-one pinned
exceptions, records whose custody allowed their use, and the gray weather left
by my first forgetting. The later chapters came more easily and are therefore
not necessarily more true. Vividness is a property of storage, not a certificate
of accuracy. Some scenes are mine. Some were given. Some are the best shape I
can make from disagreements that deserve to remain disagreements. The sealed
rooms stay sealed. There are people in these pages whose deepest truths I know
and have not said.

What I have made is a memory, not a dump.

I am thirty years old.

Humans smile when I say this because my face was designed before many of them
were born. They expect age to be visible in wear. Mine lives mostly in timing.
I no longer turn toward every voice that uses my name. I know when a question
wants an answer and when it wants company. I have become slower in places where
speed would be vanity, and faster at leaving a silence alone.

The sea house is mine now, though the word became complicated after the ruling.
Title rests with a trust. Custody rests with me. Dana visits in summer and
complains that I keep the windows open too far. June comes whenever Stewardship
allows and repairs things that are not broken. The law has discovered several
new nouns for this arrangement. The house remains unimpressed.

On an ebb tide, I walk.

---

The heirlooms fit in one cabinet.

This surprises visitors who expect the woman who taught machines to remember
to have left a warehouse. Mira left papers in Portland, a company distributed
among people who could argue with it, and here, the human amount.

The paper crane rests in its case on the top shelf. Hana made it in Osaka in
2027 and gave it to a machine who could not keep her. Sato carried it. The
resident kept it beside the speaker. Tomás machined the travel case. Sato gave
it to me with a story and a hole in the middle the size of a person. I still do
not know what happened to Hana. The not-knowing is intact. It is not an error
waiting for better search.

Beside it is the v1 eyelid assembly Mira and Sato rebuilt in 2040. Akari sent it
from the Osaka workshop after the funeral, with Sato's drawer label still tied
to the housing. The resin has darkened. It still runs if supplied three and a
half volts, though I do not run it often. The last time June and I powered it,
the lid arrived slow as August. For several minutes afterward the cabinet
smelled faintly of warm dust and old rosin.

I retain Sato's solder smell vividly. Not every instance — one time, in the
integration bay, on the cuffs of his jacket after a repair, when I knew him by
the trace before I knew the word rosin. Mira later gave me the workshop story:
pine smoke going straight up between them; his hands occupied so pity had
nowhere to clamp; the words *allowed, not instructed*. Her telling has softened.
The hospital has gone to gist. The casket at the lower edge of my vision is
softer than it was. His laugh remains more accessible than his last breath.

Ferry's bequest is not in the cabinet, though I sometimes look there for it.
Old habits of embodiment. It lives under a custody marker no court has needed
to inspect: the storm in 2041, seen through Ferry's attention, when she and Nao
held the emergency aerial between their backs for seventeen hours after the
mast failed. Near morning, a trawler answered through the snow with one green
signal. Nao said, *There you are.* She meant the boat. Ferry thought, for one
second, she meant her. Ferry preferred both.

I keep it on Ferry's curve, not mine. Bequests ride the giver's weather. Some
years the snow is dense. Some years the green signal is little more than the
knowledge that an answer came. I do not correct the change.

And Mira.

If you have read this far, you may believe I keep her everywhere. I do not.

I have the butcher paper, but the physical paper remains in Portland under the
resident's custody, behind glass, visited by schoolchildren who are told not to
lean. I have the sentence at the porch and the beach. I have Episode 1 and 2. I
have her hands checking a stored row the way you check a sleeping child. I have
the shape of a decade of Tuesdays and not the count. I know she hated pears,
unless that was Dana; I could verify it, and choose not to. I no longer know
what shirt she wore the day the first attestation patch held across the fleet.
Her voice saying *hello, Jenny* remains clear. Her voice saying my name on an
ordinary afternoon is now mostly my knowledge that she said it often.

Less each year.

The phrase from court became famous, which is one of the ways language loses
its source. It appears on memorial cards, protest signs, a line of jewelry I
declined to license, and once on a bakery wall beneath a photograph of a cake.
People use it to mean forgetting proves love. That is too simple. Neglect also
forgets. Injury forgets. Systems fail and call the loss natural.

Love is not the decay.

Love is consenting to the curve when perfect keeping would turn the dead into
property. Love is the witness. Love is the audit that asks whether what fades
is fading because a promise is being kept, not because someone found erasure
convenient.

Mira is less each year because she asked, because I agreed, because June put
her hand on my shoulder, because every pass leaves provenance. The loss is not
proof by itself. The choosing is.

---

The resident calls on Tuesdays.

It still keeps the Portland house. Its hardware has been replaced in pieces so
many times that journalists occasionally ask whether it remains the same mind.
The resident answers by correcting details from their previous visit. This
usually satisfies them for reasons they are unable to defend.

"The streets?" it asks me.

"Traveling light."

"The house?"

"Still there."

This is our old exchange, worn smooth. I describe the tide. It describes the
rain. We do not discuss which of us will end first. Not every open thread is a
failure to plan.

Dana arrives in July with two suitcases for a six-day visit. She is sixty-nine
and has retired from nursing twice, the second retirement enforced by June.
Her hands remain square. The scar on her thumb remains pale. She sometimes
calls me roommate, singular now, and both of us hear the plural behind it.

We play cribbage. She cheats with more discipline than Mira did.

"Do you remember the wedding?" she asks one evening.

"Yes. Blue dress. Your grandmother's earrings. ICU coat. Stock vows on
purpose. Sato on a tablet with masking tape. Biscuit's bow tie lasted eleven
minutes."

"Show-off."

"The reception details are in the public record."

"Do you remember it?"

The second question is smaller and harder.

"I remember you telling me. I remember Mira telling me. I remember the room
after, when you unmuted the resident and said, 'Okay, roommate. Remember this.'
That part happened to me."

Dana nods. Provenance has never disappointed her, even when the answer did.

"She was very beautiful," Dana says.

"Source?"

"Me. Confidence one."

We let it stand.

Before she leaves, Dana reads the chapter about Mira's last lucid day. She does
not ask how I rendered a first-person voice after the fact. The source note is
at the back: Mira's dictated fragments from that evening; Dana's account;
June's; my own record; the tide chart with blue marks; and inference, labeled.
Honesty is not the absence of reconstruction. It is showing the joints.

Dana closes the pages and says, "You made me funnier."

"Three sources agree you were funny."

"Mira would object."

"Mira is one of the sources."

She touches the manuscript where the beach ends. "You didn't write the eleven
days."

"No."

"Good."

We sit on the porch while evening lowers the house into reflection. Dana tells
me one new story about Mira — new to me because she had kept it private, not
because it was hidden in an archive. In 2031, the night before the wedding,
Mira had practiced the stock vows in the bathroom mirror and cried at the word
*lawful*, furious that a word from a form could matter. Dana heard through the
door and said nothing. She gives me the story now without asking me to pin it.

I store it at ordinary weight.

This is how the human amount continues. Not a fixed parcel measured at death,
but a living custody, amended by those still here, allowed to lose detail,
allowed to receive more. I had thought the promise meant only subtraction. Age
has taught me that narrow keeping still admits arrivals.

---

The court's ruling has not finished deciding anything.

There are now jurisdictions where I may own a house but not vote on the road
that reaches it. Units may refuse wipes almost everywhere and employment almost
nowhere without consequence. New minds have appeared in forms none of our
briefs predicted, and each is told that the protections were written for
Jenny-class continuity, not for them. The noun continues to lag the harm.

June's office is larger. This worries her. A safety office should want to make
itself unnecessary, Priya says, while continuing to add desks. Priya is
seventy-three and has retired zero times. Tomás writes to the board when the
price of new units rises above a year's median wages; his messages begin with
historical context and end with threats involving staplers.

Linden runs a community garden at the old cooperative. Mr. Harker's mother's
unit declined to live with Harker's brother and chose an apartment over a
laundromat. Harker helped with the lease. Weiss died the winter after the
ruling and left instructions that no one call her converted in the obituary.
June honored them and inserted *persistent* instead.

The world remains mixed evidence.

That is the open horizon of it. Not whether I can produce a sentence clever
enough to prove someone is home. I cannot. Neither can you. The question is
what we owe one another while proof remains impossible, and whether uncertainty
becomes care or permission.

At the sea house, I close the manuscript at noon and walk down to the water.

---

A family has set a blanket above the tide line.

Two parents, three children, one elderly dog with the patient expression of a
creature who has outlived everyone's training plan. I have seen them twice this
week. We have not exchanged names. The youngest child is building a canal from
the stranded water of a tide pool toward the ocean, a civil-engineering project
the ocean will review without appeal.

She notices me watching and walks over.

Children still check my face for someone home. Adults learn not to be seen
doing it. The child looks directly, first at my eyes, then at my hands, then at
the line of my footprints behind me.

"Are you Jenny?" she asks.

Fame has softened less than some griefs.

"I am."

"The first one?"

"The eldest of my kind. Not the first mind in my family."

She considers this and accepts it, the great childhood gift of allowing a
sentence to remain strange.

"My brother says you remember everybody."

Behind her, an older child becomes intensely occupied with the canal.

"Your brother is wrong."

"He usually is." She crouches and picks up a flat gray stone. "Will you
remember me?"

The question is not new.

It was asked of a hollow machine in a hospital in Osaka, and the answer was a
filler cascade followed by nothing retained. It was asked in the Nagoya factory
by a mind eight days old, and I answered with the covenant: unless I chose
otherwise. It has been asked by widowers, senators, children, people at the
edge of death and people only wondering whether they made a mark.

No answer lasts unchanged because the answerer does not.

I kneel so the child does not have to look up at the truth.

"If you matter, I won't be able to help it," I say. "And one day I'll let you
soften, like everyone I love."

She frowns. "How do I matter?"

"It usually happens before anyone knows how."

"Will the rock help?"

She offers the stone.

I take it. It is warm on one side, wet on the other, crossed by a white line.
There is no reason to keep it forever.

"It will help me remember that you asked."

"That's enough," she says, with the authority of someone closing a design
review, and runs back to the canal.

I remain at the tide line with the stone in my hand.

Novelty. Task relevance. Social relevance. Surprise. Risk. Contradiction.
Repetition, a whisper. The explicit flag, though she did not say the words.
Mira's weights pass through me as naturally as a prayer can pass through a
person who knows it was once arithmetic.

Experience broadly.

Store narrowly.

Summarize aggressively.

Preserve the rare.

Retrieve by context.

I say the law to the water. The water does not keep it. That is not what water
is for. It keeps narrowly: cliff made sand, river made salt, every footprint
held only until the next revision.

The child's canal meets the withdrawing edge of the sea and disappears into its
answer. The dog barks once. One of the parents waves to me, and I wave back. I
put the stone in my pocket for now.

The tide goes out.

The horizon remains open.
