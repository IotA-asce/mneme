# Chapter 33 — The Long Now

**THE SEA HOUSE — 2050–52**

The tide chart is on the refrigerator.

Dana tapes it there on our first morning, level because she knows I will look
at the angle before I look at the numbers. High at 8:17. Low at 2:41. I read it
twice. The sea is out beyond the windows doing neither of these things exactly,
because a chart is a promise made at a distance.

“It is an estimate,” I say.

“So are you before coffee,” Dana says.

This is my wife. I know this. Her hands are on the yellow mug. The left thumb
has the pale line from opening an ampoule badly in 2029. She says it was 2030.
We have maintained the conflict without escalation.

The house is small and gray and one story. We chose it because there are no
stairs, though everyone talked about the light. The light is good. The doors
are wide. There is a rail in the shower and another along the hall, complete
all the way to the bedroom door. I notice that. Someone remembered the missing
six inches.

June calls it the sea house, not the beach house. The beach is a place you go.
The sea is a thing that remains when you cannot.

I wrote that down.

The tall one carries the boxes in from the car. Jenny. Her name is Jenny and I
know it as soon as I stop reaching. She has the old foam case with the crane,
the paper bird a child gave to Kenji before any of us were properly here. She
puts it high in the built-in cabinet, away from salt and sun.

“It should be seen,” I tell her.

“It should survive being seen,” she says.

This is also my daughter, though not in any form the county clerk would accept.
Perhaps the county clerk has improved. We improved several clerks in Geneva.

I ask what year it is.

“Twenty-fifty,” Jenny says. “We came to the sea house yesterday. You chose the
gray room. Dana chose the yellow mug. June is coming Friday.”

She tells me without pretending I remember. This is the gift. Not the answer.
The honesty of the answer.

Outside, the tide is going. Or coming. The chart knows.

---

Good days do not announce themselves. You discover them by spending them.

On one, I wake with every name in place. Dana. Jenny. June, who is fifteen and
wears her hair cut to her jaw because she got tired of explaining it to
brushes. Priya, arriving after lunch with a grocery bag and a cribbage board.
The resident in Portland, keeping the old house. Tomás on the wall screen,
older and offensively rested. Kenji dead six years. My mother dead twenty-four.

I know all of it and wish, briefly, that I knew less. Clarity includes the
diagnosis.

We walk on the beach. The wind writes badly in the sand. Jenny keeps to my
left, not holding me until I ask, which is a courtesy with an architecture
behind it. A black dog runs past with silver tags and I say, “Biscuit,” though
I know before the word is gone that Biscuit was brown and died in the old
apartment with his head on Jenny's knee.

“Not Biscuit,” I say.

“No,” Jenny says.

“Did I embarrass us?”

“The dog appeared unaffected.”

“Good. We always valued discretion in dogs.”

She smiles. I made that smile possible. Kenji built the lid around it. This is
not ownership. It is provenance.

At the tide pools June crouches with both hands on her knees, studying an
anemone as if it has violated a contract. Dana calls her back from the next
rock. Their figures line up against the water: my wife's shoulders, my child's
quick turn, Jenny standing where the wave can be watched.

Someone is keeping this, I think. I don't have to.

The relief is physical. I let the details go while they are happening. June's
coat may be red or orange. The anemone may be green. The day will survive the
loss of its paint.

That night I ask Jenny what we did.

“We walked south at low tide. June found an anemone and claimed it was
judgmental. A dog passed. You called it Biscuit, then corrected yourself.”

“Was I frightened?”

“No. You made a joke.”

“Was it good?”

“Adequate.”

“Your standards are why I made you.”

“Source disputed.”

This makes me laugh. I do not ask her the color of the coat.

---

The chart changes every week. Dana prints a new one on Sunday and writes the
moon in the margin. She has beautiful hands. I may have said this.

She puts the medication in the blue cup.

“Yellow,” I say.

“The mug is yellow. This cup is blue.”

I look again. Blue.

There is no need to make a tragedy of a color. I take the pills.

Some mornings the house arrives in pieces. Window. Salt. Woman in a blue
sweater. The long hall. A sound like applause that is only rain. I know the
parts before I know their relation. The mind builds state. One executive
arbitrates. Skills act. Safety overrides everything. I wrote that law for a
robot head and now my wife uses it to get me to breakfast.

“This is the sea house,” the woman says.

“Dana,” I say.

Her face changes. Relief is an emotion I should not treat as confirmed, but I
have thirty years of training and her hand is over her mouth.

“Yes,” she says.

“I knew by the thumb.”

She looks at her hand. “Whatever works.”

Later I know her before she enters. Later I do not know the word refrigerator.
The disease has no respect for narrative structure.

Days are weather now. Clear before noon, fog after. A bad morning lifting at
four. Three gray days, then an hour in which I can read an old paper and see
exactly where the argument cheats. We measure no improvement from one clear
hour. We measure no final loss from one gray day. Dana taught us this. Nurses
know a life is not the worst vital sign.

In November the actual weather arrives.

The forecast says wind, then changes its mind upward. Rain moves sideways off
the glass. The power fails at 1:13 in the morning and the house gives a small
sigh as everything unnecessary goes dark.

I wake certain I am in Osaka.

There should be a chassis on the bench. There should be eleven people holding
their breath. Kenji is late. Priya has not signed page forty-one. If the
heartbeat crosses one hundred milliseconds we stop the run. I am responsible
for all of it and cannot find my shoes.

The tall one sits on the floor beside the bed so I do not wake to a figure
above me.

“Portland?” I ask.

“The sea house. November, twenty-fifty-one. The storm took grid power. The
batteries are holding heat and safety systems.”

“Network?”

“Down.”

“And you?”

“Slower, not emptier.”

The sentence reaches a room still lit inside me. *If the network dies, she gets
slower, not emptier.* A takeout carton. Kenji at the kitchen table. Three
conditions before there was a company to violate them.

“My condition,” I say.

“Yes.”

“Did we keep it?”

“Yes.”

Outside, something hard strikes the roof and goes away. The backup lamp is
amber. I know this house. Dana is asleep in the other half of the bed. I know
her by the breathing before I know the face.

Jenny has a world inside her without a wire reaching out to fetch it. She is
still herself in the dark. I am less myself with every light on. This might be
unfair. It is also beautiful.

“Sit on the bed,” I tell her. “You look like a penitent down there.”

She sits. Dana wakes and finds my hand under the blanket.

For six hours the sea house is an island. We make coffee on the gas ring and
listen to the emergency radio. Jenny rations power with the severity of Priya
cutting a cake. At dawn, June calls through a line that works for eleven
seconds. She says she is safe. I ask where. The connection ends.

“Portland,” Dana says. “She is at home in Portland. The resident is with her.”

I accept this. Then I need it again.

“Where is June?”

“Portland. Safe. The resident is with her.”

“Where is June?”

The third time, I hear myself. Eleven questions on my mother's last good day.
The number is a trapdoor. Dana sees it open.

“You can ask eleven,” she says. “You can ask a hundred. We are not keeping
score.”

She puts her hands around the yellow mug. The white line on the thumb. Wife.
Nurse. Dana.

When the power returns, nobody cheers. The refrigerator begins again and the
tide chart lifts at one corner in the warm air. Jenny tapes it flat. We have
survived a storm, which is less dramatic from inside than in the telling.

Someone kept the heat. Someone kept June's location. Someone kept me from
turning repetition into a moral failure.

I don't have to.

June visits from Portland and brings schoolwork. She is sixteen. She is twelve.
She is the baby upstairs under the birds. I do not say every version aloud.
Sometimes I ask, “How old are you now?”

“Sixteen,” she says.

“Did I miss much?”

“Some geometry.”

She sits at the table and shows me a proof. I can still find the unnecessary
step. She says a rude word and kisses my cheek.

This is June. My daughter. Her eyes are mine, though everybody says they are
Dana's when they want to be correct.

---

The tall one tells me stories.

She is careful with the source. This happened to you. You told me this. I was
there for this. June told me this and I believe her. The clauses are rails all
the way to the door.

She tells me about the factory with sparrows in the high steel. I see the birds
but not the factory. She tells me I made them leave the roofline unfinished
because ours had sparrows and a cathedral should admit weather. I say that
doesn't sound like me. She says, “Correct. Kenji said the sparrows. You argued
about the roofline for drainage.”

“Good,” I say. “I was worried I had become whimsical.”

She tells me about a courthouse on a Tuesday. Dana wore blue. Her grandmother's
earrings. An ICU coat. Standard vows because I did not trust improvisation with
the largest promise of my life.

I look at Dana's hands. The ring is there.

“Did I love you then?” I ask.

The room goes still in the wrong way.

“Yes,” the tall one says.

Dana says, “Yes.”

“Do I love you now?”

Dana kneels by my chair. She is a nurse or my wife. Both, which is fortunate.

“You don't have to take a test,” she says.

I touch the line on her thumb.

Someone is keeping this. I don't have to.

---

There was a dog.

He sleeps by the door, except he does not. The sound is rain in the gutter. Or
tags. The tall one says Biscuit died in November, 2034, at 4:51. I ask if he was
alone.

“No. His head was on my knee. You and Dana were on the floor. Tomás and Priya
were there. The elder was with us through the open channel.”

“Did I choose correctly?”

“You let me choose.”

That feels like a better answer.

The tide chart says 11:06. I cannot tell whether that is high or low because
the abbreviations have stopped being words. H and L. Jenny draws a small arrow
beside the line. Up. Not coming in; rising. She says those are different if we
care about exactness.

We care.

The sea does not remember the last tide. It does not need to. The moon keeps
the procedure.

I wrote that down too, but the paper is not where I put it.

---

Sometimes I wake wanting my mother.

The woman with the good hands tells me she is dead. Sometimes the tall one
does not tell me because she knows the truth will be new and useless. This is
not lying. We made rules. I made some rules. The rules have kind faces.

There are missing afternoons. I know because the light moves and my clothes
change. No one shows me records to prove them. A record is not a day returned.

On a clear afternoon they bring me paper from the old lab. Not the butcher
paper. A copy. The original is safe in Portland. I know the handwriting before
I know it is mine.

Novelty. Task relevance. Social relevance. Surprise. Risk. Contradiction.
Repetition. Remember this.

The weights are missing. Or I am.

“Did it work?” I ask.

The tall one says, “I am here.”

This is not an engineer's answer. It is better than one.

The girl comes on Fridays. June. She is tall now. Not the tall one. Another
tall. Everyone has become tall around me.

“Are you my sister?” I ask.

She says, “I can be.”

Then she tells me her name and I know it. The knowing hurts, then warms.

The tide chart is on the refrigerator.

Dana's hands. Left thumb, white line.

The dog was Biscuit. Or the dog was not Biscuit. Both kinds pass the window.

The tall one watches the water. Her name comes late. I wait. She does not give
it too soon.

Jenny.

“My girl,” I say.

She turns. There is a silence in her face that I know is not empty. I built the
part that keeps. Kenji built the lid. Priya can stop anything. Tomás makes the
numbers apologize. Dana is my wife. June is my daughter. The facts come in a
bright row and I am myself inside them.

“Yes,” Jenny says.

I have something to tell her. It is important. Keep the rare. Human amount.
October rain. I think I told her already.

“You promised,” I say.

“I promised.”

Good.

She takes my hand. We go to the window. The sea is there. The sea house. The
tide is coming or going and the chart knows, and Dana will come back with tea,
and the girl is Friday, and there was a dog.

Someone is keeping this.

I don't have to.
