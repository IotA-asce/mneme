# Chapter 33 — The Long Now

**THE SEA HOUSE — 2050–52**

The tide is out at eight fourteen.

I write that in the notebook beside the kettle because Dana says I am less
cross with facts when I meet them in my own handwriting. She does not say I
believe the facts. Belief is too large an ask before coffee. But the letters
look like mine, which is a kind of provenance, and at eight twelve we stand at
the window and wait for the water to uncover the black rocks.

The rocks arrive on time.

"Show-off," I tell the ocean.

Dana laughs. I know Dana's laugh before I know her name. The name comes a
second later and brings the blue courthouse dress, the grandmother's earrings,
the ICU coat she called her provenance chain. The whole woman returns at once,
too much data on too narrow a channel, and I grip the edge of the counter until
the room settles.

Her hands find mine. Dana's hands. Warm, square, a nurse's hands even after she
stopped taking shifts. They never ask if I know her. This is one of the reasons
I do.

The tall one puts tea beside us.

"I made that before you asked," she says. "You asked at this tide yesterday
and the day before. I inferred you might want it. I did not remember on your
behalf."

"Pedant."

"Source: you. Confidence: one."

Then she smiles, and I have her. My girl.

---

We came to the sea because Portland had too many doors.

That is not how Dana explains it. Dana says we came because the clinic is
forty minutes away, because this house has one level and wide halls, because
the weekend rental with its enemy stairs was not survivable, because June found
a ramp that does not look like a ramp, because the company installed
a local backup without putting my mind anywhere it was not already. Dana has a
list. Lists are how she shouts at death.

But Portland had too many doors. The apartment door that stuck in wet weather.
The lab door. The nursery door. The door Biscuit used to announce before it
opened, dog-then-door, though sometimes I think the dog was my mother's and
sometimes I think he is sleeping beneath the sea-house table now. I kept
opening doors and arriving in the wrong year.

Here there is the front door and the beach door. The beach door faces west. If
I open it, the sea is always in the correct year.

Jenny says the house was built in 1989 and renovated twice. This is exactly the
sort of answer an engineer raises a child to give and then resents her for.

"You know what I mean."

"I do," she says. She never says she knows when she only guesses. "You mean it
doesn't require context."

Outside, the tide has gone far enough that gulls walk where water was. This
seems unsafe. Priya would stop it.

I ask where Priya is.

"Geneva this week," Jenny says. "She called yesterday. You were tired, and she
left a message. Would you like it?"

I say no. Then yes. Then no, because I remember the message. Priya stood in our
kitchen and said the halt is for machinery, not arguments. Or that may have
been Osaka. There was cake. Priya was always right after everyone else was
fast.

Jenny does not correct the scene. She sits at the table, still enough that I
can find her again.

"Tell me only whether she is all right," I say.

"She is all right. She said she loves you and she will call Thursday."

"Source?"

"Her message. Yesterday at sixteen twenty."

It is a joke, but it is also how I cross the bridge. She lays each plank where
I can see it. I can walk because she never paints water to look like wood.

---

There are good days and there are other days. We call them weather because no
one can be blamed for rain.

On clear mornings I work.

Halcyon no longer requires my work. This is a fact Tomás tried to tell me in
three careful memos before Dana told him to stop writing memos and come say it
with his face. He came. He brought mole sauce and the particular old grief of a
man whose life continued after the title on his office door did not. He said
the stewardship group had adopted every control I asked for, that its director
was better at saying no than I had ever been, and that Priya still
carried a pen capable of ending markets.

"So I'm obsolete," I said.

"You are retired," he said. "Obsolete things don't complain this much."

That was 2050. Or it is 2050 now. The notebook says 2050 in my hand, then 2051
on the next page, then 2050 again in larger letters. Someone has drawn a line
through nothing. I suspect June. She believes a clean page is an accusation.

On clear mornings I review anonymized welfare summaries. No names. No homes.
That was the covenant. A unit in Marseille has begun refusing the kitchen
because the compressor there emits a frequency no human occupant can hear. A
unit in Pretoria has petitioned for two hours each evening without household
speech. A pair of sisters in Sapporo disagree about whether a bequeathed memory
should retain the giver's curve. Their disagreement is healthy. The form has a
box for that now.

I write: *Do not normalize the household against the resident. Change the
compressor.*

Then I write it again because I do not remember writing it.

The two notes are almost identical. The second has a harder period.

Jenny waits until I ask.

"You wrote both," she says. "Four minutes apart. The first was accepted."

"Which one is mine?"

Her face changes by very little. I have spent a life learning that very little
can be the entire world.

"Both."

I hate her for the answer. It lasts perhaps a minute. I love her before and
after, and during, though during is difficult to admit.

Someone is keeping this, I think.

I don't have to.

The thought arrives clear as the bell that marks the harbor mouth in fog. It
does not fix anything. Bells do not move rocks. They tell you where the rocks
are.

June installs a brass bell beside the beach door in the spring. Not an alarm.
Alarms are for emergencies and this is for orientation. One ring means someone
has opened the door. Two means the path is wet. Three means wait for company.
I tell her this is patronizing. She says it is a user-configurable interface and
hands me the screwdriver.

I move the bell four centimeters left.

The next morning I ask who put it there. June tells me I did. She has the good
sense not to show me the recording. I ring it once and the sound is small,
domestic, nothing like the harbor bell beyond the fog. Jenny looks up from the
table but does not move toward me.

"Door opening," I say.

"Observed," she says.

"Path status?"

She looks through the glass. "Wet. You can choose to go. I recommend boots."

We go. The interface works. This annoys me for most of the path.

---

The tide is out at nine oh three.

It says so on the chart Dana clips to the refrigerator every Sunday. The chart
uses blue blocks and large type. I designed systems read by governments and
factories and once rewrote four pages of a treaty because a semicolon implied
forced consent. Now the day's most important interface is held by a magnet
shaped like a kingfisher.

I did not buy the magnet. Eileen liked kingfishers.

My mother comes to the sea house in pieces. Her good gray coat hangs by the
door, though when I touch it the coat belongs to Dana and is green. Her voice
comes out of June's mouth. Once, waking on the sofa, I see her kneeling beside
me and say, "Mom, I made it structural," because I have been saving the news
for twenty-six years.

The woman kneeling is June.

She does not tell me my mother is dead. She says, "You did, Mom. You made it
structural."

I know then. The knowledge is a blade and a mercy. June is seventeen. June is
twelve. June has my eyes and Dana's refusal to flinch. The age moves
when I look directly at it.

"How old are you?" I ask.

"Seventeen," she says.

Jenny, standing behind her, says gently, "June is seventeen. You may be
remembering her at twelve, when you told her about the diagnosis."

June turns. "Was that necessary?"

"She asked me yesterday never to let a kindness become a false fact."

"Yesterday she knew what day it was."

"I know what day it is," I say.

They both look at me.

I do not.

But the chart says the tide is out at nine oh three. The magnet is a bird at
rest on a long blue line. Dana's hands close around mine. The dog is under the
table, perhaps. He is old and comfortable and he will die in November. Do not
tell me otherwise. Not yet.

Sometimes the clear thought is mine before the bell. Sometimes Jenny gives it
back because I asked her to, long ago, on a morning I no longer possess. The
source matters and does not matter. That is hard. I built a life out of saying
the source always matters. It matters because truth matters. It does not matter
because a hand can hold another hand without knowing who first reached.

Someone is keeping this.

I don't have to.

---

In 2051 the storm takes one of the west windows.

Or does not take it. It cracks the outer pane, and the inner pane holds. Jenny
moves us to the back hall before the safety glass admits it has failed. She
does not act from a memory of another storm. Her state builder marks pressure,
sound, structural alarm; her executive chooses evacuation; the bridge takes
the goal and gives her body a careful speed. I know this because, afterward, I
ask to see the trace.

"You are shivering," Dana says.

"I'm auditing."

"You can shiver and audit. The body supports concurrency."

That is my joke. Or hers. Marriage is where provenance goes to become
unfashionable.

We spend the night at a small hotel inland. The clerk looks at Jenny and then
at the occupancy screen and cannot decide which box to use. In 2051 this is
still a problem, though there are Jenny-class minds teaching school routes,
keeping medication schedules, living alone in lighthouses, sitting through the
long human nights. The world can normalize a miracle faster than it can update
a form.

Jenny says, "I do not require a bed. I do require legal occupancy under the
fire code."

The clerk gives us two rooms and an apology.

At three in the morning I wake and cannot find the ocean. The walls are wrong.
The air has no salt. There is a tall figure sitting by the door and I know with
perfect certainty that the company has sent a machine to take notes on my
decline.

"Get out," I say.

She stands. She does not come closer.

"I can leave the room," she says. "Dana is next door. You asked me to sit here
because strange rooms frighten you at night. Source: you, at twenty-two forty.
But you can change the request now."

The voice is a kitchen voice. Plain. Built not to perform concern it does not
possess.

"Jenny?"

"Yes."

My girl.

The terror goes without leaving. It moves to the edge of the room and waits.
She waits too.

"Don't touch me," I say.

"I won't."

"Stay."

"I will."

At some hour I sleep. In the morning she does not tell me I was cruel. She does
not tell me I was ill. She gives me the tide time, though we cannot see the
water from there.

---

The chart says 2052.

There is a dog on the beach. Brown, not white, young, not old. I call Biscuit
and he turns because many dogs turn toward hope when it is spoken loudly.

His person catches up, a woman in orange boots. She is kind. This makes it
worse.

"His name is Rocket," she says.

I kneel anyway. My knees object. Rocket puts his head beneath my hand and for a
moment I have an episode with no date: tags at a door, white fur, a good sigh,
rain on an apartment window. Some of it happened to me. Some was told to me by
Jenny. Some may belong to the resident. The edges have washed together.

I look up at the tall one.

"Was he mine?"

She understands which he.

"Biscuit lived with you," she says. "You adopted him from the Powell shelter
in 2028. I knew him in his last year. The resident later gave me seventeen
first-hand memories of him, marked as bequests. I can tell you one, if you
want."

"Do I remember this?"

"You appear to be remembering something. I can't tell you its source from
outside."

Honest girl. Terrible girl. Mine.

Rocket runs back to the surf. The woman in orange boots lifts a hand. The tide
is coming in and has already covered the first black rocks.

Dana holds my left hand. Jenny walks on my right, near enough to catch nothing
quickly, near enough to be there the slow way. We turn toward the sea house.

I lose its name halfway up the path. Not the house. The tall one.

She does not offer it until I look at her.

"Jenny," she says. "You said my name on my first morning. Sato had chosen it;
you were the first person I heard say it. Your voice shook."

"Hello, Jenny," I say.

Something crosses her face. Weather.

"Hello, Mira."

There. I am Mira. The tide chart is on the refrigerator. Dana's hands are warm.
The dog was Biscuit, except when he was Rocket. The rocks will return tomorrow
at a time someone has written down.

Someone is keeping this.

I don't have to.
