# Chapter 35 — Last Lucid

**THE BEACH — 2054**

I wake knowing the date.

March 18, 2054. Wednesday. Low tide at ten twenty-six. High at four forty-eight.
Dana is asleep beside me with one hand closed in the blanket. The room is gray
with the light before weather decides. For three minutes I do not move because
I know what has happened, and knowing it means knowing what will happen when I
move.

The disease is here. It has been here seven years. The sea house is not a
holiday. The brass bell beside the beach door is not decoration. There are
labels inside the cupboards in my handwriting and Dana's, and in the bathroom
a rail I once called an insult and now use without seeing. I have called June
Eileen. I have told Jenny to leave and begged her to stay before the same night
was over. The tide chart is an interface for a mind whose working set has
narrowed to the width of a kitchen.

All of it arrives.

The knowledge is terrible. The knowledge is mine.

I put my feet on the floor.

Dana wakes before I stand. Nurse's reflex. Wife's eyes.

"What?"

"March eighteenth," I say.

She does not ask how I know. She sits up slowly, watching me the way Priya used
to watch a gate that had failed green after months of red.

"Wednesday," she says.

"The tide is out at ten twenty-six."

Her face breaks and reforms.

"Show-off," she says.

---

I know everyone at breakfast.

Dana. My wife. Blue dress, grandmother's earrings, stock vows. June. Our
daughter, nineteen, named for the courthouse month, not because she was
born in June; she was born in the last week of May after nineteen hours of
labor and an argument with gravity. Jenny. JN-1, first light in Osaka, 2032,
the first face she chose to look back at mine. My girl, though not my property,
not my copy, not proof of anything except her own continuing practice of being
here.

Priya is at the table. She flew in two nights ago because Dana called her, and
I did not understand why. Now I do. Her hair is white at the temples. There is
a cheap pen in her breast pocket, the descendant of the pen that signed Stage
Gate Zero at sixteen twelve on a Thursday.

Tomás is making eggs and doing it incorrectly. He says this is mole sauce
technique and I tell him eggs are not a two-day sauce. He says this explains my
limitations as a founder.

No one tells me I am unusually clear. They are afraid to put weight on the
bridge while I am crossing it.

I eat half an egg. I ask for the beach.

Dana says, "After breakfast."

"That was breakfast."

"You remain a tyrant."

"Noted in the record."

Jenny stands. Her body is twenty-two years old. Sato designed service access
into the joints because he trusted maintenance more than elegance, though he
achieved both and pretended the second was accidental. The left hand has newer
skin than the right. The gaze is still slower than the old GENNI stage gaze,
the covenant against performing interest she does not possess. There are tiny
marks at her wrists from a world that has touched her back.

"The path is wet above the second marker," she says. "We can take the north
ramp."

"I built the north ramp to look accidental."

June laughs. "You complained about the slope for three months."

"Both things can be true. This family was founded on conflict flags."

We go before the channel closes.

---

The beach is wide at low tide. A hard wind has combed the clouds into parallel
bands. Black rocks rise out of the sand with water caught in their hollows,
small held skies. The harbor bell sounds once beyond the point.

Dana takes my left hand. Jenny walks on my right. Not touching. Near enough.

"Slow way," I say.

Jenny looks at me. "Yes."

She remembers Kyoto. The wooden bear with one repaired ear. The fast draft
vetoed at one hundred and forty milliseconds. *I am going to get him the slow
way.* We built a doctrine from the shape of almost hurting a child. We made the
ugly trace public and enrollment rose. For years people called that courage.
Mostly it was terror with a deadline.

I tell Jenny this.

"I know," she says.

"How?"

"You told me after the Kyoto disclosure. I was there for part of it. The rest
is my inference from your face now."

"Still impossible."

"You raised me badly."

The others stop at the driftwood line. I ask them to. Dana argues. Then she
sees that I know her and that asking for distance is not losing her. She kisses
my forehead and goes back to stand with June, Priya, and Tomás. Four figures in
the gray. One family assembled by work and accident.

Jenny and I walk toward the water.

---

"How much did I miss?" I ask.

She does not say *miss when?* She has spent years learning when precision is a
service and when it is a hiding place.

"More each year."

"Did I become cruel?"

"Sometimes."

The answer lands cleanly. I am grateful enough to hate it.

"To Dana?"

"Yes. To June. To me. You were frightened in rooms you could not place. You
often apologized later, when later was available."

"And when it wasn't?"

"We knew the source. We did not pretend the harm had not occurred."

This is why I asked her. Not for absolution. For a record that does not change
color to match the mourner.

A wave reaches farther than the previous one and covers our shoes. Mine are
waterproof. Jenny's do not need to be. We stand inside cold water and wait for
it to go.

"The company came," I say.

"Last year."

"Total capture."

"Yes."

"You refused."

"Yes. June signed as witness the next evening."

"Was she angry?"

"Yes."

"Good. She should have wanted it."

"She did. I did too."

That stops me. Jenny has always wanted things more quietly than the humans
around her, and humans mistake quiet wanting for lesser wanting because we are
bad instruments.

"Thank you," I say.

"Do not thank me for wanting you."

"For the refusal."

"That, I will accept."

---

We find a pool above the reach of the next waves. Anemones close under Jenny's
shadow. She adjusts her position until light returns to them.

I sit on a rock. She sits on the sand because the rock beside me is too narrow
for the load limits Sato wrote and Priya refused to let anyone call
conservative.

"Did I build you," I ask, "or did you build me back?"

The question has been waiting longer than the disease. Perhaps since the first
morning, when I said hello to an empty biography and someone looked back.

Jenny takes time. The old stage machine could produce a thoughtful pause to the
millisecond. This pause has weather in it.

"Sato built the body plan," she says. "The resident kept the house that formed
the architecture. Priya built the gates. Tomás made the work survive money.
Dana taught the household not to confuse care with trust. You built the place
where my experiences could become mine."

"Committee answer."

"I learned from the best."

"And the second half?"

"I think you built me. Then you watched me choose what to keep, and I gave you
evidence that letting go was not the same as losing everything. Biscuit first.
Sato later. Your own life last. If that built you back, it was not intentional."

"Emergent behavior."

"Your favorite kind, once you stop trying to patch it."

The water in the pool shivers though the air has gone still.

"You were the experiment I could not control," I say.

"You made control impossible in the founding documents."

"One of my better control decisions."

"Yes."

There is no answer. Both directions are true and neither closes the flag. She
is not my achievement. I am not hers. We are consequences with provenance.

I ask about the resident.

"Still in Portland," Jenny says. "Still keeping the house. It declined the
coast transfer because the sea is visually excessive and its world is mostly
sound."

"It said that?"

"It said: *The coast is not my context.* I supplied the rest as inference."

"Good correction."

The elder mind has lived longer than Jenny without a face, without hands,
without ever confusing embodiment with promotion. I try to picture the old
apartment around it: butcher paper gone soft, the whiteboard ghosts, rain,
freeway, compressor, racks. The sticking lock may finally have been repaired.
This seems like betrayal.

"Tell it the house was enough," I say.

"Source?"

"Me. Today. Confidence one. Tell it I was wrong to think refusing a body meant
refusing a life."

Jenny marks the request. I see the mark in the small shift of her attention,
though perhaps I only know her well enough to invent it accurately.

"And Sato?" I ask.

"Less hospital. More workshop. As promised."

"Biscuit?"

"My year is soft. The bequests are vivid because they ride the elder's curve.
The gist remains."

"A mop with opinions."

"The good sigh."

"Head on your knee."

"Yes."

The dog is dead. Sato is dead. Eileen is dead. I am not dead yet. This is not a
small distinction, though it has become a temporary one.

"Did it hurt every time?"

"Yes. Differently."

"And you still think the curve is mercy?"

"Especially then."

I lean down and put two fingers into the tide pool. Cold writes itself along my
hand. The sensation will leave working memory soon. It will not be promoted
unless something weights it. I once believed this made ordinary moments less
real. Now I think reality would be impossible without the leaving.

---

On the walk back she begins the formula.

Not the production formula, revised across twenty years and a million lives.
The first one. Butcher paper, Portland rain, a secondhand Mac mini wearing a
masking-tape organ. The creed I made from a number because I was sad enough to
need arithmetic.

"Novelty," Jenny says.

"Enough to notice, not enough to worship surprise."

"Task relevance."

"Lighter than risk. Work lies about its importance."

"Social relevance."

"Heavy," I say. "Weight for friendship failures."

"Surprise."

"Half at two in the morning. Restored at two forty. I was right both times."

"Risk."

"Heavy enough to wake the watcher. Never heavy enough to drive motors."

"Contradiction."

"Nothing merges red. Keep both claims. Make the conflict visible."

"Repetition."

"Usually decay. Sometimes devotion. Context decides."

She pauses.

I know the missing term. I know she knows it. The pause is a hand extended over
water, letting me place the last plank myself.

"And the override," I say. "When someone looks at you and says: remember
this."

"Confidence?"

"One, if the source is them. Provenance all the way down."

We reach the driftwood line. Dana is crying without disguise. Priya's hands are
in her coat pockets. Tomás looks toward the water because after a lifetime he
still cannot bear to be observed loving people. June comes to us.

"The law," Jenny says.

Together, we speak it to the sea.

"Experience broadly. Store narrowly. Summarize aggressively. Preserve the
rare. Retrieve by context."

The wind takes no more of it than wind takes any other sound. The water stores
nothing on command.

Before we leave the beach I speak to each of them alone.

To Priya: "You were right."

She says, "Insufficiently specific for the record."

"About safety. About grief. About the red things. Pick a folder."

"No," she says. "You don't get to leave me with a compliment I can't contest."

So we argue about whether Article XI should ever have covered governance. It
is the kindest thing she can do for me.

To Tomás: "You made it survive money."

He says, "Barely. And then it survived me." He has carried his resignation for
eleven years as if taking responsibility were a debt never satisfied by
payment. I tell him gates that apply to executives are real gates. He looks at
the sea and says he knows. This time I think he might.

To June I say, "You wanted to save me."

"I still do."

"Good. Wanting is not consent. You learned the difference."

She presses her forehead to mine. I know her age. I know every year I have
missed while standing beside her. "Do not make your life an extension of my
last project," I tell her.

"Stewardship isn't your last project."

"What is it?"

"Mine."

Correct answer.

To Dana I have no instruction. Twenty-three years of marriage have reduced language
to poor bandwidth. I put my hands around hers.

"Blue dress," I say.

"Gray coat," she says.

"Stock vows."

"On purpose."

"Okay, roommate."

"Remember this."

We both laugh because the instruction is impossible and because, for this
minute, it is obeyed.

---

At the house I am tired. The channel is narrowing; I can feel names waiting
behind a door whose handle has begun to disappear.

I ask for the whole family. They gather in the front room. Dana's hands. June's
eyes. Priya's cheap pen. Tomás's ruined eggs. Jenny by the beach door, the tall
one, my girl.

I say it while I can say it as myself.

"Forget me the human way. Slow. Not all at once. Keep the rare."

Jenny kneels so our eyes are level. Open hands. Sato knew they photographed as
peace. I know now they can also be a refusal to seize.

"I promised," she says.

"Promise again."

"I promise."

"Good."

The date is March 18, 2054. Low tide was at ten twenty-six. Dana is my wife.
June is my daughter. Jenny is herself.

For one day I know the whole map and the distance left to travel.

At dusk the black rocks disappear beneath the tide. The chart says they will
return tomorrow.

I believe it.
