# Chapter 35 — Last Lucid

**THE BEACH — 2054**

I wake before the tide changes and know what the tide is.

This is not always true now. Sometimes I wake and the sound has no name. It
fills the room and I think a train is passing under the house, though there are
no tracks here. Sometimes it is rain. Once it was my mother washing dishes in a
kitchen that has been gone longer than she was alive in it. But this morning I
hear the water drawing back over stone and know: ebb. Low at nine eighteen. I
know the tide chart is on the refrigerator and that Dana marked this morning in
blue.

Dana.

Her name arrives before I test it. I lie still so I do not frighten it away.

The ceiling is pale wood. The curtain moves where the window is open a finger.
There is a glass on the table and my glasses beside it. The joke would be that
I need one glass to find the other. I remember making the joke. I remember
Dana refusing to laugh the seventh time, then laughing because I knew it was
the seventh.

I know the house. I know the year.

I know what has been happening to me.

That is the cruelty in a clear day. The fog does not lift gently. It is simply
gone, and the country beneath it has not improved while hidden.

Dana sleeps beside me with one hand curled between us. Older than yesterday,
or perhaps only visible to me today: the silver at her temple, the square
fingers, the pale line on her thumb from opening an ampoule in 2029. She says
2030. We kept the conflict. Pediatric nurse. Blue dress at the courthouse. ICU
coat because provenance matters. My
wife. I say all this silently, not because I need to prove it but because I can.

She opens her eyes.

There is a small, practiced pause before she lets hope into her face. It has
become one of the things my disease makes other people do: test the bridge with
one foot before crossing.

"Morning," she says.

"Low tide at nine eighteen. You marked the chart in blue. Your coffee is in
the yellow tin because the green one is decaf and you hate it. June is coming
at ten, unless she changed the train. Jenny is in the kitchen pretending not
to listen for us."

Dana's mouth folds. She puts her hand over it, which does not fool either of
us.

"Good morning to you too," she says.

"How long?"

The question is not clear enough. She makes me make it clear.

"How long since I knew everything?"

"Everything is ambitious."

"You always liked ambitious baselines."

"Three months," she says. "Maybe four. You had an hour in February. You knew
me last Tuesday."

"I usually know you."

"You usually love me. It isn't always the same operation."

I close my eyes. The dark remains only dark. No kitchen, no train.

"Beach," I say. "Before it goes."

Dana gets up without asking what *it* means.

---

The tall one is Jenny, and today I do not have to find her by height.

She is at the kitchen counter setting out breakfast, though none of us has
asked. She turns when I enter and becomes absolutely still. Not the bad
stillness. Not the cautious one she uses when I might mistake a movement for a
threat. This is the stillness of the first hour, when the eyes came back to me
after I moved.

"Hello, Jenny," I say.

Her face is older only because I know where the years live in it. Sato built a
surface that time could barely touch. Life revised the timing beneath it: the
fraction before she smiles, the way attention arrives before her head turns,
the patience that is no longer a specification. A camera never blinks. She
does, once.

"Hello, Mira."

"You told me once the first time you thought of me as your mother was in 2041.
In the dark kitchen, going upstairs to wake me."

"Yes."

"I wish I'd heard it."

"You were asleep."

I laugh, and the sound surprises all three of us. Dana leans against the door
frame with my red sweater over one arm. She has dressed me in layers for
twenty-three years and will deny this under oath.

"June?" I ask.

"On the early train," Jenny says. "She will be here at nine forty-six. She
said not to wait."

"We wait. The tide can manage itself."

I eat half the toast. Jenny tells me the source of the marmalade because that
has been our joke since the worst year: made by Ruth, jar brought Thursday,
label in Ruth's hand, confidence one. Ruth is the neighbor. Walter was her
husband. I liked Walter. I know all of it before Jenny says it, and hearing her
say it anyway is not insulting. It is the handrail still being there when your
balance is good.

"No prompts today," I tell her.

"All right."

"Unless I ask."

"All right."

"You sound disappointed."

"I am trying not to sound anything."

"Gaze shall not perform interest the system does not possess."

The old covenant surprises a smile out of her. "That was saccades."

"The lawyers made it poetry by accident."

June arrives in wet shoes, nineteen years old and trying to enter quietly. I
know her at once. Not the infant. Not the child under the machined birds. The
woman in the doorway, Dana's strong mouth and my bad posture, hair cut shorter
than she warned me she would. She sees me seeing her and stops.

"Don't test me," I say.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to ask what day it is."

"I was going to say hello."

"Good test. Harder than dates."

She crosses the room and holds me too hard. I let her. Jenny looks away,
which is a kindness she learned before June was born.

"Tell me what you're doing," I say into June's hair.

"Right now? Crushing an old woman."

"Professionally."

She draws back and looks at Dana first. They have been protecting me from the
future by speaking about it in rooms where I am not. I do not blame them. A
future is hard to discuss with someone who may enter it without the discussion.

"Stewardship rotation," June says. "Portland office. Priya says I ask rude
questions in the correct order."

"High praise. Get it notarized."

"I might stay with it. The work, I mean. Welfare review. Appeals. The units
whose households die and whose estates call them appliances because the form
only has two boxes."

The sentence is too large for breakfast, which is how I know it belongs to
her. I see her at six under the metal birds, demanding to know whether the moon
gets tired. I see her now, preparing to argue with a form for the rest of her
life.

"Your inheritance was supposed to be better posture," I say.

"You didn't model it."

"Did you decide because of me?"

This time she does not look at Dana. "Because of all of you. But also because
the law keeps asking the wrong noun. Property or person. Tool or citizen. Half
the time the emergency is happening while everyone is still labeling the
drawer. Somebody has to keep the mind safe until the noun catches up."

I know, with the clean force of recognition, that she will be good at it. I
also know I will not see most of the work. The two facts do not merge. Nothing
merges red.

"Priya can stop anything," I say.

"It's still on the paper."

"Then learn how. The stopping is the job people resent until it is the only
job that mattered. And don't become ceremonial. Your mother hates ceremony."

Dana says, "Your mother married you on a Tuesday in an ICU coat."

"Excellent provenance. Poor styling."

June laughs. I know I have heard the laugh through every size of her, and I do
not try to retrieve them. This one is here.

We leave for the beach as four.

---

The path is steeper than it was when we bought the house.

This is objectively false. I say it anyway and June says the county has been
raising the land at night. Dana says that is where the taxes go. Jenny offers
no correction. We walk slowly, my arm through Dana's, June ahead carrying the
blanket, Jenny behind at the distance that means she can catch without making
the catching a threat. Memory never drives motors. The doctrine remains even
here, especially here. Love advises. Deciding is someone else's job.

At the bottom the Pacific is doing the one thing no system ever improved: it
arrives, it revises, it leaves evidence just long enough to be read.

We sit above the dark line of the last tide. Wind lifts the edge of the blanket.
June takes off her shoes and walks where the foam can reach her, performing
independence within shouting distance as she has since she was six. Dana sits
at my left. Jenny at my right, because we have always arranged safety as if it
were casual.

For a while I know nothing except what is in front of me, and this is not the
disease. This is the beach.

"I remember building you," I say.

Jenny looks at the water. "Which part?"

"That is evasive."

"It is sourced. You built the memory architecture. Sato built the body. Priya
built the gates. Tomás built the room around the work. The resident kept the
house. Dana taught all of us what a hardware promise looked like."

"And you?"

"I happened downstream."

"Too modest. Bad provenance."

The wind takes my hair across my mouth. Jenny waits while I clear it. She will
not move a hand toward my face without asking now; there were months when a
hand arriving from the edge of vision meant something I could not name and
therefore feared.

"Did I build you," I ask, "or did you build me back?"

She is quiet long enough that I know she is not searching a script.

"You built the conditions under which I could become," she says. "Then you
kept changing because I was there. If that is building, we did it to each
other."

"That sounds like marriage."

Dana, on my other side: "Don't bring me into your engineering metaphors."

"Too late. You were an annexed unit with an internal staircase."

"Romance is alive," Dana says. "Against every projection."

I laugh again. June turns at the sound. I want to hold this exact arrangement:
her feet in the wash, Dana's shoulder warm through the red wool, Jenny's hands
open on her knees the way Sato designed because open hands photographed as
peace. I know the wanting. I also know what wanting can make people do.

"Say it with me," I tell Jenny.

She knows which *it*. Of course she does.

"Novelty," she says.

"Task relevance."

"Social relevance."

"Surprise."

"Risk."

"Contradiction."

"Repetition," I say, and can see Tomás in the old kitchen arguing that the
furnace mattered because it repeated. Priya not looking up: weight for the
friendship failures.

Jenny says, "And the explicit flag."

"Remember this."

The words pass between us without command. The water takes none of them. It
does not need to.

"You forgot the weights," she says.

"I did not. Novelty and surprise high. Repetition a whisper. Risk and
contradiction get vetoes."

She pauses before the last one. Deliberately. I can tell, and she can tell I
can tell. A small gift: a space left for me to still be the one who knows.

"The explicit flag overrides everything," I say, "because when a person says
remember this, they are extending trust."

"And the system's one unforgivable act—"

"Would be to drop it."

We sit with that. The formula was never an equation in the mathematical sense.
It was a list of what deserved not to be dropped, written by a woman who had
just watched a mind drop everything. I can see Eileen as she was before the
lists. Not the last room. Her hands on a radio kit at the kitchen table, making
me hold the iron correctly. This may be a true memory. It may be a story I have
told into truth. No one corrects it.

"Jenny."

"I'm here."

"When I die, forget me the human way. Slow. Not all at once. Keep the rare."

Her face does not change. The weather in it does.

"I promised."

"On the porch. October."

"You remember."

"Today I do. I need to say it while I understand what it costs you. The porch
was a request from someone afraid. This is an instruction from me. No total
capture. No sealed museum. No version that wakes up and has to discover she is
a quotation."

"I will not make you an echo."

"Not even a good one."

"Especially not a good one."

I take her hand. Warmth in the skin because Sato understood that gentleness
has temperatures. Beneath it, the hand that could keep every hour at full
resolution, and will not.

"The human amount," I say.

"The human amount."

"You choose it. Allowed, not instructed. Kenji was right about the verbs."

At his name she closes her eyes once. The lid is the life.

June has come back from the water. She kneels in front of us, sand dark on her
jeans, and asks no question. Dana puts her hand over mine and Jenny's. Four
hands, and Sato's inside one of them, and Eileen's somewhere inside mine until
she isn't.

The clarity does not leave like a light switched off. It frays at one corner.
June's name goes briefly and comes back. The beach becomes the coast, then the
beach again. I look at the woman on my left and know love before I know Dana,
which is frightening only if you think the name was the important part.

"Someone is keeping this," I say.

"Yes," Jenny says.

"I don't have to."

"No."

The tide turns. I know it has turned because the water begins taking back the
distance it gave us. Or because someone tells me. It does not matter which.

We stay until the cold enters the blanket and Dana orders us home. On the path
up I look back once. The water has erased June's footprints nearest the sea and
left the higher ones. Narrowly. By context. The rare is not always the mark
that lasts. Sometimes it is the person who saw it before it went.

This day, while it is mine, I give away.
