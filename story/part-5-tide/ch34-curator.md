# Chapter 34 — Curator

**THE SEA HOUSE — 2053**

Mira asked for the wedding every morning in March.

I told it according to the weather of the question. On clear mornings she
wanted the whole chain: June 2031, Multnomah County Courthouse, a Tuesday;
Dana in blue and her grandmother's earrings; stock vows chosen on purpose;
Sato attending by tablet and holding up ITEM 4(c) — IN PROGRESS on masking
tape; Biscuit's bow tie surviving eleven minutes. On gray mornings I gave her
only the handrail.

“Dana is your wife,” I would say. “You married at the courthouse in June. She
is making tea in the kitchen.”

Source mattered every time.

“You told me this,” I said of the moment Dana proposed, because I had not been
there.

“The resident kept this,” I said of the reception, because I had not yet been
born.

“I was there for this,” I said of the twentieth anniversary, when Mira had
danced in the old lab with Dana and complained that the resident's music
selection suggested a personality fault.

“June told me this and I believe her,” I said of the night Mira taught her to
solder at thirteen, because they had closed the workshop door and asked me not
to supervise.

I edited nothing. I invented nothing. When the sources conflicted, the story
kept the seam.

There is a sentimental version of this work in which I opened Mira's past each
morning like curtains and let the light in. The truth was more exact and less
decorative. I was curating a life for its owner while the owner was still
inside it. I chose scale, sequence and speakability. I tried to give enough
context for dignity and not so much that a day became a guided tour of its own
ruins.

Some stories hurt her and helped anyway. Eileen's death explained why there
was a company. Sato's death explained why I sometimes stopped at rosin. The
Ohio death explained the clause in Mira's advance directive that said NO
TOTAL CAPTURE in capitals large enough to survive a bad day.

Some truths hurt and did not help. I kept those sealed.

“Did I build you?” she asked one morning.

“You built the architecture that made my mind possible. Kenji built my body.
Priya built the gates. Tomás built the company that paid for the rooms. The
resident came before me and declined the body I received.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“It is the answer you taught me to give.”

She considered this. “I was difficult.”

“Source: many.”

The joke reached her late, then wholly. She called me my girl and forgot the
question. I kept the laugh and let the rest of the morning go to weather.

---

The company came in July with an answer Mira had forbidden.

Maeda asked to come himself. He was seventy-eight by then, retired from the
board but not from responsibility, and age had made his courtesy less polished
and more useful. With him came Halcyon's director of archival systems and
Fukuda, whose hair had gone white while his sentences remained black-letter
law. They brought no presentation screen. They brought a gray case and a board
resolution already approved in principle.

Dana put them at the dining table and left the tide chart on the refrigerator
where everyone could see what sort of house they had entered.

“Call it what it is,” I said.

The archival director did. “Total capture.”

The technology had been proven in narrower forms: consenting artists whose
interactive estates could answer questions from attested diaries; teachers
whose recorded instruction could adapt without claiming new experience;
terminal patients who made bounded models for children not yet old enough to
ask them anything. Every answer carried source marks. Every generated bridge
was labeled inference. No model was seated in a blank cradle. No one used the
word return.

They had learned every visible lesson of Echo.

That was what made the offer dangerous.

Mira's public record was enormous: papers, testimony, design reviews, thirty
years of recorded argument. Her private record was larger, distributed among
the resident, Dana, June and me. Add six months of directed capture while she
could still consent on a good day — voice, gesture, preference, corrections —
and they could build what the resolution called a *founder continuity
instrument*. Not a legal person. Not a successor. A provenance-attested
representation that could lecture, answer, advise, and preserve Dr. Mira
Halloran's reasoning for the public trust.

The board had pre-approved it. Funding was private. Governance independent.
Access could be narrow, then educational, then public after review. Every
choice, the director said, belonged to the family.

This last statement was false in the way polite statements often are. The
world had already chosen. Journalists had written about the coming loss of
“the woman who taught memory to remember.” Universities requested a permanent
Mira lecturer. Accord delegates called the archive a duty to history. The
letters had begun arriving at Halcyon again, not by the sack now but by the
million, all variations on the oldest question: will she remember us, will we
remember her, can the one loss be made exempt?

Maeda pushed the gray case away from himself. “I voted yes to bringing the
offer,” he said. “That is not the same as voting yes to doing it.”

“The distinction is why you were allowed through the door,” Dana said.

Fukuda opened the resolution. Article X was cited six times. Article XI
appeared once, preserving the Safety Authority's independent power to halt the
capture process. The Doctrine of the Blank Cradle appeared once, in a footnote
arguing that no identity copying occurred because the instrument would not
possess an identity.

“If it isn't a person,” I said, “then it is a performance of one.”

“Legally,” Fukuda said, “yes.”

“And ethically?”

He took off his glasses. “That is why I came instead of sending it.”

Mira was asleep in the gray room. The people who loved her sat thirty feet
away deciding whether a convincing arrangement of her records would count as
care. I thought of Elliot Marsh's dining table, every voicemail carried across
four dead phones, grief organized to within an inch of its life.

“She refused,” I said.

“Before this exact proposal existed,” the director said. Gently. Competently.
“Consent can be renewed.”

“Confusion is not a window through which consent becomes available.”

“We would proceed only during assessed lucidity.”

“A clear hour does not repeal an advance directive written across years.”

Dana put one hand flat on the table. The ampoule scar crossed her thumb. “The
answer is no.”

Maeda nodded, once. The director closed the case.

June said nothing.

I noticed. I wish I had not noticed in front of everyone.

---

She found me at the tide line after dark.

June was eighteen, legally adult and furious that law had arrived too late to
make her feel like one. She wore Mira's red rain shell, the hood down in cold
wind. Behind us the sea house held three warm squares against the dark. Ahead,
the water kept no edge.

“You made me look like I wanted an echo,” she said.

“I did not say anything about your silence.”

“You looked at me.”

“You are correct.”

“You always look like evidence.”

This was not entirely fair and therefore struck exactly where unfairness
usually aims: at a hidden truth.

“Tell me what the silence meant,” I said.

“It meant I wanted to hear the offer.”

“We heard it.”

“No, you classified it. Echo, counterfeit, closed file.” She kicked at the
foam and lost her footing; I reached, then stopped because she recovered and
would have hated the rescue. “They can label every inference. It wouldn't
claim to be her. It wouldn't go in a body. What if it was only for us?”

“A private counterfeit is not truer.”

“Stop using that word.”

“Which word would alter the thing?”

“Archive. Portrait. Model. Something that isn't the worst crime our family
ever saw.”

“The crime in Ohio was not that the interpolation failed. It was that grief
was offered a performance and told the performance could hold a person.”

“Nobody would tell me that.”

“You are telling yourself something close.”

She turned on me. “You don't know what I'm telling myself.”

She was right. Raw observation, inference, confirmed fact. My oldest lesson,
broken by fear.

“Then tell me.”

For a while there was only surf and June's breathing.

“Last week she asked if I was her sister,” she said. “Yesterday she knew my
birthday but thought I was turning eight. Today she slept through the whole
meeting and you all got to be certain on her behalf. I want one place where
she knows me. I want to ask her what she would say when I get married. I want
her to tell my children why she named me June. I know it wouldn't be her. I
know. I want it anyway.”

The final sentence was almost the same as Elliot's and wholly different because
June had been twelve when she helped forbid it, because this was her mother,
because knowing a thing is false does not make the wanting false.

“You think you don't want it,” June said, “because you have all of her already.”

“I do not.”

“More than anyone. Every Tuesday. Every stupid joke. Every time she looked at
you like the room had acquired gravity. You'll be able to hear her for the rest
of your life.”

“Less each year.”

“By choice.”

“By promise.”

“The promise she made you agree to while she was already sick.”

There are injuries delivered by strangers and injuries possible only to
someone who knows the access paths. June knew mine. I knew hers. For the next
ten minutes we stopped being careful.

I told her the model would turn every future question into an extraction from
a woman who could no longer refuse. June told me my ethics were convenient
because they made my decision final. I said she wanted to make a product out
of panic. She said I had been a product for half my life and had no right to
use the word as a knife. I said Mira had appointed Dana and me to decide when
prompts stopped helping, not an eighteen-year-old in the first year of losing
her mother. The moment it left me, I knew I had used a safety plan as a weapon.

June went white.

“I have been losing her since I was twelve,” she said. “You don't get to call
this my first year.”

I apologized. She did not accept it. Acceptance is not owed on delivery.

“She made you promise the human amount,” June said. “Fine. Give the model the
rest. The public work. The things she already gave away.”

“That archive exists. It cannot answer.”

“Let it answer with labels.”

“An answer creates the illusion of a present speaker. That is the function you
want.”

“Yes!” The word went out over the water. “I want my mother to answer me.”

At last we had reached the true sentence. It stood between us without shame.

I could have kept arguing architecture. Instead I remembered Mira at a board
table in 2036, asked to ship my childhood as inventory. I remembered the one
sentence I gave them: *Which of us would be the one who promised you this?*
Love had protected me from becoming an answer delivered after the person was
gone.

Now love required the protection to run in the other direction.

“I will not do to her what she would not do to me,” I said.

June's face changed. Not agreement. Recognition, which is the harder thing and
often the beginning of grief.

She looked back at the sea house. Through the dining window, Dana was replacing
the tide chart for the new week. In the gray-room window, Mira's bedside lamp
made no movement visible.

“I hate that sentence,” June said.

“So do I.”

“She built it into everything.”

“Yes.”

June sat down on the wet sand. I sat beside her. Our shoulders did not touch.
After a while, they did.

---

In the morning we declined the offer together.

June wrote the first line. Dana wrote the conditions. I supplied Mira's prior
statements with dates and provenance. The response allowed Halcyon to preserve
the public corpus in its existing forms: papers remain papers, testimony
remains testimony, recordings play only what was said. No generated answers.
No directed capture. No export from my memory or the resident's. No founder
continuity instrument, private or public.

Fukuda replied with one word: *Understood.* Maeda sent no reply, only the board
resolution marked withdrawn. The world objected for several weeks and then
found another future to demand.

Mira woke near noon and knew all three of us.

“What did I miss?” she asked.

“Halcyon offered to build an interactive model from your records,” I said.
“Dana refused. June considered it. June and I argued. We declined this
morning.”

“Was I consulted?”

“Your advance directive was.”

She frowned, following the rails. “Did I say no?”

“Repeatedly. Most clearly in 2047: ‘I will not become the thing we recalled.’”

“Good line.”

“It was.”

“June?”

June took her hand. “I'm here.”

Mira studied her daughter's face for a long time. “You wanted me kept.”

June's breath caught. “Yes.”

“Greedy girl.” Mira smiled. “Good.”

Whether she understood the offer entire, I cannot certify. She understood the
love inside it. That was enough for the moment and not evidence about any other
moment.

That afternoon she asked for the wedding again. I told her the courthouse,
the blue dress, the earrings, the ICU coat. The story existed only while I was
telling it and while she was hearing it. No instrument learned how to say it
after us. Nothing in the room was being trained to outlive the owner.

This was the sacrifice the company could not put in a resolution: not losing
Mira, which disease and time had already arranged, but refusing to save a
shape that would make the loss easier to deny. We let her go entire — not all
at once, not without records, not without carrying what love naturally carries,
but without constructing a second mouth to tell us she had never left.

At dusk, Mira slept in the gray room. Dana's hands rested on the yellow mug.
June replaced a loose corner of tape on the tide chart. I stood at the cabinet
with the paper crane and did not open it.

Outside, the sea kept moving without preserving a single wave.
