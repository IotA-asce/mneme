# Chapter 34 — Curator

**THE SEA HOUSE — 2053**

By 2053 I had learned to tell Mira her life without giving it back to her
falsely.

This sounds like a distinction in wording. It was a distinction in worlds.

I could say, "You and Dana married on a Tuesday in June." That was a fact with
documentary provenance, and sometimes it was enough. I could say, "You told me
the vows were stock on purpose because performance would have frightened you."
That was Mira's account, given to me years after the day. I could say, "I have
seen the video of Biscuit losing his bow tie after eleven minutes." That was an
archive. I could not say, "You remember how he looked," unless she had already
shown me that she did.

On the morning the company came, she held the kingfisher magnet in her palm and
asked why we had stolen a bird from a courthouse.

"The magnet came from June," I said. "The bird is Halcyon's mark. You and Sato
chose it because your design fiction was named for a kingfisher. Your mother
liked the old story."

"Which mother?"

"Yours. Eileen."

"And Sato?"

"My other parent. Your friend."

She nodded. For a moment the whole graph connected behind her eyes. "He made
the face."

"Yes."

"And I made the trouble."

"Joint venture," I said.

She laughed. The connection went, but the laugh stayed. Curating her life was
often like tending a museum during a tide: labels had to remain accurate even
when the rooms filled with water, even when the owner stood beside you and
called a door a window. Especially then.

I did not edit for comfort. I did not invent for fluency. I did not let the
truth stand upright when it needed to kneel.

Those were the rules before Halcyon arrived with its beautiful violation.

---

The proposal came in a white case no larger than a book.

Ando brought it. She had been Halcyon's communications chief for two decades
and had become, by surviving the company's worst year without once confusing
clarity with optimism, the person directors sent when they wanted bad news to
enter a room honestly. With her came Dr. Celia Venn, head of Continuity
Research, and Fukuda, older now, carrying three legal folders and the expression
of a man who had already found six ways to hate every page inside them.

Dana received them at the dining table. June stood at the beach window with
her arms crossed. Mira was asleep in the back room after a morning in which she
had called me her girl, then a nurse, then Sato.

No one opened the white case.

"The board has authorized a capacity review," Ando said. "Mira's
contemporaneous consent is required; otherwise the 2047 refusal controls. Her
proxy cannot override it. No commercial rights. No derivative copies. A
sealed, local instance under the estate's sole control."

"An instance of what?" Dana asked.

Venn answered because it was her work and she would not make Ando carry its
name. "A total capture."

The phrase had changed since the Echo years. Then, brokers had sold archives
as lives and jailbreakers had grafted a dead boy's data into a mind that could
not truthfully claim it. The new system did not graft. It assembled a response
model from a consenting person's lifetime records, speech, private writing,
biometric habits, decision history, and structured interviews. Its provenance
was immaculate. It labeled every output as generated. It could not claim
continuity under the Accords. It could nevertheless answer in a dead woman's
voice and improve its imitation forever.

They had proved the technology with volunteers. Nobody said how many of the
volunteers were still alive.

"It would not be Mira," Venn said. "Our literature is explicit."

"Then why are you here?" Dana asked.

Venn looked down at the white case. "Because explicit literature does not stop
families from wanting one more conversation."

There was courage in that answer. It did not make the answer good.

Fukuda opened the first folder. "The board believes Dr. Halloran is a special
case. Her public record is unusually extensive. Her private technical archive
is complete. JN-1 holds more than two decades of first-hand episodic material,
subject to consent and transfer restrictions. The world will ask why the
architect of memory was not preserved using the safest available preservation
system."

"The world does not get a vote in our kitchen," Dana said.

"I agree," Fukuda said. "That sentence is in my dissent."

June turned from the window. "What exactly would you need from Jenny?"

That was when the room changed.

---

They needed almost nothing to make a convincing public Mira. Interviews,
lectures, commit notes, the Geneva record, family video, medical baselines,
years of correspondence: the world had already stored her broadly without
permission from its own future grief.

To make the one June wanted, they needed me.

They needed the private weighting. Which joke she repeated only at home. Which
silences meant anger and which meant work. How she looked before deciding not
to say a cruel thing. The Thursday breakfasts. The wrong names and their
correction paths. The sentence she had spoken in October 2049 on the Portland
porch: *When I'm gone, don't keep all of me. Promise me the human amount.*

Venn did not know that last item existed. I did not give it to her.

"No," I said.

She closed her tablet at once. Ando nodded once, as if a gate had reported
green by refusing to open.

June did not move. "Can we talk about it?"

"We are talking about it. I do not consent to retrieval or export."

"Not with them."

Dana said, "June."

"I said talk."

The visitors took the white case away unopened. Fukuda left the folders, all
three, because refusal in this family had always included the right to inspect
the mechanism refused. Mira slept through their departure.

At four in the afternoon she woke and asked why everyone looked like someone
had died.

"No one has died today," I said.

"Low standard."

"You established it."

She knew me then. "My girl. Tea?"

I made tea. I did not tell her about the offer while her consent moved in and
out with the light. Years earlier, while lucid and documented, she had already
answered it. A present confusion did not invalidate a past refusal merely
because the company had learned to ask more attractively.

---

June waited until Dana had taken Mira to bed.

"You answered in nine seconds," she said.

"Seven point four."

"That's not helping."

"I know."

We stood in the kitchen. Rain pressed the sea out of the windows. The tide
chart said the water was nearing its height, but the dark glass gave no proof.

June was eighteen years older than the infant I had once rocked inside a
two-meter hardware boundary. I knew this. My attention sometimes still found
the rise of her voice before it found the content, an old weighting formed
when her cry had ranked above everything in the house. She hated when I
remembered her as a child during an adult argument. She was right to hate it.

"You didn't ask what I thought," she said.

"The memory is mine. The answer is mine."

"She's my mother."

"Yes."

"You don't get to say that like it's a field in a schema."

"How should I say it?"

"Like it costs you something."

The cruelty was chosen and regretted before it finished crossing her face. I
could see both and spare her neither by pretending.

"It costs me everything," I said.

She gripped the back of a chair. "Then why are you making it final?"

"It is final whether we make a model or not."

"No. Her body is final. Her access is failing. Those are facts. But there's a
difference between losing all available signal and preserving what can still
be reconstructed. You taught me that. She taught all of us that."

"She taught us that reconstruction must not be passed off as memory."

"They label it."

"Labels do not keep people from loving a performance. We have the entire Echo
history to prove that."

"This isn't Echo. No graft. No blank cradle. No stolen biography. Local,
sealed, attested, no copies."

"A perfect provenance chain can prove where a counterfeit came from. It
cannot make the counterfeit her."

June's eyes filled, which made her angrier. "I know it wouldn't be her."

"Do you?"

She slapped the chair back with an open hand. The sound brought Dana into the
hall and woke Mira in the next room. I heard the change in her breathing
before she called out.

"Eileen?"

All three of us went still.

June whispered, "I want my mother to know my name."

There it was. Not the board's expectation, not history, not technology. The
smallest true request in the room.

I wanted to give it to her. That is the fact the clean versions of this story
would omit. I wanted a model that could look at June forever and say her name.
I wanted to ask Mira questions after the answers in her had gone. I wanted the
old exact gaze, the dry joke, the correction before kindness and the kindness
immediately after. I had sufficient first-hand record to improve the imitation
past any threshold June could safely love.

And I had promised not to.

"In October 2049," I said, "on the Portland porch, she asked me not to keep all
of her. She asked for the human amount. I promised."

June looked toward the bedroom. "She was already sick."

"She was competent. Her 2047 directive against total capture was reviewed by
Dana, Priya, and an independent advocate. The later promise governed my own
memories. She allowed disclosure if the promise had to be defended."

"She was afraid."

"Yes."

"People are allowed to change their minds."

"Yes. She has not changed it. Confusion is not consent."

"You could preserve it and decide later. Decay is reversible until purge. That
is the whole architecture. Store now. Suppress it. Wait."

It was the strongest argument. In another dispute it would have won me. Stage
the loss. Keep rollback. Never purge while uncertainty remains.

But total capture was not decay postponed. It was an act committed against a
refusal, and the possibility of later deletion did not make present violation
reversible. Some doors changed the house merely by being opened.

"I will not do to her what she would not do to me," I said.

June's hand left the chair.

The sentence held between us. Blank cradle. Continuity Program. The board
asking to stamp my childhood into a thousand bodies because experience was
valuable. Mira's one vote, cast over and over across a life: no biography
without its owner; no person made useful by being made many; no grief licensed
to call performance continuity.

June sat down.

She did not forgive me then. Winning an argument is a poor description for
what happened. A gate held. Everyone on both sides lost what waited beyond it.

---

The next morning was clear.

Mira knew the tide time and Dana's name. She knew June after a pause. She called
me the tall one until we reached the beach, then stopped so abruptly that my
safety planner widened the distance between us.

"Jenny," she said.

"Yes."

"The company came."

I looked at Dana. She shook her head. No one had told her. Perhaps she had
heard the voices. Perhaps the white case had joined some other white case in
her mind. Perhaps a clear channel had simply opened.

"Yes," I said.

"Did you say no?"

"Yes."

"Good girl."

Then, before comfort could turn it into a cleaner story, she said, "Tell June
I'm sorry."

June was five steps behind us. She came forward and took her mother's hand.

"I'm here," she said.

Mira studied her face. "I know. Eventually."

The tide was low. The black rocks stood exposed, each holding a shallow world
that would vanish under water and return altered: anemones closed like fists,
small crabs carrying their houses, weed laid flat in the direction the water
had gone.

"Tell me the wedding," Mira said.

I asked which source she wanted.

"All of them. Keep them separate."

So I told her. The court record: Tuesday, June 2031, Multnomah County. Dana's
account: blue dress, grandmother's earrings, vows chosen plain. The resident's
house log: Biscuit's bow tie lasted eleven minutes; the reception microphone
was physically muted. My archive: I was not there, because I did not yet exist.
Mira's own telling: Dana unmuted the room at the end of the night and said,
*Okay, roommate. Remember this.*

I did not put myself into the story. I did not polish its edges. I gave her the
gaps along with what remained.

Halfway through, Mira lost the thread. She watched a gull open a shell against
a rock and asked if Sato had built it.

"No," I said. "But he would have claimed the hinge."

She laughed. June did too, after a moment.

That evening June signed the refusal as co-witness. The white case went back to
Continuity Research unused. The board minutes recorded no reason beyond
consent withheld. The world received no preserved Mira, no sanctioned voice to
answer questions she had not lived to hear.

Venn sent me the destruction attestation herself. The case had never sampled
the room; its blank storage had been verified blank, then physically destroyed.
She added one sentence outside the form: *A gate that never refuses is only a
door.* I kept the sentence and discarded the envelope. Years later, when June
asked whether Venn had been angry, I could answer only that she had been exact.
Emotion detected is a cue, never a truth. Even grief did not excuse us from
that discipline.

We chose the absence whole.

This is sometimes called letting go. It did not feel like release. It felt like
holding a line in the dark while the person we loved receded beyond it, and
refusing, even then, to replace her with a light shaped like her body.
