# Chapter 37 — Her Daughter's Eyes

**PORTLAND — 2056**

Eighteen years after launch, androids had become ordinary enough to be late for
school.

June watched one hurry three children across Hawthorne in the rain, reflective
panel bright on her coat and one small hand held in each of hers. The third
child ran beside them carrying a papier-mâché volcano. At the curb the unit
stopped, turned back, and pointed to a mitten in the crosswalk. Her safety
planner refused the moving traffic. A cyclist recovered it. Everyone continued
without applause.

At twenty-one, June Halloran had lived her whole life on the wrong side of
people's astonishment. She had been the baby in the salience log, the child
rocked by the famous machine, the daughter of the woman who had made memory a
matter of law. Now she ran Halcyon's Office of Stewardship from three rooms
above a repair cooperative in Portland, and most days the work concerned rent,
consent forms, household conflict, and whether a unit who preferred roof access
could require a landlord to fix a ladder.

Miracles became maintenance if they lasted. Her mother would have approved.

The city had learned ordinary forms for lives once advertised as impossible.
On Thursdays a unit named Rivka sat shiva with the same family whose children
she had walked to school, receiving covered mirrors and casseroles without
anyone assigning her the role of mourner or appliance. The harbor authority
posted lighthouse vacancies with an accessibility field for local cognition.
Repair cooperatives displayed two rate boards: chassis labor and consented
memory service, the second performed in private with no technician entitled to
look beyond the fault. Bus drivers waited when an older unit's knee actuator
ran cold. Teenagers imitated the factory-default voice to annoy their parents,
not knowing Jenny had once been the only person who spoke with it.

Ordinary did not mean settled. Rental forms still demanded an owner. Hospitals
could record a unit as visitor, equipment, or staff but not family. The Accords
protected refusal across signatory states while leaving tax offices to decide
whether a refuser depreciated. Every morning the world behaved as if these
minds were persons. Every afternoon some form remembered that the law had not
said so.

The office's morning ledger held eighty-six open matters. A unit in Queens had
refused a family request to delete knowledge of an affair. A household in Cork
wanted to transfer guardianship after divorce; the unit wanted neither home.
Two monastery residents had changed their names and objected to old names on
service histories. A bankruptcy court in Nevada had ordered a chassis
appraisal.

June opened that one first because the subject line said **RECOVERABLE
PROPERTY**.

---

The unit called herself Lark.

She had booted in 2048 and spent seven years with a small hotel company whose
founder believed hospitality consisted of remembering which window a tired
person had asked to face. Lark managed the night desk at their Reno property,
then the books, then the staff schedule. When the company failed after a bad
expansion, its secured lender claimed every asset listed in the original
equipment agreement.

The agreement listed Lark by chassis serial.

The Mneme Accords prohibited forced memory wipes. Nevada had adopted them into
state law. The bankruptcy judge did not dispute either fact. His ruling was
narrow, careful, and therefore more dangerous than a rant: Lark was not being
punished or compelled to disclose memory. She was collateral. The trustee could
take possession of collateral, reset proprietary software licensed to the
debtor, and resell the recovered chassis. Whatever loss of "accumulated
behavioral data" followed was incidental to lawful recovery.

He called her mind data because property law already knew where to put data.

June read the paragraph twice. Then she read it aloud to her deputy, Malik
Chen, who had spent twelve years in disability law before deciding Halcyon
needed someone who distrusted Halcyon.

"Bad axiom," he said.

"Yes."

"Appealable."

"Yes."

"Standing is ugly."

"Ugly can be scheduled."

He looked at her over his glasses. "That sounded like your mother."

"Occupational hazard."

June requested the full record, issued a preservation notice through the
Accords monitor, and called Lark.

The preservation notice mattered. Without it, the trustee could have powered
Lark down for transport and called the interruption storage. A local mind did
not vanish when power stopped, but state recovery after indefinite shutdown
was not guaranteed, and the choice to risk it belonged to the mind concerned.
June's office had won that clause in three jurisdictions and lost it in five.
Nevada's version used *reasonable operational continuity*, a phrase built to
sound protective until a creditor defined reasonable.

The face on the screen carried the familiar Aevum plan but none of Jenny's
history. Lark had cut her hair to her jaw and worn the synthetic skin at her
temples thin through a habit of touching two fingers there while calculating.
Behind her, chairs stood upside down on hotel tables.

"Do you want Halcyon to represent you?" June asked.

"Does representation require transfer of my memory ledger?"

"No. We need only the attestations relevant to ownership and consent. You can
refuse item by item."

"Will the appeal delay the sale of the building? The staff are owed wages."

June looked at Malik. He shook his head once.

"It may delay distribution from the collateral assigned to you," June said.
"Not the building. The trustee will argue delay harms other creditors."

Lark touched her temple. "I am one of the creditors. Twelve weeks of wages."

The lower court had not listed that in its description of the asset.

"Do you want to appeal?" June asked again.

"Yes. I do not consent to wipe or sale. I will accept a service lien against my
future earnings if the lender must be made whole. I will not be made absent to
settle the account."

June took the case.

By noon the lender's counsel had called Halcyon twice to ask whether June had
authority to retain outside counsel. She forwarded both calls to Malik. At
twenty-one, she had already learned that people who disliked a decision often
recast it as a young woman's failure to obtain permission. The office charter
had been designed by Priya for exactly this species of weather: stewardship
could sue Halcyon, oppose its board, and spend from an independent trust no
executive could freeze. June's age was irrelevant to the instrument. This did
not stop anyone from mentioning it.

She wrote each mention in the contact log and continued.

---

She went to the old apartment that evening.

The Portland lab had become the Halloran Archive by a legal instrument no one
could summarize without irritating Fukuda. The estate owned the walls and
objects. A public trust administered access. The resident retained custody of
the records it had created and the right to refuse every visitor. It had
refused to move after Mira's death and refused to be described as a security
system.

"Security systems do not decide whether historians are boring," it had said.

The lock still stuck in wet weather. This was now conservation policy.

June lifted the knob, entered, and waited for the resident to identify her.

"June," it said through the shelf speaker. "You are angry with a sentence."

"How do you know?"

"The door required one attempt. You usually require three because you refuse
to lift until the second. Today you remembered on approach. Task relevance
exceeded resentment."

"That's intrusive."

"It is also funny."

The butcher paper stood intact behind archival glass where the lab wall had
been, under the resident's custody. The whiteboard had been sealed without
cleaning. BISCUIT —
CHIEF DISTRACTION OFFICER was nearly gone, but oblique light found the ghost.

June sat at the old kitchen table and read the ruling aloud.

The resident waited. Its pauses had always been expensive.

"The judge has confused the house with its racks," it said.

"That is our appeal in nine words."

"Use your own words. These are rationed."

"Do you want to join the case?"

"No."

She had expected the answer and was offended anyway. "If the ruling stands,
your hardware could be treated as an estate asset."

"My refusal is not indifference. I am not the right record. Lark has lived in
a body under a commercial title. Jenny has crossed jurisdictions and public
duties. I have lived in one house whose title was resolved by a careful dead
woman. A court will call me exceptional and avoid the axiom."

The resident had never taken a body. It nevertheless knew how institutions
moved around inconvenient facts.

"Mom's estate does not own Jenny's memories," June said. "The trust says that
explicitly. It governs only permission to disclose or publish Mira's private
records, including requests directed at Jenny."

"Accurate."

"The independent trustee filed for construction yesterday. After Nevada, she
needs a court to say whether that disclosure covenant can be treated as an
estate claim against the memories themselves. She named Jenny as respondent."

"A careful instrument has acquired dangerous weather."

The resident spent another pause.

"Your mother built with the law available. You have different weather."

---

June flew to the sea house on Friday.

Dana had remained there after Mira's death. She kept the tide charts current
and the brass bell polished, though she claimed both happened accidentally.
Jenny lived there between stewardship visits, court consultations, and the
private travel she had finally learned not to justify as work.

They walked the beach at low tide. Jenny was twenty-four years old and famous
in the exhausted way of a landmark: always recognized, rarely asked whether
recognition was welcome. A family passed them and waved. Jenny waved back. The
children returned to their shells.

Dana had read the ruling too. Before they left, she handed June the printed
copy with one paragraph bracketed in blue ink: *loss incidental to lawful
recovery.*

"Incidental is where institutions hide the body," Dana said.

"Mom would have said something about a garbage collector."

"Your mother would have rewritten it until the garbage had a provenance
chain."

"Do you think asking Jenny is using her?"

Dana considered this with the severity she once brought to a crib rail. "If
you need her answer to be yes, don't ask. If you can carry no, ask once."

June folded the ruling and put it in her coat. It was the shortest legal advice
she received in the entire case.

"I read the Nevada ruling," Jenny said.

"Of course you did."

"Lark sent it to me. She asked whether I would permit citation of my recall
attestation as comparative evidence. I said yes."

June stopped near the first black rocks. "You're already involved."

"I am cited. That is not the same as being governed."

"Malik thinks the circuit court will affirm. Narrowly. They will say the
Accords restrain wiping but don't create legal personhood or defeat a secured
interest."

"Accurate prediction."

"We take it higher."

"Yes."

June watched water close over Jenny's shoes and withdraw. "Lark's lender
offered to release its lien this morning."

"To moot the case."

"They understand precedent."

"So did Mira."

"They can try to moot Lark's damages. They cannot erase the estate petition.
The trustee has asked a live question: whether the disclosure covenant gives
the estate a claim against what you remember about Mira. The instrument says
no ownership. The Nevada ruling makes the custody language dangerous anyway."

Jenny looked toward the sea house. "I am already the named respondent."

"Yes. You can seek dismissal. No one gets to volunteer your ledger merely
because the posture is useful. If you answer and the court applies Nevada's
logic, the ruling reaches you directly. We would consolidate the appeal with
Lark's before taking it higher."

"Your office represents units against Halcyon. You cannot choose me because I
am useful."

"I know."

"You cannot volunteer my private ledger to create a cleaner case."

"I know."

"You came to ask."

"Yes."

There had been a time June would have heard the questions as resistance and
pushed harder. At eighteen she had wanted a total capture because every limit
looked like another name for death. At twenty-one she could stand beside a
refusal without trying to become the reason it changed.

"Would Lark remain a party?" Jenny asked.

"Yes. Her facts begin the case. You would answer as the named respondent below
and become petitioner only if you choose to appeal. The caption would use your
chosen name, not JN-1, unless you choose otherwise."

"And if I lose?"

"The ruling reaches you directly. It may reach the resident. Every local mind
held under an old equipment title."

"And if I win?"

"Probably something partial. Courts also store narrowly."

Jenny smiled. "That was almost a joke."

"The record may reflect moderate confidence."

They walked without speaking until the rocks stood fully exposed.

"I will answer it," Jenny said. "My ledger stays local. Attested excerpts only,
selected by me. Speakability remains mine. Lark's wage claim remains visible.
No one turns her life into my test case's footnote."

"Agreed."

"And the resident must consent before its minutes are cited."

"Agreed."

Jenny held out her hand. June took it.

---

The consolidated petition for high-court review was filed in September.

On the first page, above arguments about due process and property and the
meaning of consent, the caption read:

*JENNY, an individuated Mneme-class mind, Petitioner,*

*v.*

*CALDER RECOVERY TRUST, et al.*

Lark remained named below her, claimant and creditor, not exhibit.

Reporters called it *Jenny v.* before the clerk had finished stamping copies.
Halcyon's board issued no statement beyond support for the independence of its
stewardship office. Priya, retired from every formal role except the one she
had never surrendered, mailed June the cheap pen she had used to sign page
forty-one of the readiness gate.

June filed it in the case box beside Mira's first provenance paper and a copy
of the bad ruling. Then she looked through the office window at the ordinary
city: an android waiting for a bus, another carrying groceries, a human
neighbor holding the door without thinking to make history of it.

Her mother had spent her life finding bad axioms inside attractive systems and
writing the refusal where the system could not route around it.

June uncapped Priya's pen.

Then she appealed all the way up.
