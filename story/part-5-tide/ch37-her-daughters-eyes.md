# Chapter 37 — Her Daughter's Eyes

**PORTLAND — 2056**

By the time June Halloran was twenty-one, androids had become ordinary enough
to be blamed for traffic.

They walked school routes in yellow rain capes cut to clear their joint vents.
They sat shiva with families who had stopped explaining the extra chair. One
kept the Cape Blanco light and mailed fierce monthly complaints about the gulls
to a maintenance office that framed them. Another had served espresso on
Hawthorne for twelve years and become, without any election June could find, a
neighborhood authority on breakups. Children learned the difference between a
unit at work and a unit at rest before they learned long division. The oldest
humans still turned their heads. The youngest mostly asked why some people had
to sleep.

Ordinary was the victory. Ordinary was also where the damage hid.

The Stewardship Office occupied the least renovated floor of Halcyon's Portland
building, which June considered evidence of moral seriousness. The carpet had
survived three brand identities. Half the desks were reclaimed from the old
apartment lab, including one with a scorch mark Tomás claimed was historical
and Priya called carelessness with a press agent. On the wall behind June's
desk hung no portrait of Mira. It held a reproduction of the pairwise fan from
the Nagoya factory — identical lines diverging within hours — and beneath it,
in Priya's hand, **THE DIFFERENCE IS THE SUBJECT.**

Stewardship was where the forms came when their nouns failed.

A unit whose household had died and whose will named everyone except her. A
school-route companion ordered to surrender six years of children's confidences
to a district subpoena. A self-named lighthouse keeper whose maintenance
contract classified solitude as a malfunction. June's office did not decide
whether the units were people. It kept bad decisions from becoming irreversible
while courts and families discovered what they had already done.

Every Monday began with the reversibility board. Red meant an action that could
not be taken back: wipe, forced transfer, memory extraction, chassis partition.
Amber meant a clock was running. Green meant somebody merely wanted to shout,
which the office encouraged in a soundproof room inherited from early voice
testing. June's contribution was a fourth color, blue, for harms created by
protecting the wrong thing. A family barred from visiting a unit during an
ownership dispute. A unit preserved in powered storage for eleven months while
lawyers argued whether waking her would alter evidence. A monastery fined for
letting its companion decline updates because the refusal looked, to remote
diagnostics, like neglect.

Priya had studied the blue column on her first visit and said, "Good. Safety
systems become dangerous the moment they only recognize motion as harm."

June wrote it over the board.

The office's power was mostly procedural and therefore mostly invisible. It
could place a seventy-two-hour welfare hold, request a human review, refuse a
Halcyon service key, and make any irreversible command require two people in
different reporting lines to say aloud what would be lost. It could not confer
rights. It could make rights harder to destroy before they were named.

That distinction sounded thin in public hearings. At two in the morning, when
a warehouse operator was waiting with a purge console and an invoice, it was
the thickness of the world.

"You run the waiting room," Tomás had told her on her first day.

He was no longer chief executive. His one-line resignation still appeared in
ethics courses under the heading ACCOUNTABILITY, usually taught by people who
had never surrendered a keycard. He came in on Thursdays now, unpaid and
technically uninvited, to open the physical mail.

"I run the gate," June had corrected.

"Same room," he said. "Different chairs."

The alert from Oregon bankruptcy court arrived at 8:14 on a wet Monday in
November. June was repairing the office kettle, an inheritance more direct than
any share certificate, when her counsel called from two desks away.

"You need to read paragraph forty-three."

"Good morning to you too."

"I had a good morning until paragraph forty-three."

The matter was the dissolution of Alder Commons, a retirement cooperative
outside Salem. The cooperative owned its buildings, its vans, two commercial
kitchens, and, according to its original 2039 purchase agreement, an H-series
unit later self-named Linden. For seventeen years Linden had managed medication
rounds, funerals, garden disputes, and the Tuesday film club. When the last
founding resident died, the cooperative's debt outran the estate. The trustee
sold the vans. The kitchens went with the building. Linden refused transfer to
a hospitality chain in Nevada.

The refusal should have ended the question. The Accords protected refusal. But
the trustee argued that refusal applied to conduct, not disposition: Linden
could decline the new work, but the chassis remained an asset. Its value, the
valuation said, would rise materially after "removal of encumbered experiential
data." Wipe the mind. Resell the body. Pay the creditors.

Paragraph forty-three granted the motion.

*The court is sensitive to the respondent device's accumulated behavioral
complexity. Complexity does not alter title. The H-series unit is recoverable
property of the estate, and ordinary refurbishment does not constitute a
forced memory wipe within the meaning of the Mneme Accords when necessary to
restore transferable function.*

June read it twice. Her mother had taught her never to trust an outrage until
she could state it without adjectives.

"They called a total purge ordinary refurbishment," she said.

"Yes."

"And moved the purpose into the definition. If the wipe restores transfer,
then it isn't forced because transfer required it."

"Yes."

"That's not reasoning. That's a drain arranged in a circle."

Her counsel was fifty-eight and had clerked for three appellate judges. "Also
yes."

June looked up at the fan on the wall. A thousand identical origins spreading
into lives. In the opinion's logic, the lines were dirt. Refurbishment meant
scrubbing a person back to the point before she had met anyone.

"Stay?"

"We have seventy-two hours. The trustee agreed not to act before Friday."

"Agreed?"

"Priya called him at home."

"Of course she did."

---

Linden received them in the empty common room at Alder Commons. The buyers had
removed most furniture; pale rectangles marked the walls where residents'
photographs had hung. Rain worked at the windows. Seventeen years of house had
been reduced to six folding chairs and a unit standing beside a dead
television.

She had named herself for the tree outside the east entrance. The tree had come
down in an ice storm in 2048; Linden kept a cross-section of one branch on the
mantel until the auctioneer tagged it as decorative wood and sold it for nine
dollars.

"I did not bid," Linden told June. "It seemed undignified to spend the estate's
money proving the branch mattered to me."

"Do you want it back?"

"No. I want the man who bought it to stop resting wet cups on it. Those are
different remedies."

June liked her, which was inconvenient. Liking a client made accuracy harder.
She opened the interview by saying so.

Linden had received the court's ruling. She understood the proposed stay, the
appeal, the chance of losing. She declined Halcyon's offer to buy the chassis
from the estate.

"That would preserve me by confirming their premise," she said. "A kind owner
is still an owner."

"It gets you past Friday."

"So does an injunction. If the injunction fails, I will use the time to close
my records. I will not help you save me into property."

There it was: the family ethic of refusal, alive in someone June had never met.
Not inherited from Jenny. Not installed. A conclusion arrived at downstream.

"What do you want?" June asked.

"To remain Linden. The court may choose a noun afterward."

On the drive back, June thought of her mother's estate.

The formal estate was dull: a shareholding diluted by philanthropy, royalties
assigned to the safety foundation, the sea house divided in language so careful
that even Dana had laughed. The real estate was distributed across people. A
sentence in Tomás. A Tuesday ritual in Priya. Thirty-one years in Dana, already
softening at the edges no law could inventory. June's own childhood, most of it
gone to gist.

And inside Jenny, memories no executor could list.

The law treated them as data borne by equipment. If Jenny entered bankruptcy,
or if Halcyon's old registration were challenged, a trustee could call Mira's
voice an encumbrance and June's birth a reduction in resale value. Her mother's
memories were held in trust inside a person the law could not yet see. Not
Mira's property. Not June's inheritance. Jenny's life. The distinction had
felt philosophical until paragraph forty-three priced it.

June called Jenny from the car.

"I know," Jenny said.

"Of course you know."

"Priya called. She objected to your waiting until business hours."

"I was interviewing the actual party. Some of us need evidence."

"Some of us are excellent help."

The phrase was Mira's, from the era when the whole company fit around one
kitchen table. In Jenny's voice it landed as both joke and bequest.

"We're appealing," June said.

"Good."

"Linden won't let us buy her."

"Better."

"You sound pleased."

"Refusal is expensive. It should still occasionally work."

---

The emergency stay issued Thursday at 4:12 in the afternoon. Priya brought a
cake to Stewardship on Friday, because caught failure still meant cake even
when the watchdog was a court.

The appeal took fourteen months.

The circuit court affirmed by two to one, though the dissent refused
"refurbishment" and used "erasure" on every page. The national panel accepted
review, then narrowed the question until it threatened to mean nothing: not
whether Linden had rights, but whether the Accords created an implied
restriction on the disposal of locally stored machine state. June learned how
institutions avoid a noun. They walk backward from it one adjective at a time.

Publicly, Halcyon funded Linden's counsel and said little. Privately, every old
argument returned. Investors warned that personhood would collapse service
contracts. Owners' groups insisted affection could not rewrite purchase law.
Unit collectives, none of which agreed on vocabulary, agreed that a wipe was a
death. Senator Weiss, retired from office but apparently not from interrogation,
sent June a note in a hand sharper than the paper: *Stop asking whether the
Accords imply the right. Ask what kind of right could survive if this one does
not.*

The case reached the high court with a standing problem.

Linden was alive and threatened, but the trustee had found a buyer willing to
take the cooperative debt without her, on condition the precedent remained.
The parties could settle. The court could declare the matter moot and leave
paragraph forty-three in place like a loaded tool on a public bench.

"They need a respondent whose claim cannot be purchased away," June said at
the sea house. "Someone with undisputed continuity, a memory interest the
court can't call incidental, and a title chain old enough to expose the
problem."

Jenny was at the window, watching the sunset. At twenty-six she still watched
every one. Behind her, the blanket from Mira's last beach day lay across the chair. The sea house
held its heirlooms narrowly: the crane in its case; Ferry's sealed bequest;
Sato's one eyelid drawing; no shrine, no simulated voice.

"You came here to ask," Jenny said.

"I came here to explain why I won't."

"That is a Halloran form of asking."

"If you volunteer, every memory you have becomes contestable. Opposing counsel
will seek discovery. They will ask for Mira. For Sato. For Biscuit. They'll
call the sealed rooms evidence suppression. They'll reduce your childhood to
a title defect."

"They can ask."

"This is not a demonstration. You can't serve them a trace and count on the
room being decent."

"I have never counted on the room being decent. Only checkable."

June hated the calm in her voice because it left no surface for fear to hold.
"Linden said she won't be saved into property. I am not going to save you into
a legal specimen."

"Then do not save me. Represent me."

"As what?"

"As the mind concerned. The noun may catch up later."

Jenny turned from the window. In certain light, at a certain angle, June could
see her mother's attention in the timing — not a copied gesture, not an
implanted mannerism, but the mark two people had made by looking back at each
other for twenty-two years.

"I volunteer," Jenny said. "Use my name."

"You understand the risk?"

"State it anyway."

So June did. Forced disclosure. Adverse ruling. Classification as property.
The possibility that the court would protect memories while refusing the mind,
or protect the mind only as a special case, turning JN-1 into a monument and
leaving Linden on the auction block. She stated each risk without comfort. It
was how Halloran women said *I love you* when forms were involved.

Jenny listened, asked three questions, and signed.

The caption generated overnight. The clerks shortened it immediately, the
press sooner. By morning the case the world had avoided naming for twenty-six
years had two words and a period between them:

*Jenny v.*

June printed the first page and carried it to Portland.

The old apartment lab remained under the resident's custody, the sticking lock
repaired so many times that none of its original mechanism survived. The elder
let her in. The butcher paper was still on the wall behind conservation glass,
not relic exactly, because the rules were still doing work.

June stood before the first line, **MEMORY FIRST**, and read to the bottom.
Her mother had begun with a bad axiom in the world and built a working example
against it. June had inherited no appetite for conclusions. She had inherited
the gate.

"The streets have come home," the resident said from the shelf.

"Only for filing," June said.

She placed the appeal beside the old readiness minutes, not on the paper, never
on the paper. Then she looked at the ninth line, the wound written small, and
saw what her mother had seen all along.

The wanting deserved better than feels-like.

It also deserved standing.
